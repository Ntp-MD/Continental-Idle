# Research file — Human behaviour on a tile grid (Domain J)

Scope: the observable, measurable behaviour of people inside a building — walking and flow,
bottlenecks and bends, queuing and waiting, wayfinding and decision points, privacy and
territory, staff versus guest paths, acoustic/visual intrusion, and emergency movement —
converted into rules a design agent can test on a discrete grid. Every rule carries a named
source, an evidence tier, and a tile/A\*/queue check. Claims that cannot be falsified on the
grid are pushed into `## Weak or contested` rather than promoted to rules.

Related file: `architecture-theory.md` (Domain TH) already owns programme, adjacency, zoning,
desire lines (TH-14), egress width arithmetic (TH-12), decision-point asymmetry (TH-19), and
integration (TH-20). This file does not restate them; it supplies the *behavioural* evidence
that fills them in and the checks TH cannot perform (flow, density, queues, waiting, privacy,
complaint causation).

Host grid this file is written against:
`1 tile = 0.5 m`, per-floor 2D, tile states `walkable | blocked | door` only; wall = one blocked
tile; doors = runs of door tiles; rooms = flood-fill typed by fixture tags; movement = A\* octile
with diagonals only where both corner neighbours are open (a 1-tile corridor is strict single
file); vertical links = lift/stair portals **with queues** (queue length, waiting time and
boarding time are the model of vertical congestion); access by tags (public/guest/staff/service).

Derived grid constants used throughout (arithmetic, not claims):
- 1 tile of length = 0.5 m; 1 tile of area = 0.25 m²; 4 tiles = 1 m²; 2 tiles = 1.0 m.
- **Tiles per person = (m² per person) × 4.** Every published space-per-person band below has a
  tile equivalent, and every tile figure below has a m²/person equivalent.
- Free-flow walking ≈ 1.2 m/s = **2.4 tiles/s**; the measured mean in 1.5 m corridor experiments
  was 1.07 m/s ≈ 2.1 tiles/s. Use 2.0–2.4 tiles/s for level movement, and note that octile A\*
  covers a 20 × 20-tile (10 m × 10 m) square in ~28.3 tile-steps versus 40 forced single file
  (TH-11) — the same walk is ~1.41× cheaper in an open hall.
- Flow per metre of route width: **1.3 persons/(m·s)** level, **1.1** down stairs, **0.88** up
  stairs (MSC.1/Circ.1238 Annex 1 Table 1.2). Per 0.5 m tile of effective width that is
  0.65 p/s ≈ **39 persons/min per tile of width**; a 2-tile (1.0 m) door run ≈ 78 p/min.

## Rules

### HB-01 Grade every route and room against a pedestrian level-of-service band, in tiles per person
- Rule: Before accepting any plan, classify each walkable region by the space it affords per expected person and label it with a level of service. Do not treat "a couple of tiles per person" as comfortable: on the published bands, 1–4 tiles per person is near- or past-capacity standing, not walking. Declare a target LOS per space type in the programme and test the built plan against it.
- Evidence: US DOT/FHWA report *Capacity Analysis of Pedestrian and Bicycle Facilities* (FHWA-PD-98-001) reproduces the Highway Capacity Manual walkway bands: current HCM walkway LOS by pedestrian space — **A ≥ 12 m²/ped, B 3.7–12, C 2.2–3.7, D 1.4–2.2, E 0.6–1.4, F ≤ 0.6 m²/ped**, with walkway *capacity* defined at 6 ft²/ped ≈ 0.56 m²/ped; a recommended revision gives A ≥ 5.6, B 3.7–5.6, C 2.2–3.7, D 1.4–2.2, E 0.75–1.4, F ≤ 0.75 m²/ped. Separate bands for terminals (A+ ≥ 2.3 m²/ped, F ≤ 0.7) and for queuing (LOS A ≥ 1.21 m²/ped, F < 0.19 m²/ped, capacity 2 ft²/ped ≈ 0.19 m²/ped). ×4 converts all of these to tiles/person.
- Source: FHWA, *Capacity Analysis of Pedestrian and Bicycle Facilities*, Section 3 — https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/safety/pedbike/98107/section3.cfm — T1; background on the LOS construct: *A Review of Pedestrian Flow Characteristics and Level of Service Criteria*, Collective Dynamics — https://collective-dynamics.eu/index.php/cod/article/view/A17 — T2.
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: universal (bands are facility-specific: walkway / terminal / stair / queue)
- Confidence: High for the band structure and the values quoted; Medium for transferring street-facility bands unchanged to an interior floor plate.
- Grid translation: For each region compute `tiles_per_person = walkable_tiles_in_region / peak_persons_in_region`. Label with the walkway band above (A ≥ 48 tiles, B 15–48, C 9–15, D 5.6–8.8, E 2.4–5.6, F ≤ 2.4 tiles/person). Validation: (a) primary circulation must be ≥ LOS D (≥ 5.6 tiles/person) at design flow; (b) rooms intended for standing/waiting must be ≥ 4.8 tiles/person (queue LOS A = 1.21 m²); (c) no continuously-moving region may fall to ≤ 1 tile/person (0.25 m²/ped ≈ 4 p/m², above the standstill/queue density in HB-03). Reject any revision that moves a region down a band without a logged reason.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Deliberately brief pinch points (a doorway, a turnstile bank) legitimately drop a band for a few tiles — but only for a length under ~2 tiles, or the queue that forms is modelled in HB-10. Symptom of ignoring this: a plan that "looks open" but shows a corridor region at 3 tiles/person under design occupancy, i.e. LOS E, where the A\* simulation starts producing stop-and-go.

### HB-02 Size every route and door from flow per unit width, in persons per minute
- Rule: Compute a required width for each route and door run from the design flow: `required_width_tiles = ceil(peak_persons_per_min / 39)` for level movement, `/ 33` for a stair portal, `/ 26` for upward stair flow. Check the door run first, not the corridor — the door is almost always the narrower number.
- Evidence: IMO *Guidelines for Evacuation Analysis* MSC.1/Circ.1238, Annex 1 Table 1.2, give maximum mass-flow rates per unit width: **corridors and doors 1.3 persons/(m·s)**, **stairs down 1.1**, **stairs up 0.88**. Independent FHWA/HCM tables converge on the same order: platoon flow reaches **59 ped/min/m** at LOS F and terminals are held to **≤ 37 ped/min/m** at LOS A+; crossflow cases are tabulated at **75 ped/min/m** at LOS E. The agreement of an IMO evacuation figure (78/min/m) with the upper empirical pedestrian observations (59–75/min/m) is why this rule caps design flow below the code maximum.
- Source: MSC.1/Circ.1238 as published/quoted at https://puc.overheid.nl/doc/PUC_1952_14/ — T1; FHWA Section 3 (Tables 6, 8, 10) https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/safety/pedbike/98107/section3.cfm — T1.
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: universal for level routes, doors, stairs and portals; lift-car capacity is a separate queue problem (HB-13)
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: Build the OD matrix (roles × typical trips, per TH-11) and assign each route tile the sum of trips crossing it per minute (`flow_p_min`). Effective width = the shortest walkable run perpendicular to the flow direction, in tiles. Validation: `flow_p_min ≤ 39 × effective_width_tiles` on level routes; `≤ 33 ×` at stair portals; `≤ 26 ×` for upward stair flow; `≤ 39 × door_run_tiles` at every door. Report the binding element per route. Assert `min door run = 2 tiles` (1.0 m ≈ 78 p/min) wherever modelled flow > 30 p/min, because a 1-tile door cannot carry the corridor feeding it.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Flow-per-width assumes an unobstructed, level, single-purpose stream. Trolleys, luggage, luggage carts, queueing that spills into the route, and door swings all reduce the effective figure — this rule sizes the *skeleton*, HB-04/HB-05/HB-10 handle the losses. Symptom: a 2-tile corridor passing HB-02 arithmetic but failing HB-04 because two people cannot pass in it.

### HB-03 Hold design density below the speed-collapse knee
- Rule: Treat ≈ 0.5 persons/m² (≈ 8 tiles per person) as the design ceiling for movement space. Above it, walking speed stops being a property of the person and becomes a property of the crowd, and the plan's A\* travel-time estimates become invalid.
- Evidence: MSC.1/Circ.1238 Annex 1 Table 1.1 makes speed a function of density: free speed ≈ **1.2 m/s up to about 0.5 p/m²**, degrading monotonically and approaching zero around **3.2–3.5 p/m²** (the extracted band values were 1.2 m/s at D = 0 and 0.5, 0.20 m/s at D = 3.2 and 0.10 m/s at D = 3.5; the intermediate gradient is reported directionally, not as a fitted curve). A separate pedestrian-dynamics study fixes the practical ceiling for maintaining a 1 m interpersonal distance at **≤ 0.16 p/m² (≈ 6 m²/ped)**. Empirical crowd work reports stress markers rising with speed and with density, and measured mean walking speed in 1.5 m corridors at 1.07 m/s with local densities reaching 3 p/m² at bends.
- Source: MSC.1/Circ.1238 https://puc.overheid.nl/doc/PUC_1952_14/ — T1; "Estimating density limits for walking pedestrians keeping a safe distance from each other", *Sci Rep* — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7810874/ — T2; "The connection between stress, density, and speed in crowds" — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10442413/ — T2; PLOS ONE bend study (see HB-05) — T2.
- Class: FACT
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High for the qualitative knee and the 1.2 m/s free speed; Medium for the exact intermediate slope (the retrieved table was only partly machine-readable) — class kept: the density-speed table is read from MSC.1/Circ.1238; what is project judgement is *using* 0.5 p/m² as a design ceiling for movement space, not the measured knee itself
- Grid translation: From the A\* occupancy model, compute per region `density_p_per_m2 = persons / (walkable_tiles × 0.25)`, equivalently `tiles_per_person`. Validation: assert movement regions stay `≥ 8 tiles/person` (≤ 0.5 p/m²) at design flow; flag any region `≤ 4 tiles/person` (≥ 1 p/m²) as speed-degraded and re-run its travel times with a density-discounted speed (`speed_factor = 1 − 0.28 × (density − 0.5)` per MSC band interpolation, `HEURISTIC` fit — Confidence Low, use only to rank plans, never to report a time).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Standing spaces (queues, seats, bars, counters) are *not* movement space and are graded on the queue band instead (HB-01/HB-10). Symptom: a lift lobby that passes the movement test because everyone is counted as "moving" while standing still.

### HB-04 Do not run two-way demand through a single-file corridor; add passing bays
- Rule: On this grid a 1-tile-wide corridor is strict single file: no two occupants can pass, no diagonal movement is possible, and the octile cost collapses to Manhattan. Either widen a two-way route to ≥ 3 tiles (1.5 m) or insert passing bays, and price the queueing that results when you do neither.
- Evidence: The A\* movement rule is a host-grid fact (diagonals only where both corner neighbours are open), so a 1-tile run admits one person across any cross-section. Empirically the same geometry is what pedestrian research calls a *laterally constrained walkway*: flow per metre width cannot be realised because a person's shoulder width plus lateral clearance exceeds the constraint, and the HCM walkway bands exist precisely because space per pedestrian below ~0.6 m² marks the loss of free flow (FHWA Section 3). ADA/guidance clearances reinforce the order of magnitude: a single wheelchair user needs 30 × 48 in (760 × 1220 mm) of clear floor — 1.5 × 2.5 tiles for one person's *static* envelope, which on a 1-tile corridor cannot be accommodated at all.
- Source: FHWA Section 3 https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/safety/pedbike/98107/section3.cfm — T1; 2010 ADA Standards Ch. 3 §305.3 https://www.access-board.gov/ada/chapter/ch03/ — T1; host-grid movement spec — ENGINEERING arithmetic.
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: For each corridor, compute `width_tiles` and `bidirectional = true if trips arrive from both ends`. Validation: (a) flag every `width_tiles ≤ 1` segment with `bidirectional` as a **conflict segment**; (b) require a passing bay — a locally 3-tile-wide, ≥ 3-tile-long walkable pocket — every ≤ 12 tiles (6 m) along any retained single-file route; (c) report `single_file_share = single-file movement tiles / all movement tiles`, target < 0.10 for public routes; (d) compute the conflict penalty as `expected_conflict_delay = conflicts_per_min × 6 s`, where 6 s is a placeholder for one pull-in-and-repass manoeuvre (slow, pass, resume), `HEURISTIC`, Confidence Low — the *existence* of the penalty is the point, not its magnitude.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Single file is correct where the design intent is control or defence — a service corridor, a sterile zone, a detention pinch, a one-way housekeeping route (HB-14). Symptom: an agent "saves" 20 tiles by narrowing a corridor to 1 tile and the vertical-portal queues lengthen, because the corridor is now the bottleneck that HB-02 arithmetic said the door was not.

### HB-05 Break or avoid sharp bends on high-flow routes
- Rule: Do not put a 90° bend in a route carrying significant two-way flow. Prefer a chamfer (two 135° steps) or a fan-out, and where a hard corner is unavoidable widen the outside of the turn.
- Evidence: A controlled pedestrian study of five bend angles (0°, 45°, 90°, 135°, 180°) in 1.5 m-wide corridors found that **speeds are significantly different across the space within the bend for every angle except 0°**, that **average walking speeds are significantly lower near the inner corner than the outer corner**, that **densities are higher in the vicinity of the bend than at the corridor start and end** (peaking near 3 p/m²), and that shockwaves and stop-and-go "turbulent" conditions appear near inner corners. The authors' own design recommendation: "instead of one sharp (or larger) turning angle a series of smaller angles could be placed when designing corners." The same study reported no significant speed difference between 135° and 180°, i.e. beyond ~135° the penalty is largely gone.
- Source: "Pedestrian flow characteristics through different angled bends: Exploring the spatial variation of velocity", *PLOS ONE* 17(3):e0264635, 2022 — https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0264635 (open access: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8893709/) — T2.
- Class: FACT (also argued as: / DESIGN PRINCIPLE)
- Scope: routes with modelled flow above a low threshold; not applicable to low-flow room-to-room links
- Confidence: High (single well-controlled experiment, stated design recommendation quoted) — class kept: the measured velocity drop inside every non-zero bend is from the read PLOS ONE study; the flow threshold that decides when the rule bites (`flow_p_min > 20`) is this project's working value, and the "prefer a chamfer" remedy is the study's own stated recommendation
- Grid translation: Detect bends: consecutive route direction changes at a walkable tile where the incoming and outgoing flow axes differ by 90° or more. Validation: (a) any tile at the *inside* corner of a ≥ 90° bend on a route with `flow_p_min > 20` must be part of a locally widened (≥ 3-tile) zone, or the bend must be replaced by two 45° steps (a 1-tile chamfer is exactly a 45° pair on this grid); (b) report `sharp_bends_on_high_flow_routes` and drive to zero; (c) apply a bend friction of +1.5 tile-steps per un-widened 90° corner in route-cost comparisons (`HEURISTIC`, Confidence Low).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Corners are the *mechanism* of privacy (TH-09: a legible territorial boundary needs a turn). A low-flow private corridor may turn sharply at no cost. Symptom: an agent removes all corners to buy octile efficiency, and the resulting open plan fails room typing and the privacy gradient.

### HB-06 Cap decision points per trip, and make every junction asymmetric
- Rule: Count the number of junctions a typical route passes through and cap it per user group; where the cap cannot be met, ensure each junction has one branch that is physically dominant (wider, straighter, better-lit by configuration). Signage cannot compensate for a plan that asks the same question at ten identical crossroads.
- Evidence: Peer-reviewed wayfinding work finds that route difficulty rises with the interconnection density of the layout (Werner & Schindler 2004, reported in the HERD review: "higher interconnection density (ICD) correlates with wayfinding problems"), and that people preferentially act on the physical affordance rather than the symbol — "over 40% chose exit doors over signs when available" (Tang et al. 2009, same review). Passini's account of wayfinding as a decision sequence (TH-19) makes the count of decisions, not the count of signs, the design variable; an open-access integrative review supplies the operational definition the grid metric uses — wayfinding is "a problem-solving process of determining and navigating a route to a destination", and the decision points are "nodes" at which "two or more alternatives exist" (Jamshidi et al. 2020, reporting O'Neill 1991a).
- Source: "Wayfinding in Healthcare Facilities: Contributions from Environmental Design and Cognition Research", HERD — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4287692/ — T2 (quotes are verbatim from the review's synthesis of the two cited studies); Paul Passini, *Wayfinding in Architecture* — T3, catalogue https://searchworks.stanford.edu/view/2376159; Jamshidi et al., "Wayfinding in Interior Environments: An Integrative Review", *Frontiers in Psychology*, 2020, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2020.549628 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7677306/ — T2, open access, read directly (source of the node/alternative definition behind `decision_points(path)`; no effect size taken from it).
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal; binds hardest in hospital, hospitality, education, transit, and any publicly-accessible multi-floor plan
- Confidence: Medium-High (review-level synthesis; effect directions consistent, magnitudes not transferable to a generic grid)
- Grid translation: Junction = walkable tile with ≥ 3 open neighbour directions; merge adjacent junction tiles into one node. Validation: (a) `decisions_per_trip` for each OD pair = junction count on the A\* path; cap per role (guest arrival→room ≤ 4, patient→clinic ≤ 6, staff resupply ≤ 3 — `HEURISTIC`, Confidence Low; the *metric* is the point, the caps are project-settable); (b) at every junction compute `branch_asymmetry = widest_branch_tiles − second_widest_tiles` and require > 0 on public routes (extends TH-19); (c) `symmetric_junction_count` reported and driven to zero.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Deliberate manifolds (a queue feeder before a bank of counters, a fare gate line) are symmetric by design and correct. Detention and sterile-area plans invert the rule: multiple identical branches are a security feature. Symptom: the plan is legible at eye level but each trip in the model crosses eight junctions at `asymmetry = 0` — this is the maze failure that TH-19 and TH-20 both detect.

### HB-07 Reject the symmetric, repeating floor plate
- Rule: A floor whose corridors and room fronts are translation- and mirror-symmetric with no distinguishing landmark is the single most disorienting configuration in the wayfinding literature. Repeat the *cell*, not the *vista*: vary the junction spacing, terminate at least one axis on a distinguishable space, and make each branch of the repeated pattern differ in width or length.
- Evidence: In a controlled comparison of two regular buildings, **63.2% of participants in the regular, symmetrical building felt "completely lost" during a tour, in contrast to only 6.5% of those in the regular, asymmetrical setting** (Baskaya et al. 2004, quoted verbatim in the HERD wayfinding review). Lynch's "imageability" argument (TH-09, TH-19) gives the mechanism: a legible image needs distinguishable elements, and symmetry removes the cues that let a person tell one junction from another.
- Source: HERD wayfinding review https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4287692/ — T2 (source of the Baskaya figure); Kevin Lynch, *The Image of the City*, MIT Press 1960 — T3 (teaching PDF https://cus.ubt-uni.net/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Kevin-Lynch-The-Image-of-the-City.pdf).
- Class: FACT (the experimental contrast)
- Scope: all multi-junction plate types: hospitality floors, hospital wings, school corridors, office plates
- Confidence: Medium-High — the contrast is large and the direction is robust; the absolute percentages come from one study of two specific buildings [classification: the rule also carries DESIGN PRINCIPLE for its part of the claim - the field holds one label per spec §27]
- Grid translation: Compute the plan's automorphism profile: `symmetry_orders` = number of non-trivial translations/reflections of the blocked-tile pattern that map the walkable graph onto itself. Validation: `symmetry_order > 1` on a publicly-accessible floor raises a flag; require that ≥ 30% of junctions differ from their symmetry partner in `width_tiles` or `length_to_next_junction`, and require that each repeated corridor terminates on a room whose tag is not shared by its mirror partner (`landmark_present`).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Symmetry is legitimate (and desirable) where the users are staff who know the plan, or where the product is the symmetry (civic, ceremonial). Repetition cost must also be weighed against the manufacturing gain in TH-23's kit-of-parts. Symptom: an agent reuses one corridor module four times because it is cheap, and every trip in the model has near-identical alternative costs — a flat `branch_asymmetry` field-wide.

### HB-08 Keep the public route network sparse; segregation is a feature you can compute
- Rule: Maximise reachability without maximising the number of links. A room graph where everything connects to everything is harder to navigate, not easier. Hold `mean_connectivity` (doors per room) low on public routes and pay for reach with a few strong spines.
- Evidence: The space-syntax argument is that simpler routes to more destinations, not more doors, produce accessibility: "an accessible segment is more easily reached than a segregated one because it can be arrived at by simpler routes from other segments" and "a more accessible segment should be more likely to be selected as part of a route between other pairs of segments" (Space Syntax Ltd). The wayfinding review's interconnection-density finding (Werner & Schindler, in HB-06) is the interior, measured counterpart: more links, more reported wayfinding problems.
- Source: Space Syntax Ltd, *The Space Syntax Approach* https://spacesyntax.com/the-space-syntax-approach/ — T3 (quotes verified in TH-11/TH-20); HERD wayfinding review https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4287692/ — T2; Hillier & Hanson, *The Social Logic of Space*, Polity 1984 — T3.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal; inverted for detention and healthcare clean/unclean separation, where extra links are deliberately absent for control
- Confidence: Medium (the two evidence streams agree in direction; the indoor sample is small)
- Grid translation: On the room graph (nodes = flood-fill rooms, edges = door runs) compute `mean_connectivity = 2 × edges / nodes` and `icd = edges / (maximum possible edges)`. Validation: require `mean_connectivity ≤ 3.0` for publicly-reachable room sets and `icd ≤ 0.25` (both thresholds project-settable, `HEURISTIC`, Confidence Low); require that removing any single non-spine door leaves the room graph connected (no orphan) — i.e. sparsity without fragility. Report the count alongside the integration ranking (TH-20) so the two cannot be traded silently.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Guest-room floors legitimately have connectivity ≈ 2 per room (bedroom + bathroom) and a spine corridor; retail units need many frontages. Symptom: a plan with 6 doors into one public room because each neighbour got its own opening — high ICD, poor legibility, and every route through it is ambiguous.

### HB-09 Put the destination in view before you write a sign
- Rule: For each primary destination, require that it is visible from the junction where the decision to go there is taken; if it is not, either relocate it or place a distinguishable intermediate landmark on the axis. Signs are the fallback where sightlines cannot reach.
- Evidence: Over 40% of test subjects chose an exit door over a sign pointing to the same place when both were available (Tang et al. 2009, in the HERD review) — physical opportunity beats symbolic instruction. On the commercial side, a study of **153 retail stores** measured visibility with object-based isovists and found rent rises with visible area (≈ IDR 40.74 per m² per month per m²·person of visibility) with **layout variation explaining 38.4% of the price difference** — the value of a unit is largely a function of how much of it can be seen from the flow. Both point to sightline exposure as the first-order layout variable.
- Source: HERD wayfinding review https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4287692/ — T2; "Impact of visibility on indoor retail store rent", *J Property Investment & Finance* — https://ideas.repec.org/a/eme/jpifpp/jpif-01-2022-0004.html — T2; visibility graph analysis method: "Application of visibility analysis and visualisation in hospital wayfinding" — https://scispace.com/pdf/application-of-visibility-analysis-and-visualisation-in-2pncam2pz9.pdf — T2 (method cited; no number taken).
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: hospitality, healthcare, retail, civic, education; weaker for staff who navigate from memory
- Confidence: Medium-High for the direction; the IDR figures are single-city, non-transferable in magnitude
- Grid translation: Compute a per-floor visibility graph on walkable tiles (a tile sees another if the segment between centres crosses no blocked tile; door tiles transmit, walls do not). Metrics: `visible_share_of_destinations` from each junction, `isovist_area_tiles` for each room's entry point, and `sign_demand = junctions where the next-leg destination is not visible`. Validation: require `visible_share ≥ 0.6` at the junctions on the top-decile trips, and require every public destination to be visible from at least one of the junctions on its A\* approach; report `isovist_area_tiles` per unit and rank retail/amenity placements by it.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Privacy and infection control both *reduce* visibility deliberately (a consultation room, a soiled-utility room, a hotel suite threshold). The rule applies to the public destination set only. Symptom: an amenity placed at the correct integration rank (TH-20) but around two corners from every approach — computationally central, experientially hidden.

### HB-10 Give every queue its own pocket, sized in tiles, and keep it off the through-route
- Rule: Wherever a service point (desk, counter, gate, scanner, lift, cloakroom) will make people stand, allocate a standing pocket of explicit area, positioned so the queue forms outside the moving stream and never blocks a door run or a route turn.
- Evidence: Published queue level-of-service bands give standing space directly: **LOS A ≥ 1.21 m²/ped down to LOS F < 0.19 m²/ped, with queue capacity at 2 ft²/pedestrian ≈ 0.19 m²/ped** (FHWA Section 3, Table 21). The IMO evacuation guideline treats a queue as a distinct flow state — density **≥ 3.5 p/m²** (≈ 0.29 m²/ped ≈ 1.1 tiles) or a flow differential > 1.5 p/s — i.e. standing crowd is a *predicted* condition to be provided for, not an accident. Waiting-distance work finds preferred interpersonal distance of **1.0–1.2 m regardless of surrounding density** (Küpper & Seyfried 2023): people hold that spacing while stationary even when they tolerate less while moving.
- Source: FHWA Section 3 https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/safety/pedbike/98107/section3.cfm — T1; MSC.1/Circ.1238 https://puc.overheid.nl/doc/PUC_1952_14/ — T1; "Waiting in crowded places: influence of number of pedestrians and interpersonal spacing", *Sci Rep* 2023 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10498346/ — T2.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal where any service point exists; all building types
- Confidence: High for the area bands; Medium for the 1.0–1.2 m lateral spacing (measured in a waiting context, one study) — was `STANDARD`; demoted: FHWA and MSC bands publish area-per-pedestrian level-of-service values, no read document requires a queue to be given its own reserved pocket off the through-route; that obligation is this file's design rule standing on the published areas
- Grid translation: For each service point with a fixture tag (desk, counter, gate, lift portal, bar), reserve `queue_tiles = peak_queue_persons × q tiles`, with `q = 4` tiles/person for a comfortable/LOS-A queue (1.0–1.2 m spacing → 1 tile lateral × 1 tile depth per 0.5 m, minimum 2 tiles/person laterally) and `q = 1` tile/person as the absolute packed floor (0.25 m² ≈ 4 p/m², above the IMO queue density — never the design value). Validation: (a) the reserved pocket must be contiguous and must not overlap any tile on a top-decile A\* through-route; (b) no queue pocket may abut a door run such that the standing head blocks the run; (c) assert `queue_tiles` ≥ `arrival_rate × mean_service_time × q` (Little's law on the queue length); (d) report `queue_spill_tiles = required − provided`, which must be 0.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Mobile/queue-less service (room-service delivery in hotels, table service, kiosk check-in, gate-free retail) legitimately removes the pocket — but then the load reappears elsewhere (lift, corridor) and must be re-counted, not assumed away. Symptom: a beautiful lobby whose only defect is that the modeled queue of 12 at the desk runs back through the entrance door and cuts off the amenity route.

### HB-11 Size the lift and stair lobby for the queue that will form in it
- Rule: Treat the lift lobby as a standing-capacity problem, not as leftover corridor. Provide standing area for the peak waiting population, keep the boarding zone clear, and never allow the waiting area to be the only circulation route past it.
- Evidence: The IBC codifies this for occupant-evacuation elevator lobbies: the lobby "shall accommodate, at **3 square feet (0.28 m²) per person, not less than 25 percent of the occupant load** of the floor area served by the lobby", plus "one wheelchair space of 30 inches by 48 inches (760 mm by 1220 mm) for each 50 persons, or portion thereof". 0.28 m² = 1.12 tiles per waiting person. Empirically, waiting-population spacing preferences (1.0–1.2 m, HB-10) imply more like 2–2.4 tiles per person for an acceptable queue; and crowd flow through the door itself is capped at 1.3 p/(m·s) (HB-02), so a lobby narrower than the car's discharge footprint cannot drain.
- Source: IBC 2023 FBC Ch. 30 §3008.6.4 via UpCodes https://up.codes/viewer/florida/fl-building-code-2023/chapter/30/elevators-and-conveying-systems — T1 (code adoption varies by jurisdiction; the requirement quoted is for occupant-evacuation lobbies); MSC.1/Circ.1238 — T1; Küpper & Seyfried 2023 — T2.
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT (the 3 ft² / 25 % figure)
- Scope: any floor served by a lift portal; the evacuation-lobby clause applies only where occupant-evacuation elevators are required
- Confidence: High for the code text as retrieved; Medium for generalising it to non-evacuation lobbies [classification: the rule also carries STANDARD for its part of the claim - the field holds one label per spec §27]
- Grid translation: `lobby_waiting_tiles ≥ ceil(0.25 × floor_occupant_load) × 1.12` as the code floor, and `× 2` (2.2 tiles/person) as the comfort target; round up to whole tiles. Add a 2 × 3-tile clear patch (760 × 1220 mm ≈ 1.5 × 2.4 tiles) per 50 occupants. Validation: (a) `lobby_tiles ≥ required`; (b) the 1.5-tile-deep band directly in front of each portal door tile is free of any other door run, queue pocket or fixture — this is the boarding zone; (c) the lobby must not be the sole route to any other room (min-cut test from TH-11), so that a full lobby does not lock the floor.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Single-storey and horizontal types have no lobby and the check is void. Ground-floor arrival lobbies need far more than the code floor because they absorb everyone at once (see HB-13's peak model). Symptom: an agent gives each upper-floor lift hall exactly 4 tiles, and the queue model shows a standing population of 11 at morning peak, with the surplus spilling onto the through-route and reducing `effective_width_tiles` in HB-02 mid-simulation.

### HB-12 Design the wait, not only the wait time
- Rule: Wherever a wait exists, engineer its *perception*: make it occupied, make progress visible, make the expected duration knowable, make it look fair, and let people wait in groups rather than alone. A 90-second wait that reads as uncertain and unfair is worse operationally than a 3-minute wait that reads as explained and progressing.
- Evidence: Maister's eight principles of waiting-line psychology, as published by the author: (1) "Occupied Time Feels Shorter Than Unoccupied Time"; (2) "People Want to Get Started"; (3) "Anxiety Makes Waits Seem Longer"; (4) "Uncertain Waits Are Longer than Known, Finite Waits"; (5) "Unexplained Waits Are Longer than Explained Waits"; (6) "Unfair Waits Are Longer than Equitable Waits"; (7) "The More Valuable the Service, the Longer the Customer Will Wait"; (8) "Solo Waits Feel Longer than Group Waits". Two experimental results bound how much to claim: an ED randomised trial (n = 100) found that giving patients a waiting-time estimate did **not** significantly change satisfaction (5.92 vs 5.45, p = 0.476) though most preferred having one; and a laboratory queue study (30 participants, 1,416 waiting trials) found stress rising significantly with perceived wait but **no effect of observed queue length**. Direction of the principles is well-established practice; several individual mechanisms are weakly or not replicated.
- Source: D. Maister, "The Psychology of Waiting Lines" (1985/2006) — http://davidmaister.com/blog/201/ — T3 (author's own publication; also hosted at http://www.columbia.edu/~ww2040/4615S13/Psychology_of_Waiting_Lines.pdf); Alrajhi KN et al., *Saudi Med J* 2020;41(8):883–886 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7502966/ — T2; "How long is too long? Examining waiting times and stress in human queues" — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12369606/ — T2.
- Class: HEURISTIC
- Scope: all customer-facing wait points; weaker for staff self-service points
- Confidence: Medium for the set as design guidance; Low for any single principle as a predicted effect size
- Grid translation: Convert the principles into layout facts that can be checked: (a) `progress_visible` — the waiting pocket must have an unobstructed sightline (visibility-graph edge) to the service point it is queuing for; (b) `occupied_waiting` — within the waiting pocket, count amenity fixtures (seating, display, drinking water, menu board, product frontage) per 8 tiles, require ≥ 1 at high-wait points; (c) `fair_order` — one feeder line serving n counters, or physically channelised lanes, rather than n independent lanes visible side-by-side (n parallel lanes of visibly unequal length is the "unfair" configuration); (d) `group_seating_share` — fraction of waiting seats in clusters of ≥ 2 adjacent tiles facing each other, target ≥ 0.5 in hospitality; (e) `wait_tiles × occupancy ≥ expected_wait_s × arrival_rate × q` so the wait can be *stood* without spilling.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Principle (7) is an argument for *not* over-investing in speed at premium service points; it must not be used to justify under-sizing. In emergencies the whole set is suspended (HB-21). Symptom: a plan with three separate visible queues of visibly different lengths at one service cluster, and no sightline from the waiting seats to the desk.

### HB-13 Model the vertical link as a queue with a peak, and design the floor to that peak
- Rule: Do not treat a lift or stair as a free edge in the graph. Every portal carries an arrival process, a queue, a waiting time and a boarding time, and the plan must be checked against the *peak* interval — check-in/check-out, shift change, end of clinic, bell — not the daily average.
- Evidence: Flow into and out of a portal is capped at the door figure 1.3 p/(m·s) (MSC.1/Circ.1238, HB-02), and standing in the lobby is a coded capacity requirement (IBC §3008.6.4, HB-11), which together mean the portal is a queue, not a link. Hotel fire research documents the behavioural counterpart: during a real high-rise office fire, pre-evacuation times depended on which actions occupants took and which floor they were on (NIST TN 1664), and video analyses of evacuation report delayed response in **35%** of cases, with slow response defined as **≥ 30 s** and mean pre-movement times of **0.68 min with voice messages versus 1.68 min with alarms alone** — the arrival process feeding a portal is bursty and human-scheduled, not Poisson-smooth.
- Source: MSC.1/Circ.1238 https://puc.overheid.nl/doc/PUC_1952_14/ — T1; IBC §3008.6.4 via UpCodes — T1; NIST TN 1664, Kuligowski & Hoskins 2010, *Occupant Behavior in a High-rise Office Building Fire* — https://www.nist.gov/publications/occupant-behavior-high-rise-office-building-fire — T1; "Evacuation behaviors and emergency communications" — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10620751/ — T2.
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: every multi-floor type with a portal; void for single-storey
- Confidence: High for the queue framing; Medium for the specific pre-movement figures (different context: evacuation, not routine)
- Grid translation: For each portal define `peak_arrivals_p_min` (from the programme: e.g. `rooms × 2 guests × checkout_departure_share` for a hotel morning). Compute expected queue by Little's law `L = λW`, with `W = waiting + riding + boarding + walk from lobby to portal door`; boarding time `= persons_per_car × 1.5 s` (`HEURISTIC`, Confidence Low). Validate: (a) `L ≤ lobby_waiting_tiles / 2.2` (HB-11 comfort density) so the queue fits the lobby; (b) report `pct_wait_over_90s` from the M/M/c approximation and `average_wait` per portal, both must be inside project targets; (c) `vertical_trip_cost` used in route selection must be `walk_tiles + wait_tiles_equiv + car_riding_equiv`, never a zero-cost portal edge.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Stair-only, low-occupancy, and single-storey types have no portal queue. Destination-control/reservation systems reduce `W` without changing the grid; if the brief has them, say so in the model, do not silently assume them. Symptom: an agent stacks 12 floors because the A\* path cost looked flat, and the queue diagnostic then shows 40 people in a lobby sized for 9.

### HB-14 Keep service and staff movement out of the guest and public stream
- Rule: Give every operational flow (housekeeping, food, linen, waste, supplies, portering) a route that does not share a corridor with the public flow at peak, or where it must share, provide a physical refuge or a time separation. Write the rule down as: clean must not cross dirty, service must not cross guest.
- Evidence: Hospitality design teaching states the separation rule in exactly these terms ("clean must never cross dirty, and service must never cross guest") and lists the resulting failures concretely: "rooms not ready", "food arrives cold", and guests seeing "the machinery" (Studio Matrx, *Service Flow & Staff Spaces* — T4). Peer-reviewed healthcare work supplies the measured side of the same logic: an infection-control design paper specifies **separate staircases and lifts for waste**, and the operational load of unseparated staff movement is quantified in nurse step-count data (HB-15). The mixed-stream cost is therefore both a perceived-quality problem (guests see the work) and a hygiene/safety problem (the work contaminates the stream).
- Source: Studio Matrx Academy, *Service Flow & Staff Spaces* https://www.studiomatrx.org/students/hospitality-design/service-flow-and-staff-spaces — T4 (rule quoted; no number taken); "Designing Hospital for better Infection Control: an Experience", *Mil Med J Armed Forces India* — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4923482/ — T2; IBC §3008.6.4 for the evacuation-lobby overlap case — T1.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE (the rule)
- Scope: hospitality, healthcare, retail with delivery windows, education with lab/kitchen blocks; near-void in single-stream small offices
- Confidence: High for the principle as professional convention; Medium for the causal link to specific complaints (T4 + reasoning, no controlled study retrieved) [classification: the rule also carries BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION for its part of the claim - the field holds one label per spec §27]
- Grid translation: Tag every trip in the OD matrix with a stream (`public | guest | staff | service | waste`). Validation: (a) compute `stream_overlap_tiles` = walkable tiles used by > 1 stream class at overlapping peaks; require `stream_overlap_tiles / staff_route_tiles ≤ 0.15` for hospitality and healthcare (`HEURISTIC`, Confidence Low); (b) where overlap is unavoidable, require a **refuge bay** — a 3-tile walkable pocket within 8 tiles of every overlap point so a trolley can be passed; (c) check the waste/soiled route never passes a food, clean-storage or pharmacy door tile (crossing test on the A\* paths); (d) service routes must not reduce `effective_width_tiles` on any public route below the HB-02 requirement while loaded.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Small buildings cannot afford two routes; then the correct answer is *time separation* (delivery windows, night housekeeping) and the check becomes a schedule check, not a geometry check. Symptom: a single 2-tile corridor serving both the guest arrival and the linen route — geometrically legal, operationally a daily collision.

### HB-15 Minimise the weighted staff path, because staff repeat it hundreds of times per shift
- Rule: Staff routes are not a rounding error in the circulation budget: they are the dominant operating cost and the dominant source of fatigue. Place support fixtures (pantries, linen, utility, supply, break) so that the *product* of trip frequency and path length is minimised, and measure it before finalising the corridor grid.
- Evidence: Measured nurse walking in a hospital: mean distance per shift **4.17 km on day shifts (883 records), 6.18 km on long-day shifts (991), 4.76 km on night shifts (1,050)**, with the emergency ward longest and call volume a significant predictor — i.e. the distance is a function of the layout and the workload, and it is large. Infection-control design guidance prescribes the local distribution of fixtures that produces those distances: **one hand-wash basin per six beds**, **bed centres at least 8 feet (≈ 2.44 m) apart**, and a **24–32 bed** nursing unit as the working module.
- Source: "Factors Affecting Nurses' Walking Distance: Age, Clinical Ladder Level, Wards, Nurse Calls, Weekend", *J Nurs Manage* 2025 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12411061/ (Osaka repository PDF https://ir.library.osaka-u.ac.jp/repo/ouka/all/102760/JNursManage_2025_5540600.pdf) — T2; "Designing Hospital for better Infection Control", *Mil Med J AFMI* — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4923482/ — T2. **Layout-sensitivity half (added on retrieval):** Obeidat, Bani Younis & Al-Shlool, "Investigation into the impact of nursing unit layout on critical care nurses", *Heliyon* 8 (2022) e08929, DOI 10.1016/j.heliyon.2022.e08929 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8850728/ — T2, open access, abstract read twice with consistent extraction; mean walked distance 2.28 km in the unit reported as private-room, vs 2.87 km and 2.96 km in the two reported as open-ward, with station centrality named as a driver — the same role covering 26–30 % more ground purely from layout, which is the causal form of this rule that the 2025 study alone does not supply. (Layout descriptors are the article's own categorisation, carried at one remove; the km figures are the abstract's.)
- Class: FACT (the measured distances)
- Scope: hospitals, hotels, large offices, schools, retail back-of-house — any type with a repeating internal task
- Confidence: High — was `FACT (the distances) / STANDARD (the fixture ratios, in healthcare)`; demoted one limb: the nurse-walk distances are read from a measured study (T2), but no healthcare standard (HTM/FGI) was opened for the support-fixture ratios, so the ratios and the trip-frequency table are working planning values, not a standard's allocation [classification: the rule also carries HEURISTIC for its part of the claim - the field holds one label per spec §27]
- Grid translation: Define staff trip classes with frequencies (per occupied room, per shift): housekeeping cart round, linen fetch, waste run, meal run, supply fetch, break walk. Compute `staff_m_per_shift = Σ (trips × octile_steps × 0.5 m)` from the built plan. Validation: (a) compare against a project target set from the 4–6 km measured band and the programme's occupancy — e.g. flag any floor whose staff walking exceeds the target by > 25%; (b) enforce fixture ratios directly on tiles: a support/utility room within **24 tiles (12 m)** of at least 90% of the rooms it serves (`HEURISTIC`, Confidence Medium — a translation of the "basin per six beds" locality logic, not a quoted standard); (c) bed/fixture spacing check: 8 ft centre-to-centre = 4.9 tiles → assert ≥ 5 tiles between bed centres and ≥ 3 clear tiles at one side.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Over-distributing support space kills net area and creates unsupervised dead corners. The minimisation objective is `trips × distance`, so a fixture used once per shift can be far; one used 40 times cannot. Symptom: an agent places the sole utility room at the far end of a 60-tile corridor and the model shows 8 km/shift; or the mirror error, six oversized utility rooms eating the plate.

### HB-16 Keep sleeping and long-stay rooms away from noise-generating shared elements
- Rule: Position is an acoustic intervention. Rooms whose function requires quiet must not share a wall with, or open directly onto, a lift lobby, stair door, service room, plant, public corridor, or the street-facing side of a busy route. Test this geometrically before you buy absorption.
- Evidence: Noise is one of the strongest measured predictors of a bad guest stay: in a survey of **609 hotel guests**, attributes raising the odds of poor sleep included **hallway noise (OR 2.01)**, **noise from the adjacent room (OR 2.07)**, **outside noise (OR 2.23)** and **AC/heater noise (OR 1.57)** — all four are layout-and-fixture decisions, not décor. Hospitals show the same mechanism at larger scale: a systematic review of **33 studies** measured daytime ward noise at **Leq 37–88.6 dB(A)** and night-time at **38.7–68.8 dB(A)**, against WHO guidance of **≤ 35 dB(A) by day and ≤ 30 dB(A) at night**, and names staff conversation, alarms, doors and trolleys as the sources — i.e. the corridor *is* the noise source. Open-office research supplies the third-party confirmation: dissatisfaction with privacy is highest in conventional open-plan layouts (469 participants, 26 organisations, 7 office categories).
- Source: "Examining key hotel attributes for guest sleep and overall satisfaction" — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10130565/ — T2; "Environmental noise in hospitals: a systematic review" — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7935697/ — T2 (WHO targets as reported there; WHO *Guidelines for Community Noise* is the primary T1 source); Danielsson & Rogström, "Noise and Perceived Privacy – Flexible Office Space Matters" — https://acoustics.org/pressroom/httpdocs/155th/danielsson.htm — T2.
- Class: FACT
- Scope: hospitality, healthcare, residential, education, office (long-occupancy and concentration rooms generally)
- Confidence: High for the direction and the hotel ORs; Medium for the exact ORs (from one survey) — note that a second extraction of the same table reported outside noise at OR 2.37, so treat magnitudes as approximate — class kept: the measured association is the FACT; the `HEURISTIC` label in the Grid translation attaches only to the project-settable tile thresholds (≤ 1 shared wall tile, 4 m portal distance), and the geometric prohibition is this file's design response to the measurement
- Grid translation: For each quiet room compute three geometric exposures against the built plan: `shared_wall_tiles` with a portal/lobby/service/plant room; `distance_tiles` to the nearest portal door tile and to the nearest public-corridor tile; `facade_exposure` to the street edge. Validation: (a) quiet rooms must share ≤ 1 wall tile with a lift/stair portal housing or a service room; (b) `distance_tiles ≥ 8` (4 m) from any bedroom head-wall tile to a portal door tile (`HEURISTIC`, Confidence Low, project-settable); (c) rooms whose only door opens directly onto a lobby get a threshold — a 2-tile vestibule or an off-axis door — so the corridor path does not run past the open door; (d) report `quiet_rooms_exposed`, target 0.
- Exceptions / failure mode: The grid has no material model, so this rule cannot see a well-built party wall — it can only penalise proximity and shared openings. Where the brief specifies acoustic separation, state the assumption; where it does not, geometry is the only evidence available. Symptom: a hotel floor with two of eight rooms directly opposite the lift, which is precisely the room the operations team will complain about forever.

### HB-17 Measure privacy as doors plus exposed sightline, at proxemic distances
- Rule: Privacy in a plan is not a mood; it is (a) the number of controlled boundaries between arrival and the space, and (b) how much of the space's interior can be seen by someone who has not crossed them. Specify both, and lay the space's fixtures out at proxemic distances so that a person can occupy it without being inside a stranger's personal zone.
- Evidence: Altman's framing of privacy as boundary regulation (TH-06) is operationalised here with measured spacing: Hall's proxemic bands, as commonly reported — **intimate 0–0.46 m, personal 0.46–1.22 m, social 1.2–3.7 m, public > 3.7 m** — and the finding that people hold roughly **1.0–1.2 m of preferred distance while waiting irrespective of surrounding density** (Küpper & Seyfried 2023). Office research confirms that it is *perceived* privacy, largely a function of visual and speech exposure, that drives dissatisfaction rather than raw density (Danielsson & Rogström, n = 469). Hospital evidence gives the payoff: in a cohort of **83,635 inpatients**, being farther from the ward entrance was associated with **critical illness OR 1.15 (1.08–1.23)**, **mortality OR 1.16 (1.02–1.33)** and **+13 h length of stay** — spatial exposure and remoteness are not cosmetic.
- Source: Proxemics bands: EBSCO Research Starter, *Proxemics* https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/social-sciences-and-humanities/proxemics/ and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proxemics — T3/T4 (concept and values as commonly reported; Edward T. Hall, *The Hidden Dimension*, 1966 is the primary); Küpper & Seyfried 2023 — T2; Danielsson & Rogström — T2; room-location cohort — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6520200/ — T2; Altman, *The Environment and Social Behavior* — T3.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: residential, hospitality, healthcare, education, office; deliberately waived in retail halls, markets and transit concourses
- Confidence: Medium-High for the framing; Low for the exact proxemic numbers as interior design dimensions (culturally and individually variable, and the retrieved values are secondary reports)
- Grid translation: (a) **Nesting depth** = door-run count on the A\* path from the public entrance (TH-07's measure, restated as a privacy metric); (b) **Sightline intrusion**: from every walkable tile the subject does *not* have permission to enter (public corridor, other guest's route), count the interior tiles of the room visible through its door run using the visibility graph from HB-09; require `visible_interior_share ≤ 0.25` for private rooms and `= 0` for toilets, nursing rooms, consultation rooms — the mechanical fix on this grid is an off-axis door or a 2-tile vestibule; (c) **Proxemic spacing**: 0.46 m ≈ 1 tile (intimate boundary), 1.2 m ≈ 2.4 tiles (personal), 3.7 m ≈ 7.4 tiles (public). Assert ≥ 2 tiles between facing seats that strangers may use, ≥ 5 tiles between a waiting seat and a conversation seat, and ≥ 1 clear tile behind every seated position that faces a route.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Sightline exposure is *wanted* for supervision in a nursery, a reception counter and a retail till — so the check must be run per room tag, never globally. Symptom: a "private" consultation room whose door opens straight onto the corridor and 60% of whose floor is visible from it, passing the door-count test while failing the exposure test.

### HB-18 Do not buy supervision with sightlines alone
- Rule: Treat "visibility from the station improves outcomes" as an unproven hypothesis. If the brief requires supervision, provide it by explicit staff presence, call systems, and short paths — and state the visibility claim as an assumption, not evidence.
- Evidence: The best available outcome study directly tested this and found it **did not hold**: in 83,635 adult inpatients, "location of a patient at admission in relation to the nurse's station did not impact their outcome," while distance from the *ward entrance* did (critical illness OR 1.15; mortality OR 1.16; LOS +13 h). Visibility-focused inpatient design remains common practice (Karki, *Visibility-Based Hospital Inpatient Unit Design*, Louisville dissertation; "Measuring the structure of visual fields in nursing units", Health Design knowledge repository), i.e. the design convention outruns the outcome evidence.
- Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6520200/ — T2; dissertation https://ir.library.louisville.edu/context/etd/article/5458/viewcontent/Dissertation_Uttam_Karki.pdf — T2; https://www.healthdesign.org/knowledge-repository/measuring-structure-visual-fields-nursing-units-0 — T3 (403 on retrieval; title listed, no claim taken); "Productivity, satisfaction, work environment and health after relocation to an open-plan office" — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8304243/ — T2 (listed; no claim taken).
- Class: HEURISTIC (as a design driver) — and a documented null result
- Scope: healthcare, education, retail, custody — every "line of sight" claim
- Confidence: High that the single large study found no station-proximity effect; Low that this settles the question (one retrospective design, one country, admission-location only)
- Grid translation: Do not score a plan for supervision from `isovist_area` alone. If a brief demands it, compute and report `visible_room_share` (fraction of occupied tiles in supervised rooms visible from a staff fixture tile) **as an annotation, not as a credit**, and separately satisfy the claim with measurable proxies: `staff_to_room_path_tiles ≤ k`, `count_of_call_points`, and `staff_presence_tiles` at the required hours. Validation: reject any design rationale whose *only* justification is "the nurse/reception/teacher can see everyone"; require a second, path-or-fixture-based justification.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Sightlines do demonstrably support *perceived* safety in outdoor and public-space settings (Newman's defensible space, TH-09) — that literature is about crime and territory, not clinical outcomes, and should not be imported here as if it answered this question. Symptom: an expensive centralised pod-and-spiral ward justified solely by visibility, with a longer average staff path than the perimeter alternative.

### HB-19 Give circulation a standing margin, because that is where informal contact happens
- Rule: If the brief values incidental interaction (community, collaboration, supervision, retail dwell), the design lever is the width and fixtures of circulation, not the meeting room. Provide standing and sitting room inside the movement zone at junctions, lifts and stair landings, sized from measured co-presence.
- Evidence: In a diary study of **274 residents** of four high-rise buildings (19–36 floors), **46% of reported social interactions took place in circulation areas (corridors, lobbies, halls)**, against 16.2% in open/green space and 15.9% in the home — circulation is the dominant encounter medium in the one residential type where it can be measured. Space-syntax theory gives the mechanism: shared, integrated routes are where co-presence is structurally likely, and "a more accessible segment should be more likely to be selected as part of a route between other pairs of segments". Open-office work adds the negative case: when the layout supplies no semi-private back-up space, the same exposure becomes the complaint ("insufficient back-up rooms or more flexible work ways", n = 469).
- Source: Nguyen et al., "Where do People Interact in High-Rise Apartment Buildings?", *IJERPH* 2020 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7369851/ — T2; Space Syntax Ltd — T3; Danielsson & Rogström — T2.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: residential towers, offices, campuses, hotels with a social brief, healthcare waiting; not for service corridors or detention
- Confidence: Medium (one diary study, one housing type, self-reported encounters)
- Grid translation: At each junction node (HB-06) and each portal landing, reserve `standing_tiles = max(4, ceil(expected_co_present × 2))` walkable tiles that are inside the movement zone but outside the swept through-route (a tile is "through-route" if it lies on ≥ 3 top-decile A\* paths). Validation: (a) `encounter_capacity = Σ standing_tiles / 2` persons must be ≥ the modelled co-presence at peak; (b) at least one seating fixture within 3 tiles of each landing on residential and office floors where social interaction is a programme goal; (c) `standing_tiles` must not violate HB-02's width arithmetic or HB-03's density floor — the margin is additive to the route, never carved out of it.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Standing space in a corridor is also a congestion risk; the difference is whether it sits *off* the main vector. Symptom: an agent widens a corridor to 5 tiles to "encourage interaction" and the through-flow model now shows people crossing a social cluster, dropping `effective_width_tiles`.

### HB-20 Provide standing comfort at 1.0–1.2 m spacing, and treat 1 tile per person as packed
- Rule: Wherever people stand for more than a few seconds, use a lateral spacing of 2 tiles (1.0 m) minimum and 2.4 tiles (1.2 m) as the target; 1 tile per person is packed standing and is a failure state, not a design state.
- Evidence: Küpper & Seyfried (2023) measured preferred interpersonal distance while waiting at roughly **1.0–1.2 m irrespective of surrounding density** — the strongest available direct evidence that the spacing people *choose* when waiting is larger than the spacing they tolerate in a moving crowd. Consistent with this, the FHWA queue band runs from LOS A at **1.21 m²/ped (4.8 tiles)** to capacity **0.19 m²/ped (0.76 tiles)**, and the IMO guideline flags a queue at **≥ 3.5 p/m² (≈ 1.14 tiles/person)** — i.e. one tile per person is at or beyond the *capacity* end of every published band. Note that the brief's working assumption of "2–4 tiles per person is comfortable" sits in the LOS E/F range of the walkway bands (HB-01) and should be corrected: 2–4 tiles/person is a *packed* figure, and comfortable standing begins near 5 tiles/person.
- Source: Küpper & Seyfried 2023 https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10498346/ — T2; FHWA Section 3 Table 21 — T1; MSC.1/Circ.1238 Annex 1 — T1.
- Class: FACT (measured preference)
- Scope: all standing/waiting functions; also governs the lateral spacing of standing fixtures (counters, bars, urinals, checkouts)
- Confidence: High — was `STANDARD (bands) + FACT (measured preference)`; demoted the band limb: the 1.0–1.2 m figure is one measured waiting-distance study (Küpper & Seyfried 2023) turned into a project band, not a value published by a standard; FHWA/MSC give area per pedestrian, not lateral spacing [classification: the rule also carries HEURISTIC for its part of the claim - the field holds one label per spec §27]
- Grid translation: Standing-fixture spacing: `lateral_tiles_per_person = ceil(1.0 m / 0.5) = 2` minimum, `2.4 → 3` target, so a queue lane should be laid out **2 tiles wide per person abreast** and **1.5–2 tiles deep per person in line**. Validation: (a) `queue_tiles / queue_persons ≥ 2`; (b) any standing region modelled at < 1.2 tiles/person triggers a packed-standing flag; (c) for a bank of n abreast service positions, aisle-to-counter depth ≥ 3 tiles (1.5 m) so the person being served is not pressed by the next in line.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Boarding/alighting surges, security screening and fare gates compress to the packed band by design and for a few seconds only; that is acceptable, but it must be time-limited in the model, not assumed steady-state. Symptom: an agent computes lobby area at 1 tile/person because "it is only standing", and the comfort check in HB-01 then fails at every LOS band.

### HB-21 Size emergency movement from pre-movement plus flow, and keep the exit where the mental map expects it
- Rule: Evacuation capacity is not walking capacity. Add an explicit pre-movement allowance to every egress calculation, and design so that the primary exit is the one people already believe is there — usually the entrance route reversed. Do not rely on signage to redirect a population.
- Evidence: Video analyses of evacuation report **delayed response in 35% of cases** with a slow-response threshold of **≥ 30 s**, and cite mean pre-movement times of **0.68 min when a voice message was given versus 1.68 min for alarms alone**; the same review names "taking the familiar exit" as a documented risk. NIST's study of a real high-rise office fire found pre-evacuation duration depended on the actions occupants took and the floor they were on. Against that, movement once started is bounded by the flow-per-width figures in HB-02 (1.3 p/(m·s) level, 1.1/0.88 on stairs), and > 40% of test subjects followed an exit *door* rather than a sign (Tang et al. 2009, HB-09's source).
- Source: "Evacuation behaviors and emergency communications" https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10620751/ — T2; NIST TN 1664 https://www.nist.gov/publications/occupant-behavior-high-rise-office-building-fire — T1; MSC.1/Circ.1238 — T1; HERD wayfinding review — T2.
- Class: FACT (behaviour)
- Scope: universal; the pre-movement allowance is type-specific (assembly, healthcare, detention, hospitality highest)
- Confidence: High for the delay effect and the familiarity bias; Medium for using the 0.68/1.68 min figures outside evacuation (they are fire-cue-specific) [classification: the rule also carries STANDARD for its part of the claim - the field holds one label per spec §27]
- Grid translation: `RSET_proxy = pre_move_tiles_equiv + path_tiles / (2.0 tiles/s) + portal_queue_worst_case`. Set `pre_move = 30 s` (0.5 tiles-time units is meaningless — instead add 30 s directly) as the minimum allowance and 100 s where alarms are the only cue. Exit-congruence check: the first junction after every occupied room must have its widest branch (`branch_asymmetry`, HB-06) pointing down the A\* path to the *nearest public entrance*; validate `familiar_exit_match = true` for ≥ 0.9 of room tags, and report `egress_detour_index = evac_path_tiles / entrance_path_tiles ≤ 1.3`. Egress width still follows TH-12; this rule adds the timing and the direction.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Deliberate deflection is correct in high-security and sterile contexts and in "defend-in-place" strategies (hospitals, some high-rises) — then state the strategy and drop the congruence test. Symptom: a plan that passes its width arithmetic but whose evacuation route reverses the arrival axis at the second junction, which is where the 35% would hesitate.

### HB-22 Cap occupancy per room by a declared density band and check it against the room's function
- Rule: Every room's capacity must be computed from a stated m²/person band matched to what people *do* in it, and the plan must be rejected where the declared occupancy exceeds the capacity the tiles support. Do not carry an occupancy figure that has no arithmetic behind it.
- Evidence: The published bands differ by activity and facility type — walkway LOS runs A ≥ 12 m²/ped to F ≤ 0.6 while terminals sit at A+ ≥ 2.3 m²/ped and queues at A ≥ 1.21 m²/ped (FHWA Section 3); the IMO model puts standing crowds at 3.5 p/m² and treats anything denser as stalled; assembly-type occupant-load factors in model codes are the legal counterpart (TH-12's business factor of 150 ft² gross/occupant ≈ 14 m²/person is a *design-occupancy*, not a *standing-density*, figure). The point of the rule is to force the designer to name which band applies.
- Source: FHWA Section 3 — T1; MSC.1/Circ.1238 — T1; IBC §1005 egress sizing via https://up.codes/s/means-of-egress-sizing — T1 (already used in TH-12).
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High — class kept: the m²/person bands are read from FHWA/MSC published level-of-service tables and the code limb is IBC §1005 (as used in TH-12); the `HEURISTIC` label in the Grid translation attaches only to which activity maps to which band, which is this file's assignment
- Grid translation: `capacity(room) = floor(usable_walkable_tiles / tiles_per_person[activity])` with a project table, e.g. seated-with-desks 12 tiles/person (3 m²), seated lounge 6 (1.5 m²), standing reception 3 (0.75 m²), dense queue 2 (0.5 m²), circulation-adjacent standing 2 (0.5 m²) — these are direct conversions of the bands above and are labelled `HEURISTIC` only in their activity mapping, `FACT` in their area. Validation: (a) assert `declared_occupancy ≤ capacity` for every room; (b) assert `Σ capacity of the rooms opening onto a corridor ≤ corridor_flow_capacity × 60 / 39` per minute; (c) report `floor_density_p_per_m2 = occupancy / floor_area` and compare to the 0.5 p/m² movement knee (HB-03).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Rooms designed for transient use (a lobby crossed, not occupied) are correctly excluded from the occupancy sum; fixtures and circulation must be deducted from `usable_walkable_tiles` first, or capacity is over-stated. Symptom: an "80-person function room" whose tile count supports 80 only if no dance floor, bar or serving station is drawn.

### HB-23 Protect concentration and conversation from the through-route
- Rule: Speech privacy and concentration are layout properties. Rooms whose task is conversation or focused work must not open onto, and must not share a thin boundary with, a route carrying through-traffic; provide a buffer room or a turn instead.
- Evidence: The open-plan literature is unusually direct about cause: across 469 participants in 26 organisations and seven office categories, the "greatest levels of dissatisfaction for privacy were reported by those in traditional open plan offices", and the criticism named was insufficient "back-up rooms or more flexible work ways" — that is, the complaint is produced by the absence of a *refuge adjacent to the open area*, i.e. a zoning fact. Hospital noise data identify conversation and corridor activity as principal sources (HB-16). Medical/outpatient evidence on the other side shows that giving people information about a wait does not reliably change satisfaction (Alrajhi, n = 100, p = 0.476) — so the fix is spatial, not informational.
- Source: Danielsson & Rogström https://acoustics.org/pressroom/httpdocs/155th/danielsson.htm — T2; "Workspace satisfaction: the privacy-communication trade-off in open-plan offices" https://escholarship.org/uc/item/2gq017pb — T2 (title listed; body not retrieved this pass); PMC7935697 — T2; Alrajhi et al. — T2.
- Class: STANDARD (the requirement)
- Scope: offices, healthcare consulting, education, hospitality back-of-house meeting rooms
- Confidence: High that the association exists; Medium on the causal chain (survey, not intervention study) [classification: the rule also carries FACT for its part of the claim - the field holds one label per spec §27]
- Grid translation: Tag rooms by task sensitivity (`focus | conversation | quiet | neutral`). Compute `route_load_tiles` = number of movement tiles with `flow_p_min > 20` (HB-02) adjacent to or sharing a wall with each sensitive room. Validation: (a) sensitive room door tiles must not open directly onto a high-load tile — require ≥ 1 intervening walkable tile that is not itself on the top-decile through-route (a vestibule or an off-axis lobby satisfies this); (b) ≥ 1 buffered room or non-sensitive room between any sensitive room and a portal lobby; (c) report `sensitive_rooms_exposed / sensitive_rooms`, target ≤ 0.1; (d) on open floors require `refuge_tiles ≥ 1 tile per 20 open-plan tiles`, which is the direct answer to the named complaint.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Retail floors, markets and workshops accept exposure and should be exempted by tag, not silently. Symptom: a "quiet room" placed where it is the shortest route to the amenity — a room that reads private on the plan and behaves like a corridor in use.

### HB-24 Never let a door run be the weakest link
- Rule: Check every door run twice: as a capacity element (persons per minute through its width) and as a friction element (a pinch where queueing, turbulence and crossflow start). Where a door serves a crowded space, widen the opening, split it into two runs with a pier between, or move it off the corner.
- Evidence: The published door-flow figure is 1.3 p/(m·s) — a 1.0 m (2-tile) run therefore caps at ≈ 78 persons/min — and the IMO guideline treats a flow differential above 1.5 p/s as the signature of a queue forming, i.e. a door that cannot drain its upstream population is a queue generator by definition. FHWA/HCM marks walkway LOS F at ≤ 0.6 m²/ped and the platoon table reaches 59 ped/min/m, so a door narrower than the corridor it serves must produce a standing shock. A NIST-hosted study is titled, precisely, "Questioning the linear relationship between doorway width and pedestrian evacuation flow rate" — the assumption that double the width gives double the flow is itself contested, and effective width is lost at each jamb.
- Source: MSC.1/Circ.1238 — T1; FHWA Section 3 — T1; "Questioning the linear relationship between doorway width and flow rate", NIST publication server https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=861412 — T1 (title and URL verified this pass; PDF body not machine-readable, so no coefficient is taken from it); PLOS ONE bends (HB-05) — T2, for turbulence at constrictions.
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High for the arithmetic and the pinch mechanism; the non-linearity itself is real but unquantified here
- Grid translation: `door_capacity_p_min = 39 × door_run_tiles`. Validation: (a) for every door, `upstream_flow_p_min ≤ door_capacity`, computed from the A\* trip assignment; (b) require `door_run_tiles ≥ 2` at every entrance to a space with capacity > 20, and ≥ 4 (two runs plus a 1-tile pier, or one 2 m run) above 80; (c) require the door not to be within 2 tiles of a 90° bend (HB-05) — a corner immediately outside a door is where the density peak forms; (d) compute `min_cut` over the walkable graph: every door run whose removal disconnects > 10% of occupancy is a single point of failure and needs a second run.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Access control, one-way flows and security vestibules intentionally create pinches; then the standing pocket from HB-10 must be provided on the queue side. Symptom: a wide plan with 1-tile doors everywhere, which passes the corridor width test and fails HB-02 at every threshold.

### HB-25 Encode publicness in the route hierarchy, in tiles, and verify it against the zoning
- Rule: The main route should look, measure and behave like the main route: wider, straighter, better connected and better integrated than its branches. Publicness is a configuration property, and the plan should be tested for it rather than asserted.
- Evidence: The space-syntax claim is exactly that configuration, not finishes, produces publicness: "an accessible segment is more easily reached than a segregated one because it can be arrived at by simpler routes from other segments", and "a more accessible segment should be more likely to be selected as part of a route between other pairs of segments" (Space Syntax Ltd). Measured corroboration in buildings: 46% of resident interactions occurred in circulation space (HB-19), and indoor retail rent tracks measured visibility, with layout explaining 38.4% of price variation (HB-09). Against it, the predictive claim is contested indoors (see `## Weak or contested`), which is why this rule uses configuration for *self-critique*, not forecasting.
- Source: Space Syntax Ltd https://spacesyntax.com/the-space-syntax-approach/ — T3; Hillier, *Space is the Machine*, Cambridge UP 1996 — T3; "Bill Hillier's Legacy: Space Syntax — A Synopsis of Basic Concepts, Measures and Empirical Application", *Sustainability* 13(6):3394, 2021 https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/13/6/3394 — T2; critique: "Limitations, critiques and inconsistencies of the space syntax methodology" https://www.academia.edu/32698940/LIMITATIONS_CRITIQUES_AND_INCONSISTENCIES_OF_THE_SPACE_SYNTAX_METHODOLOGY — T4.
- Class: HEURISTIC (method solid, prediction contested)
- Scope: multi-route types: hospitality, healthcare, civic, education, retail, campus
- Confidence: Medium-High for the correlation between route width and perceived hierarchy (`HEURISTIC`); Low for behavioural prediction indoors
- Grid translation: For each movement tile compute `betweenness_tiles` = number of top-decile A\* paths crossing it (a grid-native "choice" value), and for each room the raw integration of TH-20. Validation: (a) Spearman rank correlation between `betweenness` and `width_tiles` ≥ 0.5 on the movement skeleton — busy routes must be wide; (b) Spearman between `zone_publicness_rank` and `integration_rank` ≥ 0.6 (TH-20's test, restated); (c) the single most-integrated room on each floor must carry a public tag; (d) `spine_continuity`: the top-betweenness tiles must form one connected set from the main entrance to the far end, not a scatter.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Deliberately flat hierarchies (open office, market hall, gallery) must be declared so the test is skipped rather than fudged; control-driven types invert it. Symptom: an equal-width grid of corridors where the "main" route exists only in the parti diagram, and the betweenness/width correlation is ~0.

### HB-26 Do not design to unverified behavioural defaults
- Rule: Refuse layout decisions justified by folk behaviour — "shoppers turn right", "the first metres inside an entrance are a dead decompression zone", "people always walk the shortest line", "a queue feels long at ten people". Either convert the claim into a check that the grid can measure, or record it as an assumption with no design weight.
- Evidence: Each of those claims has a contested literature. Turning direction in stores was examined in *Clockwise versus counterclockwise turning bias* (J. Retailing & Consumer Services, 2022), and the entrance "decompression zone" was directly re-tested under the title "Decompression zone deconstructed: Products located at the store entrance do have an impact on sales" (2018) — the existence of a peer-reviewed *refutation attempt* for a maxim so widely repeated as fact is the point; effect sizes could not be extracted from either paper this pass, so neither may drive a dimension here. Laboratory queue work found **no effect of observed queue length** on stress (30 participants, 1,416 trials), and an ED trial found an information intervention did **not** move satisfaction (n = 100, p = 0.476) — so "shorter looks better" is not safely general. Contrast the claims that *did* survive: bend-speed effects (HB-05), symmetry and disorientation (HB-07), door-affordance over signage (HB-09), flow per metre width (HB-02).
- Source: "Clockwise versus counterclockwise turning bias", *J Retailing & Consumer Services* 2022 — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0969698922000583 — T2 (title verified; body not retrievable); "Decompression zone deconstructed", 2018 — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/328756004_ — T2 (403 on retrieval; title cited); PMC12369606 — T2; PMC7502966 — T2.
- Class: HEURISTIC
- Scope: universal, but most operative in retail and hospitality where folk behavioural claims cluster
- Confidence: High that these claims are not safely usable as design numbers [classification note: and, for the specific claims, `REJECTED AS UNSUPPORTED`]
- Grid translation: Maintain an assumption register beside the plan. Every behavioural statement must carry one of three labels: `measured` (with a source in this file), `heuristic` (with a tile check and a Low/Medium confidence), or `unverified` (design weight zero, and it may not appear in a rationale). Validation gate: reject any generated design note containing a behavioural claim not in the register; reject any dimension whose only justification is an unverified claim.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence: right-turn bias may well exist under specific conditions. The rule is about *design weight*, not about metaphysics. Symptom: an agent that places the till bank on the left because "shoppers turn right", a decision with no measurable basis and no test that could falsify it.

## Weak or contested

- **The brief's "2–4 tiles per person is the comfortable range" is wrong against the published bands.** 2–4 tiles/person = 0.5–1.0 m²/ped, which is HCM walkway **LOS E–F** (0.6–1.4 and ≤ 0.6 m²/ped) and, at the packed end, above the IMO density at which a queue is declared (3.5 p/m² ≈ 1.14 tiles/person). Comfortable standing begins around 5 tiles/person (FHWA queue LOS A = 1.21 m²/ped) and comfortable *walking* around 9–15 tiles/person (LOS C). Rules HB-01 and HB-20 use the sourced bands; the brief's numbers are retained only as the packed-queue floor.
- **Maister's eight waiting principles are practice doctrine, not experimental findings.** They are quoted from the author's own publication (T3). Two mechanisms were directly contradicted or not supported by experiments retrieved here: an ED trial found a waiting-time estimate did not change satisfaction (n = 100, p = 0.476), and a lab queue study found no effect of observed queue length on stress (30 participants, 1,416 trials). Use the principles to *rank design alternatives*, never to predict a satisfaction score. HB-12 encodes them only as checkable geometry (sightline to the service point, fixtures in the waiting pocket, one feeder rather than visible parallel lanes).
- **Space-syntax prediction indoors.** The measures are computable and this file uses them that way, but the claim that integration *predicts* movement, retail value or safety is contested: method-dependence of the graph, correlational evidence, and the large difference between street networks and small interior graphs. The 38.4 %-of-rent-variance figure is from 153 stores in one city with one visibility method; treat the direction, not the magnitude. HB-25 therefore scores *self-consistency* of the skeleton (busy routes should be wide), not forecast footfall.
- **Nurses'-station visibility.** The strongest outcome study retrieved found *no* association, while visibility-driven ward design remains standard practice. Either the metric is wrong (isovist from a station ≠ usable supervision), or the outcome measure is insensitive, or the effect is confounded by ward acuity. HB-18 suspends the claim rather than picking a side. Note also that the positive finding in the same study — distance from the **ward entrance** predicted adverse outcomes — is itself single-study and observational and should not be quoted as established.
- **Proxemic distances.** Hall's bands (intimate 0–0.46 m, personal 0.46–1.22 m, social 1.2–3.7 m, public > 3.7 m) were retrieved from secondary reference material, not from *The Hidden Dimension*; the exact boundaries are known to vary by culture, sex, task and personality. HB-17 and HB-20 use only the 1.0–1.2 m waiting figure, which has a direct primary measurement (Küpper & Seyfried 2023).
- **The bend result is one experiment.** Five angles, 1.5 m corridors, a defined participant set, jogging and walking conditions. The direction (inner lane slower, density higher at bends, ≥ 135° essentially free of penalty) is worth designing to; the "+1.5 tile-steps of corner friction" in HB-05 is an invented ordinal and is labelled HEURISTIC/Low.
- **Door-width non-linearity.** NIST-hosted work exists whose very title questions linear scaling of evacuation flow with doorway width, but its body could not be read in this pass, so HB-24 uses only the code/IMO per-metre figure and adds effective-width losses qualitatively (jamb loss → widen, split, or move off the corner). No discharge coefficient is claimed.
- **Second evidence pass — searched and still not text-verifiable.** A further decision-point-complexity study (*Quantifying Spatial Complexity and Signage Distribution in Large Buildings*, IJGI/MDPI) returned **403 Forbidden** and was read zero, so HB-06's decision-point counts still rest solely on the HERD review's reported figures, not on a primary branch-point census. Two queue-perception candidates were unreachable in machine-readable form: the University of Surrey open-repository copy of *Perceptions of waiting time in different service queues* (redirects to a signed S3 PDF that did not parse) and the Semantic Scholar record of *Shorter Wait Times: The Effects of Various Loading Screens* (returned no extractable content). Neither is cited anywhere in this file, and no wait-perception magnitude was added to HB-12.

- **Hotel guest-noise odds ratios.** One cross-sectional survey (N = 609). Two separate extractions of the same table returned "outside noise" at OR 2.23 and OR 2.37, so treat all four ORs as approximate and the *ordering* (hallway and adjacent-room noise ≈ equally strong) as the usable content. Association, not experimental causation — the rooms were not randomised.
- **Retail behavioural defaults (right-turn, decompression zone).** Both have large trade literatures and at least one peer-reviewed re-test each; no effect size was retrievable in this pass, so HB-26 assigns them zero design weight rather than adopting or denying them.
- **Pre-movement times from fire contexts used for routine design.** The 30 s / 0.68 min / 1.68 min figures are evacuation-cue-specific. They belong in HB-21's safety check and must not be folded into a normal-occupancy queue model — a routine lift queue is not 100 seconds of hesitation.
- **Gaps not closed this pass.** (i) No lift traffic *quality-of-service* numeric band (CIBSE Guide D / EN 81-70 style AWT and % > 30 s targets) could be retrieved from a primary or T1/T3 source; HB-13 therefore prescribes *which* queue measures to compute and to compare against a project target, and names no target value. (ii) Counter/service-desk dimension standards (till-lane length, queue-lane width per retail guidance) were not retrievable; HB-10/HB-20 derive spacing from LOS bands and measured waiting distance instead. (iii) The Baile/LaPointe/McCarty lavatory territorial study, the original Passini decision taxonomy, and HCM's "design pedestrian" body width were all sought and not text-verified — none is cited for a number. (iv) No controlled study linking a *specific* floor-plate feature to a *specific* operational complaint was found outside the office-noise, hotel-noise and hospital-noise surveys, which are all cross-sectional.

## Type-specificity audit

Behavioural evidence is not type-neutral: the flows, waits, privacy needs and staff ratios of a hospital are not those of a hotel. This audit says which rules hold everywhere and which must be re-weighted or switched off per brief.

| Rule | Universal? | Type where it binds hardest | Type where it must be relaxed or inverted |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| HB-01, HB-02, HB-03, HB-04, HB-24 (flow, width, density, pinch) | Universal | assembly, transit, healthcare, education, commercial | detention (control overrides flow), residential (flow so low the checks are trivially passed) |
| HB-05 (bends) | Universal as physics | transit, retail, assembly, hospital main spines | residential and hotel room corridors (flow too low to matter) |

Type-specific flow character (what to plug into the OD matrix per type, as a starting frame — these are programme inputs, not findings from this file's evidence): **hospital** = high-frequency short staff trips dominating total distance (HB-15's 4.17–6.18 km/shift is the design driver), patient trips low-frequency and unsupported, visitor trips high-decision-load and information-hungry (HB-06, HB-07, HB-09 bind hardest), plus a clean/dirty stream split (HB-14). **Hotel** = sharply bimodal guest flow (mass arrival, mass departure) that is a *portal queue* problem (HB-11, HB-13), a staff flow that must be invisible (HB-14) and a room product whose value is destroyed by adjacency noise (HB-16). **Office** = two commuting peaks plus a dense internal micro-flow to shared amenities; the measurable complaints are acoustic and visual privacy and lack of refuge, not capacity (HB-17, HB-23). **Retail** = flow is the product; visibility and route hierarchy determine value (HB-09, HB-25), standing queues dominate service points (HB-10), and this is the type where the most behavioural folklore circulates, so HB-26 matters most. **School** = bell-synchronised corridor surges (HB-02, HB-04, HB-05 at the corridor corners), high decision-load for unfamiliar visitors and young users (HB-06), classroom-interior arrangement effects that are small-sample and not dimension-bearing (Tobia et al., N = 77), and supervision demands that HB-18 refuses to satisfy with isovists alone.
