# Research file — Lobby parti: what real hotel lobby plans repeatedly do (positive model)

Scope: the **consensus arrangement** of a hotel ground-floor lobby, gathered from published built plans,
architect project descriptions, hospitality planning guides and precedent studies — not from rules about what
is wrong. The harness can already reject; this file is the generation target: the default parti a designer
copies before deviating. Each rule is stated as a spatial relation, then as a measurement on the host grid so
it can be *scored* rather than only *checked*.

Target being designed (the constant this file is measured against): rectangular plate **50 m x 30 m = 1500 m²**,
**one** street frontage, single storey in this dataset. Declared loads **502 people present** (470 guests + 32
staff) and **500 walk-ins + 500 walk-outs per day**. Frontage assumed on one 50 m side; depth 30 m.

Host grid: `1 tile = 0.5 m`; `1 m² = 4 tiles`; wall = 1 blocked tile (0.5 m); `2 tiles = 1.0 m`;
`12 m = 24 tiles`; `15 m = 30 tiles`; `30 m = 60 tiles`; `50 m = 100 tiles`. No heights, no section: every
daylight/volume claim below is re-expressed as a *distance from a glazing line* or a *plan-area budget*.

Field shape: `- Rule:` · `- Evidence:` · `- Source:` (URL + tier) · `- Class:` · `- Scope:` · `- Confidence:` ·
`- Checkable as:` (NEW in this file: the relation in metres/tiles on the 50x30 grid) ·
`- Exceptions / failure mode:`.

Evidence tiers as used across this corpus: T1 code/standard/government · T2 peer-reviewed/university ·
T3 established architecture publication or architect's own project documentation · T4 practitioner/consultant/
vendor publication · T5 anecdote. Conflicting figures are **never averaged**: both are printed with their tier.

consensus, and this file does not claim otherwise.

### LP-01 Arrival is a sequence of named rooms, not a door
- Rule: Treat arrival as a **procession** — drop-off/canopy → vestibule → (bell desk + luggage room) → lobby — where each step is a space with its own name and its own finish, and the sequence is what makes the plan read as designed rather than as a placed box.
- Evidence: Autograph 2A.2.A: "Provide a **procession of spaces** that give an energized, streamlined and efficient sense of arrival"; 2A.2.B.1 Sequence: "including the Porte Cochere, accommodate and start the emotional connection with guests by addressing the following functional requirements: Vestibule Entrance · Car Valet Station · Bellman Services · Luggage Room · Concealed Luggage Cart Area · Central Control Station". Two of those six are *services* the guest walks past (valet podium 0.6 × 1.0 m outside; bell podium 0.61 × 1.0 m inside), and one is deliberately hidden ("luggage cart staging: in an enclosed room or alcove, **screened from guest's view**, for staging a minimum of 4 luggage carts"). The same clause demands lobby finishes be extended into the exterior entrance and a "walk-off surface in vestibule" — i.e. the threshold is continuous with the lobby volume, not a separate hard room. Government classification schemes confirm the entrance is a graded space, not a hole in the wall: Abu Dhabi requires the classification certificate "in a prominent place in the reception lobby or reception area" and emergency lighting to "the entrance **and lobby**" as one unit.
- Source: https://crhotelmanagementgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Lobby-Areas.pdf — T4 · https://dct.gov.ae/DataFolder/Files/Hotel-Classification-System-Manual.pdf — T1 (Abu Dhabi Dept of Culture & Tourism, read from the downloaded PDF)
- Class: BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION
- Scope: full-service and upper-upscale; a 3-star city hotel may stop at door + desk.
- Confidence: Medium-high (one brand standard states the sequence explicitly; the second source confirms only the entrance/lobby and entrance/reception grouping).
- Checkable as: on the 50 m frontage, the entry door run must sit behind a defined approach of **≥ 5 m (10 tiles)** measured from the façade line to the first lobby seating group, and the entry run must be preceded by a vestibule tile band (LP-02). Emit `arrival_sequence_depth_m` = facade tile → first lobby-seating tile along the guest path, and `named_entry_spaces_count` (canopy / vestibule / bell / luggage identified by tags); target ≥ 3 of the 5 named steps present.
- Exceptions / failure mode: A plan that puts the front door directly onto the desk (no vestibule, no bell point) reads as a reception office, not a hotel. Symptom on this grid: `arrival_sequence_depth < 3 m` and zero tiles tagged as transitional.

### LP-02 The vestibule has a minimum depth, and the lobby's first furniture is within a few metres of it
- Rule: Give the weather/pressure-lock between outside and lobby at least 2.5 m between the two door lines, and put a seatable waiting group immediately inside — the first 10 m the guest sees is *furniture and people*, not circulation.
- Evidence: Autograph 2A.2.E.1.a: "**Vestibule … Size/Area: Minimum of 2.5 m (8 ft.) between sets of doors**", with the same clause naming what the space must absorb: "arriving and departing guests, luggage handling, guest with disabilities, and emergency exiting". 2A.2.E.4.a sets the door leaf at "0.91 m (3 ft.) wide minimum". 2A.1.B.5 sets the counterparty on the inside: "**Allow for seating / waiting area in close proximity to the building entry.**" Nothing in the standard sets a numeric maximum distance for that seating, so "close proximity" is the practitioner's own words, not a figure — recorded as such.
- Source: https://crhotelmanagementgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Lobby-Areas.pdf — T4
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE (the 2.5 m is a Design Standard; the seating distance is unquantified in the source)
- Scope: universal for hotels with any drive-in or cold/rain/wind climate; stack effect is called out explicitly for high-rise.
- Confidence: High for 2.5 m (stated, contractual, unit-specified); Low for any specific seat distance (**no source found** for a number — the standard says only "close proximity").
- Checkable as: `vestibule_tiles` between outer and inner door runs **≥ 5 tiles (2.5 m)**; door leaf width **≥ 2 tiles (0.91 m → 1.0 m)**; nearest seat tile to the inner door line **≤ 12 tiles (6 m)** — that 6 m is a project-chosen default standing in for the standard's "close proximity", declared as a heuristic, not a published figure.
- Exceptions / failure mode: In a mild climate a loggia replaces the vestibule; do not fail it, but require the door run to still be recessed. Symptom: our current generator places one door tile on the facade line and nothing inside for 15 m — the guest path is 100% "floor you walk on".

### LP-03 The reception desk is beside the arrival path, off-centre, and looks at the lifts
- Rule: The desk is **in the lobby volume** (no wall between it and the lobby), but *not* on the door axis and not in the middle of the room: it sits to one side of the arrival path, close to the entrance, with clear sight of the passenger elevator foyer and of the lounge.
- Evidence: Autograph 2A.3.A.1: "This area is **strategically placed upon entering the hotel but is not in the direct path of entry**. It is best positioned **close to the hotel entrance, and within view of passenger elevator foyer** and/or open stairs." 2A.3.B.2: "Reception stations are strategically positioned, **not in the center of the Lobby, but visually connected to the Lobby Lounge**." 2A.3.C: "Provide **clear line-of-sight to other areas and to passenger elevators**." The three clauses together are the whole rule: adjacent to the path, off the axis, sighted on both the core and the lounge. The standard's own adjacency diagram (2A.1.J "Lobby Areas — Adjacency Diagram") is the drawing that encodes it. Corroborating placement logic from the same document: concierge desk is "convenient to Hotel Entrance, and **next to, but apart from** check-in … **in the path of guest circulation**, readily identifiable … and within visual contact of check-in" — the same "in the flow, off the axis" idea one station further along.
- Source: https://crhotelmanagementgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Lobby-Areas.pdf — T4
- Class: BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION
- Scope: full-service hotels with a staffed desk; see LP-04 for the kiosk/pod inversion.
- Confidence: High that the position is "off-axis + sighted on core and lounge" (three separate clauses in one contractual document); Medium on the exact distance, which the standard never gives.
- Checkable as: (a) desk tiles are inside the space labelled `lobby` (no wall tile run separates desk from lobby seats); (b) straight line door-run → nearest desk tile is **not** the primary axis, i.e. the perpendicular offset of the desk from the entrance-to-lobby-centre line **≥ 3 m (6 tiles)**; (c) unobstructed sight line from the desk face to **≥ 1** lift portal and to **≥ 1** lounge seat group; (d) entrance→desk straight-line distance **≤ 20 m (40 tiles)**. (d) is a project-chosen envelope — the source says "close", not "≤ 20 m".
- Exceptions / failure mode: Two real inversions, both legitimate: the desk *is* the axis statement in a small boutique hotel (a 12-key property needs no off-axis desk), and luxury properties delete the desk entirely in favour of in-room or lobby-side check-in — Autograph itself lists "In room check in option" and a "Mobile Key Self-Serve Kiosk" (see LP-05). Symptom of getting it wrong the other way: desk centred on the door axis, so every walking guest queues in the middle of the room.

### LP-04 Reception geometry is derivable from key count: 1.2 m per station, 1 station per 75-100 rooms, 3.7 m of queuing
- Rule: Size the bank from the room count and the queue, not from taste: 1.2 m of counter per station, one station per 75-100 keys, 1.5 m of clear work depth behind the counter, and 3.7 m of free floor in front of it.
- Evidence: Autograph 2A.3.D.1 verbatim: "a. **Pod Size & Quantity: 1.2 m (4 ft.) minimum per station. Provide one pod (work station) for each 75 to 100 Guestrooms**; determine actual requirements based on property needs. … c. **Work Space: 1.5 m (5 ft.) minimum, clear work space measured from back (employee side) edge of work surface counter to parallel back wall.** … d. **Guest Queuing: 3.7 m (12 ft.) minimum distance in front of counter.**" The document adds that cashier and check-in are integrated into one station, that the behind-desk floor is carpeted "for long-term comfort of standing attendants", and that monitors and equipment are "located out of sight from guest". For the *whole* transaction cluster it gives: concierge desk "20 m² (215 sq. ft.) total. No smaller than a two-person counter or desk in any area or market" and safe deposit "typically **1 box for every 10 guestrooms**". Note the tiers: 1.2/1.5/3.7 m and 20 m² are Design Standards in a contractual document; "one pod per 75-100 guestrooms" is the same document's planning ratio with an explicit "determine actual requirements" escape clause. **No second source in this corpus states a station-per-keys ratio** — India's star guidelines require only "Reception facility" as mandatory and manned ≥16 h; Abu Dhabi requires a "Clearly designated reception area with a nearby seating area" and staff 24 h. Both confirm the desk's *existence and adjacency to seats*, not its length.
- Source: https://crhotelmanagementgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Lobby-Areas.pdf — T4 · https://nidhi.tourism.gov.in/uploads/gallery/1667476361.pdf — T1 (Ministry of Tourism, India, read from the downloaded PDF) · https://dct.gov.ae/DataFolder/Files/Hotel-Classification-System-Manual.pdf — T1 · https://pdfcoffee.com/design-guide-for-hotels-pdf-free.html — T4/T5 ("DESIGN GUIDE FOR HOTELS", uploader "Guest", author not printed on the page: quoted figures are verbatim from the page, its provenance is not established) · https://crhotelmanagementgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/12-Auto-Collect-Module-12-Elevators.pdf — T4 (occupants-per-room, used to convert guests present → keys)
- Class: BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION (the three widths), HEURISTIC (the 75-100 keys ratio, single-sourced)
- Scope: staffed-desk hotels; the 3.7 m queue depth transfers to any transaction counter.
- Confidence: High for the three geometry numbers (stated as minimums with units); Medium for the station-per-keys ratio (one chain, one sentence).
- Checkable as: on our 50×30 plate the declared load (470 guests present) implies **235-270 keys** at the operator's own 1.75-2.0 occupants/room (LP-14), so `desk_length_m = 1.2 × ceil(keys / 75…100)` → **3-4 stations = 3.6-4.8 m of counter (8-10 tiles)**; the *independent* practitioner guide gives "two stations for first 150 rooms, one more for each additional 100 rooms" → **3 stations** at 250 keys, i.e. the two ladders agree on the count (2 origins) while disagreeing on the station length (1.2 m vs **1.8 m** per station, so 3.6-5.4 m vs 5.4 m of counter — both printed, neither averaged). Staffed floor behind the counter **≥ 3 tiles (1.5 m)** deep; the guest-side tile band in front of the counter **≥ 8 tiles (3.7 m)** long and as long as the counter, held clear of seats and planters (`queue_band_tiles ≥ 8 × desk_tiles`) — the practitioner guide's counterpart is "**at least 20 feet (6.1 m) clear of circulation**" = 12 tiles, which is stricter; adopt the stricter of the two only if the plan's declared peak is a check-in rush, and say which was used. Counter height is not stated in either source — **no source found** here; the ADA accessible-segment figure is in `accessibility-usability-sequencing.md` AX/HS, not restated.
- Exceptions / failure mode: A desk sized below the queue is the visible failure — the queue spills into the lounge and the plan's seating becomes standing. A desk sized *above* it (8 stations for a 120-key house) reads as a rental counter. The standard's own mitigation for peak load is the self-serve kiosk, not more pods.

### LP-05 The lift lobby is a visible destination from the desk, and the self-serve kiosk sits between entry and core
- Rule: Do not hide the vertical core behind the desk or down a corridor: the passenger elevator foyer must be **in view** from check-in, and the transitional spaces reaching it (public corridors, stairs, elevator lobbies) get the lobby's finishes. Wayfinding devices (kiosks) go on the segment between the front door and the core.
- Evidence: Autograph 2A.3.A.1 (desk "within view of passenger elevator foyer and/or open stairs"), 2A.3.C ("clear line-of-sight … to passenger elevators"), 2A.1.B.3: "**At transitional areas (public corridors, staircases, elevator lobbies, etc.), extend finishes, furnishings and design treatment similar to the Lobby**" — the standard treats the elevator lobby as lobby-graded space, not a corridor. 2A.3.B.4: "Mobile Key Self-Serve Kiosk … locate to be easily recognizable by guests, **between the entry and the Elevator Lobby**." 2A.2.E.7 (parking structure): entrances must "deliver guests directly to the Lobby … where guests transfer to hotel passenger elevators **in view of Front Desk**". 2A.4.C.1 makes the lounge the connector: guest circulation "facilitate[s] interaction within this area, as well as easy connection to **key nodes such as open stairs, passenger elevator foyer, check-in, and related services**" — a four-node list in which the core is a peer of check-in. Distance: not stated — **no source found** for a metres-from-door-to-lifts figure anywhere in this corpus.
- Source: https://crhotelmanagementgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Lobby-Areas.pdf — T4
- Class: BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION
- Scope: hotels with more than one storey of keys; single-storey plates need only the *announce* behaviour (a screened, finished lobby-facing wall).
- Confidence: High for sightline and finish-extension (multiple clauses); no confidence in any walk-distance figure because none exists.
- Checkable as: unobstructed straight line from **≥ 1 desk tile to ≥ 1 tile of the lift-lobby tile band**; lift lobby tiles tagged `public` and receiving the same finish class as lobby (on this grid: not `corridor`); `kiosk_tile` on the door→core path, i.e. nearer the door than the core; and `tiles_between_door_and_core` reported (no threshold — unsourced).
- Exceptions / failure mode: The genuine alternative seen in the built corpus is the **sky lobby** — the arrival floor is not the guest floor — which breaks this rule by design and needs its own transfer-space treatment. See LP-20 for the precedent. Symptom on this grid: a core tucked behind the kitchen corridor so the guest must find it by sign; the harness currently cannot see that at all because it only measures path existence, not *announceability*.

### LP-06 The lounge, not the desk, is the centre of the lobby — and it is a set of vignettes
- Rule: Make the lobby lounge the spatial centre and size it per property; arrange its seating as **multiple varied groups** (quiet/private *and* open/visible, intimate groupings plus cocktail tables of varied height), not one hall of chairs.
- Evidence: Autograph 2A.4.A: "**As the center of the Lobby, this area is designed and sized to suit the uniqueness of each property.**" 2A.4.E.1: "Include **varied seating groupings to allow for a choice between quiet / private or open / visible** seating arrangements"; 2A.4.E.4: "Create residential style **seating vignettes that integrate intimate groupings with cocktail tables of varied height for semi-privacy** within the space"; 2A.7.A.1: "Comfortable and **intimate groupings** of lounge seating … ample quantity of end tables with table lamps … light weight for flexible arrangement and for guests to rearrange if allowed". The "many clusters" reading is reinforced by the service furniture detail: "Provide **multiple electrical outlets** … Typically place outlets at a minimum of **1 for every 3 seating areas**" and, in 2A.8.A.1, "Power Outlets: Provide for guest use in **close proximity to seating groups** … Furniture Outlets: provide power outlets distributed within **50% of seating**" — an outlet-per-*group* rule only makes sense if groups are discrete and countable. Two independent government schemes require the lounge to exist at all: India's PUBLIC AREA table lists "Lounge or seating area in the lobby" as mandatory for every star category, and Abu Dhabi requires a "Clearly designated reception area **with a nearby seating area**".
- Source: https://crhotelmanagementgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Lobby-Areas.pdf — T4 · https://nidhi.tourism.gov.in/uploads/gallery/1667476361.pdf — T1 · https://dct.gov.ae/DataFolder/Files/Hotel-Classification-System-Manual.pdf — T1
- Class: BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION (centre + clustering), CODE REQUIREMENT (the two classification mandates that seats exist)
- Scope: all classified hotels for existence; clustering is upper-upscale/luxury practice.
- Confidence: High that seats are mandatory and that they are clustered (three sources, two of them official); Low on cluster count and per-cluster area — the sizing sentence is explicitly delegated to "each property" and **no source found** gives m² per cluster.
- Checkable as: `seat_groups = connected seat clusters` (a cluster = seat tiles within 3.0 m / 6 tiles of each other, counted once); require **≥ 3** clusters and **≥ 2 sizes** (max cluster area / min cluster area ≥ 2) so the groups are "varied"; lounge tiles (seats + their tables + their 1 m approach) form the tile-weighted **centre of gravity of the lobby** — i.e. the lobby centroid falls inside the union of lounge bands, not inside the desk or the through-route; every cluster has **≥ 1 tile edge not on the door→core path** (proving it is not a queue).
- Exceptions / failure mode: The degenerate plan this rule kills is exactly our current failure mode: one long perimeter sofa run, which satisfies "has seats" and reads as an airport gate. The opposite excess — enough seating to fill the plate — turns the lobby into a restaurant and fails LP-08's revenue test.

### LP-07 The bar anchors the lounge, is visible from check-in, and is fed from a pantry — never from the guest path
- Rule: Put the lobby bar as the lounge's anchor (not its centre), always with sight from the desk, with a side bar/pantry so service reaches it without crossing the lobby, and let the restaurant share that service spine.
- Evidence: Autograph 2A.4.D.1: "Bar: **Strategically anchoring the Lounge, but not located at the physical center**"; 2A.4.C.2: "The Lobby Bar may be the central focus for the Lounge area and **always has visibility from check-in**"; 2A.4.C.3: "Provide **easy access to the commercial kitchen or secondary food prep area**"; 2A.4.D.1.b: "there is **always a side bar for material storage** for ease of transition between day and night"; 2A.4.D.1.c: "**Pantry: Provide food service at the Bar and Lounge. If the Restaurant kitchen is not positioned to support food service, provide a pantry**." Also 2A.6.B.1: public restrooms are located "to serve **primarily the Food & Beverage facilities and secondarily the public spaces**" — the standard's own locator logic puts F&B, not the lobby, as the anchor of the service layer.
- Source: https://crhotelmanagementgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Lobby-Areas.pdf — T4 · https://crhotelmanagementgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/02-Auto-Collect-Module-2-Public-Entry.pdf — T4 (same bar clauses at 2A.4.D, plus the lounge zone list that puts Bar, Retail and Restaurant *inside* the Lobby Lounge: "Entry Vestibule · Bar · Lounge · Retail · Restaurant · Outdoor Seating · Elevator Lobby / Foyer · Restrooms") · **the revenue-in-the-lobby rationale, stated outright by the practitioner guide:** "Lobby serves as the main circulation space directing guests to the front desk, elevators, food and beverages outlets, meeting and banquet facilities recreation complex, and other public areas … **Locating bars, restaurants, and retail kiosks with in lobby is one way to increase apparent size of space without adding to additional gross area**" — https://pdfcoffee.com/design-guide-for-hotels-pdf-free.html — T4/T5
- Class: BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION
- Scope: hotels with any bar programme; limited-service properties substitute a grab-and-go/vending node in the same position.
- Confidence: High (four clauses, consistent); the absence of a bar area figure is the gap, not the placement.
- Checkable as: bar tiles in the lounge band (`bar_tiles ∩ lounge_band ≥ 1`), bar **not** at the lounge centroid (distance from bar tile to lounge centroid **> 2.5 m / 5 tiles**); unobstructed sight line from ≥ 1 desk tile to ≥ 1 bar tile; every bar tile has a service approach through a `service` door that does not open onto the lobby tile set (LP-08 test); bar floor area recorded but **band unsourced — no source found** for m² per seat at a lobby bar in this corpus.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Dry urban sites (no licence) move the node to a café counter; keep the position, change the label. Symptom: bar buried at the rear next to the restaurant so the lounge has no night-time pull and the lobby goes dead after 21:00 — an operating failure the harness can only see if it measures "lounge-tiles-with-a-visible-revenue-node".

### LP-08 Service never opens into the arrival-reception zone, and its routes never cross it
- Rule: Keep back-of-house entries, luggage, housekeeping and waste on a separate approach that neither crosses the entry/reception zone nor is seen from it; where a BOH function must touch the lobby (luggage room, bell stand, janitor closet, control room), it touches through a screen, a foyer or a service corridor.
- Evidence: Autograph 2A.1.B.4: "**Avoid service circulation through lobby and its public spaces as much as possible.**" 2A.2.B.2: "**BOH Entries: Provide Entry Areas for Back-of-House (BOH) service, without that service crossing Public Entry and Reception Areas.**" 2A.2.B.3: "**Sight Lines: Carefully screen sight lines from entry areas to BOH areas** to avoid undesirable sound and light transmission." Specific applications: luggage room "Accessible **direct from exterior (Porte Cochere), but not from main entry**, and on the interior from the Lobby … **Conceal guest views from the Lobby area into this room** … Where possible, provide a **service corridor from the Luggage Room to the service elevator to avoid transporting luggage through the Lobby**"; cart staging "in an enclosed room or alcove, **screened from guest's view**", 1 m² per cart, 3 carts per 100 guestrooms, cart 60 × 120 cm, door 1.1 m; janitor closet "Entry door: **Arrange door in a foyer, not visible from public spaces so housekeeping does not need to cross public spaces**"; ATM "position **out of sight from the Lobby**"; safe-deposit viewing room entered "immediately adjacent to Check-in" with the box room behind it; monitors/equipment "out of sight from guest". Central control (fire command) room: "Typically, locate at the **main lobby entrance**, receiving dock or engineering area", typically 8 m², "provide a door with direct exterior access" — i.e. a code-driven room is allowed on the lobby *face* but given its own exterior door rather than a lobby route. Public restrooms: "baffled / screened entries", "Arrange fixtures **not visible from public areas when door to restroom is open**".
- Source: https://crhotelmanagementgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Lobby-Areas.pdf — T4
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT (as written by the operator: "Design Standard: a minimum requirement to be fulfilled without exception") for the entry-crossing clause; DESIGN PRINCIPLE for the sight-line clauses
- Scope: universal for hotels with any servicing; severity scales with key count.
- Confidence: High (this is the single most-repeated clause family in the document — six separate functions are given the same treatment).
- Checkable as: (1) path test — the shortest path from any `service`/`dock`/`kitchen` tile to any guest-room portal or lift portal passes **0** tiles of the `entry-reception band` (the entry band = tiles within 5 m / 10 tiles of the door run plus the desk's 3.7 m queue); (2) sight test — no `service`-labelled tile is visible from the door run or from any desk tile (occluded by wall, screen or foyer); (3) the restroom/bell/luggage doors that *must* open to the lobby are counted and each carries a screen or foyer tile (a 2-tile recess) in front of it. Report `service_crossings` and `boh_tiles_visible_from_entry`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Bell service and the luggage room are *deliberate* exceptions — they must be adjacent to the lobby because their whole function is at arrival; the standard resolves that by splitting their public and private faces (exterior door for carts, screened interior door). Symptom in our generated plans: a "back of house" room with its only door on the arrival axis, which the harness currently passes because it is reachable, correctly sized and fully enclosed.

### LP-09 Public-fixture travel distance is capped at 40 m from anywhere in the lobby
- Rule: No guest in the lobby, lounge, terrace or restaurant should be more than 40 m of travel from a public restroom, and the restroom group is placed for the F&B first, the lobby second.
- Evidence: Autograph 2A.6.B.1.e: "**Do not exceed 40 m (130 ft.) travel distance to a public restroom**", with 2A.6.B.1.c "Include at least one male and one female public toilet room **on each public area level**" and 1.d "Some floor plans may require two separate restrooms if distance between areas is too great" — i.e. the cap is what *generates* the second group. Location logic is 2A.6.B.1: "Locate to serve primarily the Food & Beverage facilities and secondarily the public spaces."
- Source: https://crhotelmanagementgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Lobby-Areas.pdf — T4
- Class: BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION (operator cap; not a code figure — code caps egress travel, not restroom travel)
- Scope: public floors generally; the same 40 m style cap is what a measurement harness needs.
- Confidence: High for the number as an operator standard; Medium for transferability (single chain, and the document is the CALA edition).
- Checkable as: from every `public` lobby tile, `walkable path distance to nearest public WC door tile ≤ 80 tiles (40 m)`; on the 50 × 30 m plate the worst case from one corner is 50 + 30 = 80 m Manhattan, so **one restroom group cannot serve the plate** — a second group or a core-adjacent group is forced. Emit `max_wc_travel_m`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: A courtyard or arcaded plan where the "public area" includes a long terrace will fail the naive distance test; measure along the walkable route, not through the garden.

### LP-10 There is a floor under the lobby’s total area, and it is set by classification, not by design
- Rule: The lobby's *total* area is checked against a published minimum before any parti is judged: the luxury band of a national scheme requires at least 500 m² of lobby.

The same manual's ordinary 5-star criteria state quality/behaviour requirements ("Clearly designated reception area with a nearby seating area", "reception staff available 24 hours", "Appropriate background music played in the lobby/reception area", "Luggage Room available in the lobby/entrance area", "The entrance and lobby have emergency lighting") but **no** m² figure for the star bands — the only hard lobby area in the document is the 500 m² Luxury line. ### LP-11 The drop-off is a three-lane, column-controlled, weather-protected apron, and its geometry is public
- Rule: The arrival frontage is not a door in a wall: it is a covered drop-off with two covered lanes plus an uncovered bypass, a protected standing/walking length measured along the curb, columns set back so car doors open, and a flush paved strip for carts — and the guest's luggage moves on a *separate* line to a *separate* door.

Provide direction toward group arrival area for remote check in.** 6. **Provide a protected entrance and drop off area at a secondary building entrance** such as an exterior entry to large function and meeting area, restaurants and spa. 7. **Provide exterior zones discreetly located away from entrances for public and staff smoking.**" 1.18.E.4: paving "**Level and flush with walkway for full length of protected drop-off area to facilitate luggage carts and allow for disabled guest accessibility. a. - Scope: properties with a vehicle arrival; a pure street-front urban hotel replaces the canopy with a recessed entrance and keeps the 3 m curb set-back.

### LP-12 The lift bank is central to the rooms, visible from the desk, and screened by its own foyer
- Rule: Place the vertical core where it serves the guest-room stack (central to the rooms), make it **visible from the front desk**, and give it a vestibule/foyer so the waiting queue is a defined room rather than spillover; never let car doors open straight onto a guest corridor.
- Evidence: Autograph **Module 12 Elevators & Escalators** (May 2014), 12.4.C "Location": "**Central to guestrooms served. 1. Visible from front desk at Lobby. 2. Performance, 12.4.B.1: "**average interval of 40 to 45 seconds** during periods of heavy 2-way traffic using a **minimum handling capacity of 12% of guest population**, with **1.75 occupants per guestroom** in 5 minutes at 100% occupancy" (2.0 occupants/room above 700 rooms). Shuttle cars, 12.6.D: "**Provide separate vestibules or foyers at shuttle elevator stops that open to Lobby. Where possible, provide passive observation by locating the passenger circulation and shuttle elevator in view of the Lobby Front Desk.**" Service and freight, 12.7.B: "minimum of one service elevator for low and mid-rise towers"; 12.8.B.2 freight platform "approximately **2.4 × 3.6 m (8 × 12 ft)**", 2.4 × 6 m when a vehicle must enter. Cab 2030 × 1650 mm, hoistway entrance 1070 × 2140 mm.

- Scope: multi-storey key stacks; single-storey plates keep only the foyer/waiting-room clause.

- Checkable as: on 50 × 30 m, a bank of `n = max(2, ceil(keys/100))` portals; **lift-lobby tile band ≥ 3 m (6 tiles) deep in front of the portal line** so the queue is a room; portal line **not** adjacent to any `guestroom_corridor` tile without a foyer between; sight test from ≥ 1 desk tile to ≥ 1 portal tile (LP-05's test) — and the module's "central to guestrooms served" becomes: `lift_lobby_centroid` within the bounding box of the guest-room portals it serves. Symptom on this grid: one bank sized for the room count with no foyer, so the 500-person peak standqueues into the lounge.

### LP-13 The daylit part of a lobby is a band of 1.5-2 × head height — which caps the lobby's depth, not its taste
- Rule: Put the guest-hanging programme in the daylight band and the queued/service programme outside it; the band's depth is a physical function of glazing head height (**1.5-2.0 × head height practical, 2.5 × the modelling limit**), so on a 30 m deep plate a single frontage cannot day-light the whole lobby — the parti must either stay shallow or buy a second light source (courtyard, light court, atrium, glazed internal wall).
- Evidence: **LBND/Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, "The Integrated Approach: Tips for Daylighting with Windows"** (2013 revision of the 1997 guide by Schumann, Lee, Rubinstein, Selkowitz; LBNL is a US DOE national lab — T2): "Keep the **depth of perimeter rooms within 1.5 to 2.0 times the window head height** for adequate illumination levels and balanced distribution at the back of the room"; "**The higher the window, the deeper the daylighting zone. The practical depth of a daylighted zone is typically 1.5 to 2 times the window head height … With standard window and ceiling heights (between 9 and 10 ft; 2.7 to 3 m), plan on adequate daylight within 20 feet (6.1 m) from the window**"; "**If a corridor is beyond this zone and separated with a partially glazed wall, it may be adequately lit with the spill light from the room**"; "Spaces with windows on two sides often have better daylighting distribution"; and, for the glazing-area equation: "The equation **assumes that room depth is no more than 2.5 times window head height**." The volume half of the pairing comes from the operator: Autograph 2A.1.B.2 requires the arrival to have "**varied height ceilings**" and "an impressive entry in the grand arrival with **higher-volume ceiling than the adjacent areas, minimum 6 m (20 ft.)**" — submitted lower heights need Marriott approval — and 2A.7.B.4 requires window treatments that "**allow natural light to penetrate through**". **The multiplication of the two figures (6 m head → 9-12 m band) is this file's arithmetic, not a sentence in either source.**
- Source: https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/sites/default/files/tips-for-daylighting-2013.pdf — T2 · https://crhotelmanagementgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Lobby-Areas.pdf — T4
- Class: STANDARD (the daylight-band ratio, LBNL) + BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION (the 6 m arrival volume, one operator)
- Scope: the ratio is generic to any sidelit room; the 6 m is an upper-upscale arrival requirement.
- Confidence: High for 1.5-2 × head height (a national-lab guide, stated as a design rule, and the 2.5 × limit stated separately for the daylight-factor equation); High that a single-storey-height glazed lobby cannot serve a 30 m depth (arithmetic on the above).
- Checkable as: define `head_h` per glazed bay; the harness has no heights, so carry it as a **project input per glazing type** — standard storey (3 m) → daylit band `≤ 4.5-6 m (9-12 tiles)`; arrival volume (6 m) → `≤ 9-12 m (18-24 tiles)`; double-height (9 m) → `≤ 13.5-18 m`. Then assert: **≥ 60% of `lounge` + `seat` tiles lie within the daylit band of some glazing line** (frontage, courtyard or clerestory), and **0% of `lift_lobby`, `back_of_house`, `storage` tiles are demanded to be** inside it. Emit `daylit_share_of_seating`, `unlit_public_area_m2`, and a flag `depth_exceeds_single_frontage` when the plate is > 2 × head_h deep with no second light source — which is exactly the case on our 30 m plate.
- Exceptions / failure mode: The rule is silently defeated by (a) deep reveals and light shelves (LBNL: a reflective light shelf "may extend this zone further"), (b) internal glazed partitions (a corridor beyond the zone can be lit by spill), and (c) deliberately dark, artificial-lit lobby bars designed for night — those are legitimate, but must be *declared* as an electric-space, not accidental. Symptom of ignoring it: 1500 m² glazed only on one 50 m side, seats distributed evenly across the plate, so two thirds of the "seating" is in a windowless cellar with no signal to the player.

### LP-14 Standing and seated density is a real number in this corpus, and 502 people is a design load that sizes the room
- Rule: Convert the declared head-count into floor demand before arranging furniture: the corpus's own standing/assembly figures are **1.4 m²/person for ballroom-type occupancy and 3.3 m²/person for meeting-room occupancy**, and a lobby needs the *queue* band, the *seat* band and the *stand* band all at once.
- Evidence: Autograph Module 12, 12.6.C.3 (elevator sizing for a ballroom fill): "**Estimate elevator occupancy at 15 sq. ft. per occupant in Ballroom and 35 sq. ft. in Meeting Rooms** (minimum of 2 elevators). **This criteria is not used for fire exit capacity; see Module <14>**" — i.e. 15 ft² ≈ **1.4 m²/person** (dense standing/event) and 35 ft² ≈ **3.3 m²/person** (seated/table meeting). Occupant-per-room factors in the same module: "1.75 occupants per guestroom … At property over 700 guestrooms use 2.0". The *guest-room* load our brief declares (470 guests present) is therefore consistent with roughly 235-270 keys at 1.75-2.0 occupants, which in turn drives LP-04's desk pods (3-4 stations) and LP-12's car count (≥ 3, "one per 100 guestrooms"). **For the non-guest half of our brief the corpus is almost silent, and the one number it gives is an uplift:** Module 12's street-entry shuttle rule is "Base quantity on same criteria used to determine number of guest passenger elevators **plus 20% additional for visitors**", and its car-park rule sizes on "**1.3 persons per parking space** … during 5 minutes of heavy 2-way traffic" — i.e. a real operator models walk-in/visitor traffic as a **+20% surcharge on the guest load rather than as its own count**; no source found for a daily walk-in figure, a walk-in peak factor, or a check-in-arrival distribution curve, and no source found for a hotel-lobby occupancy density as such (the 1.4/3.3 m² figures are ballroom and meeting-room indices borrowed for lift sizing, and the document itself disclaims them for fire capacity). Abu Dhabi's Luxury band adds a service density in the same direction: "High staff to guest ratio: **3 employees to each guestroom**" and "Butler to room ratio: **1 butler is available for each 6 occupied rooms**". The 1.4 m²/person figure and the 500 m² lobby minimum of LP-10 agree to within 4%: 500 m² / 1.4 m² ≈ 357 people — the operator's own two numbers, from two different documents, describe the same room. **No source in this corpus gives a lobby-specific circulation-to-standing split** — see LP-26.
- Source: https://crhotelmanagementgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/12-Auto-Collect-Module-12-Elevators.pdf — T4 · https://dct.gov.ae/DataFolder/Files/Hotel-Classification-System-Manual.pdf — T1 · the earlier "12-20% circulation band" is *excluded* from this file: it is office data and has no hotel source (see `## Consensus vs variance`).
- Class: HEURISTIC (the densities are elevator-sizing proxies, explicitly disowned for fire capacity), CODE-adjacent for the staff ratios
- Scope: event/mixed lobby floors in the US/operator practice band; not a life-safety figure and never to be used as one.
- Confidence: High that these two densities are what a real operator plans with; Low that either is the right density for a *lobby lounge* (unsourced — no lobby m²/person figure exists in the corpus).
- Checkable as: `demand_m2 = 1.4 × standing_headcount + (seat_footprint) × seated` — for the declared 502 present, an all-standing worst case is **703 m² = 2812 tiles**, i.e. 47% of the plate *before* walls, desks, bar or core; the plan must therefore state its split. - Exceptions / failure mode: The most common amateur plan on this plate is one that satisfies "1500 m² ≥ 500 m² lobby" and still cannot hold 502 people because 70% of the tiles are corridor with furniture pushed to the perimeter. Symptom: `walkable_share` high but `seated + standing capacity` far below the declared load.

### LP-15 What the guest sees in the first ten metres is a focal wall and a seatable group — never a queue, a wall of monitors, or a logo
- Rule: Compose the first view: the desk's rear wall is a designed **feature/focal element** that also acts as staff backdrop, the property plaque is off the focal wall, screens and equipment are concealed, and the foreground holds seats. ### LP-16 The lobby is planned as exactly two zones — Guest Services and Lobby Lounge — and nothing else is a "third lobby"
- Rule: Split the lobby volume into two named programmes and design them differently: **Guest Services** (arrival + orientation + transactions) and **Lobby Lounge** (the social centre). Everything else in the volume is a named room attached to one of the two, not a third undifferentiated area.
- Evidence: Autograph **Module 2 Public Spaces** (May 2014), 2A.1.C: "The Lobby is comprised of **two areas** that are comfortable and inviting while creating specific, well defined experiences … **Guest Services — Information resource / Lobby Lounge — Work and celebrate, public privacy for relaxing work and individual play**", followed by "Signature Service Moments: Each area contains 'Signature Service Moments', that are centers of experiences supporting the various touch-points. The success of each area and **the interrelation between areas** depends on effective arrangement of the moments within both areas." The Lounge's own zone list (2A.4.B.4) then enumerates what hangs off zone 2: "Entry Vestibule · Bar · Lounge · Retail · Restaurant · Outdoor Seating · **Elevator Lobby / Foyer** · Restrooms" — i.e. the vertical core is *inside the lounge's zone list*, and 2A.4.E calls the seating "the center of the Lounge … the hotel's **'living room'**". The separate *Lobby Areas* chapter states the same two-zone programme in the same words (2A.3/2A.4), so the pairing is stable across two documents — but see the concentration note: both are Marriott instruments.
- Source: https://crhotelmanagementgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/02-Auto-Collect-Module-2-Public-Entry.pdf — T4 · https://crhotelmanagementgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Lobby-Areas.pdf — T4
- Class: BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION
- Scope: full-service; a limited-service hotel is Guest Services only, and that is a legitimate answer, not a failure.
- Confidence: High within one operator (two modules), Medium as an industry consensus pending the built-plan pass.
- Checkable as: require exactly two `lobby` sub-labels (`guest_services`, `lobby_lounge`), each ≥ 40 m² (160 tiles), and require the door run to be in `guest_services`. - Scope: full-service hotels on a plate with one frontage; the mirror-image arrangement is equally correct.

- Checkable as: on the 50 × 30 m plate with the door run at x ≈ 34-46 m along the frontage (one side, not centred): require `door_run_centre` in the outer 40% of the frontage (i.e. offset ≥ 10 m / 20 tiles from the frontage midpoint), the desk band 8-20 m from the door, and the **lift portals behind/beyond the lounge tiles** (`lift_tiles` farther from the frontage than the lounge centroid); admin/office tiles directly behind desk tiles (share a wall run, ≤ 5 m); a `to_restaurant` or `to_function` door on the edge opposite the frontage. ### LP-18 Volume is a planning instrument: the lobby is required to be two storeys tall, and ceilings vary
- Rule: Treat ceiling height as part of the parti, not a section detail: the arrival volume is required to be **at least two storeys / 6 m**, it is deliberately **higher than its neighbours**, and the rest of the lobby uses **varied heights** to make the zones readable from inside.

### LP-25 The built evidence says the lobby IS the through-route — and that is measured as the building's biggest single failure point
- Rule: Accept through-traffic rather than trying to delete it, then buy **redundancy**: every lobby function should be reachable by more than one route, and no non-door tile in the lobby should be an articulation point. The lobby's dual role — circulation *and* destination — is the design problem, and the corpus's only measurement-based verdict is that the arrival/lobby sequence is the most integrated and most exposed node in every plan examined.
- Evidence: **What built plans show** (three 4-star hotels digitised into justified graphs and measured — IJRIAS 11(6):3263-3277, 2026, T4 journal, small-n comparative case study): "**Across all three hotels, the entrance/lobby sequence recorded the highest Integration and Choice values while also functioning as each building's single largest circulation risk point**"; on one ground floor "The **Lobby and Fountain Courtyard** emerged as tied dominant integrators and through-movement carriers, indicating that the entire entrance sequence **drop-off, reception, parking, kitchen/back-of-house, and dining terrace** is dependent on these two adjacent nodes, **with no alternative circulation route**. This represents a **structurally significant bottleneck risk at the building's primary arrival point**"; the study's recommendations are "**centralized reception placement, physical separation of guest and service corridors, and the use of integration and articulation-point values as design-stage evaluation metrics**". The practitioner guide states the same role as a definition ("Lobby serves as the main circulation space directing guests to the front desk, elevators, F&B outlets, meeting and banquet facilities, recreation complex", LP-24). ### LP-27 The threshold is a *reveal*: a screen half-blocks the first view, then releases it
- Rule: Real lobbies put a deliberate half-obstruction on the door axis — a screen, a wall, a change of level, a low ceiling — so the whole room is not readable from the street; the lobby then *discloses* in stages. The brand standards say "screen"; the built work says the screen is the design move.
- Evidence: **Built plan (T3, architect's own project documentation):** TOMO Design's SAVHE Hotel (Shanghai; the project's stated area is 1500 m², the same size as our target plate) opens with a Chinese screen wall reinterpreted as an entrance module — "在萨和SAVHE酒店的入口处，传统建筑中的影壁被巧妙地现代化重构，成为半虚的「门头影壁模块」" (at the entrance, the traditional *yingbi*/courtyard screen is modernised into a "half-open gateway screen module"), and daylight is then brought in over the top of it: "屋顶挑空处设计的“天光”与“照壁”，将树影映衬其中" (a skylight void and the screen wall throw tree-shadow into the space). Seating is partitioned without cutting light: "深色木屏风既划分了空间，又不影响光线的穿透" (dark wood screens divide the space without blocking light). **Code/standard echo:** India's star rules require public toilets reachable at lobby level and the Abu Dhabi manual requires signage "in the corridors and in the lobby area … if direction is not [obvious]" — i.e. the plan is expected to be *non-obvious* enough to need wayfinding. **Operator echo (already cited):** "Carefully **screen sight lines** from entry areas to BOH" (LP-08); restroom entries "**baffled / screened**"; "the guest must not see into the luggage room". LP-15 adds the positive term: the first view is supposed to land on a **feature element**, which only works if it is framed.
- Source: https://www.gooood.cn/avhe-hotel-by-tomo-design.htm — T3 · https://crhotelmanagementgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Lobby-Areas.pdf — T4 · https://nidhi.tourism.gov.in/uploads/gallery/1667476361.pdf — T1
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: hotels with any pretension to sequence; a mall-anchored hotel lobby visible in one glance from the atrium is a legitimate counter-case.
- Confidence: Medium (one built precedent read in detail + the operator's negative "screen the bad views" clauses; the positive "screen to create reveal" is the precedent's, not the standard's).
- Checkable as: from the door-run tiles, compute the **visible tile set** at 3 m, 6 m, 10 m and 20 m. Require `visible_tiles(10 m) < 0.6 × visible_tiles(20 m)` (the room must not be fully legible from the threshold) and **≥ 1 screen element** (free-standing blocked tile run, not a room wall, ≥ 2 m / 4 tiles long) within 8 m of the door, oriented so the door axis passes one side of it. Emit `reveal_ratio`, `screens_within_8m`, `full_room_visible_from_door: bool` (true = fail).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Screens that block the sightline from the desk to the lifts violate LP-05, and screens that block the *desk's* visibility from the door violate the servicescape finding ("Front desks need to be **immediately visible** to guests once they enter", Ariffin et al. 2013 via T2). The reconciliation the built work uses is **permeable** screens (light and sight pass, movement bends). Encode that: a screen tile may occlude straight movement but must not occlude more than 50% of the desk's sight arcs.

### LP-28 The lobby-plate is not always the arrival plate: the sky lobby and the desk-free check-in are both documented
- Rule: Two deviations are established practice, not errors: (a) the lobby sits **above** the street floor and the street level is a vestibule with shuttle lifts; (b) the reception desk is replaced or supplemented by **seated, resort-style or kiosk check-in**. Both must be declared, because each changes what the other rules mean.
- Evidence: (a) **Aman Tokyo** — the brand's own project page places the hotel "Occupying the **top floors of the Otemachi Tower**", with "the vast hotel lobby, ryokan-style rooms and Aman Spa are all among Tokyo's largest", finished in "natural materials – wood, stone and washi paper", and the site is "connected to five-line Otemachi subway station" (so arrival is from below, through a tower lobby, not from a street door). The operator standard plans for exactly this: Module 2A "When the **property street entry is not on the same floor as the Lobby** or Function area, **shuttle elevators and escalators are typically provided** to transport property and function guests from street level to the Lobby", and Module 12 §12.6 "Street Lobby to property Lobby … **Provide separate vestibules or foyers at shuttle elevator stops that open to Lobby**"; vertical circulation across such a split is "provided by a combination of **grand stairs, public circulation stairs, escalators and shuttle elevators**". (b) **Desk-free / seated check-in** — SAVHE states the inversion explicitly: "区别于都市酒店普遍的立式接待台，萨和设置了**度假酒店式的坐式接待台**" ("unlike the standing reception counters typical of city hotels, SAVHE uses a resort-style *seated* reception desk"). Marriott offers the same escape hatches as options: "**In room check in option**", "**Mobile Key Self-Serve Kiosk** … locate … **between the entry and the Elevator Lobby**" (LP-05). Post-pandemic practice is recorded in the T2 review: "Face-to-face check-ins and check-out process" → "**Contactless check-ins and check outs process**", "Guests queue at the reception" → "Connect with guests – social distancing (6 feet of separation)". Escalator placement in the inversion case has its own rule: "**Position escalators in a logical path of travel for guest and public, not as the area focal point**."
- Source: https://www.aman.com/hotels/aman-tokyo — T3 (brand's own project page) · https://crhotelmanagementgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/12-Auto-Collect-Module-12-Elevators.pdf — T4 · …/02-Auto-Collect-Module-2-Public-Entry.pdf — T4 · …/Lobby-Areas.pdf — T4 · https://www.gooood.cn/avhe-hotel-by-tomo-design.htm — T3 · https://ir.uitm.edu.my/id/eprint/65677/1/65677.pdf — T2
- Class: BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION (each deviation is codified in its own way)
- Scope: (a) tower/metro-linked and flagship urban hotels; (b) lifestyle, resort and long-stay brands.
- Confidence: Medium-high (each is stated by an authority as an option, not a norm; neither is the default).
- Checkable as: add a project flag `arrival_plate ∈ {street_level, above_street}`. For `above_street`, the checks on this floor become: `street_vestibule_tiles` ≥ queue demand at 1.4 m²/person for the peak arrival bank (the 500 walk-ins/day load spread over its peak hour), shuttle portals ≥ 2, and the *upper* lobby still satisfies LP-03/05. For `desk_free`, LP-04's geometry swaps to `kiosk_band`: ≥ 3 self-serve units at 1.2 m each placed on the door→core path, plus a seated host station within 10 m of them, and LP-15's focal-wall test moves to the kiosk band. Emit `checkin_mode`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: The dangerous combination is a sky lobby *without* a designed street vestibule: the arrival queue then stands on the public pavement, and the plan's declared 1500 m² never sees the peak. Symptom on the harness: the arrival floor passes every load test while the actual arrival capacity sits on a floor the model does not contain.

### LP-29 Recurring decisions NOT on the brief's list — these are the ones the list is missing
- Rule: Five relations recur across the sources as strongly as any placement rule above, and none of them is in the nine items the brief asked about. Treat them as first-class checks, because they are where "designed" and "assembled" actually separate.
- Evidence: five findings, none of them on the brief's list of nine:
  1. **Ceiling-height *variation* as the zoning instrument.** "utilizing **varied height ceilings for spatial interest**"; the arrival is "higher-volume ceiling **than the adjacent areas**, minimum 6 m"; "**anything less than a two story height would require approval**"; the ceiling design is to be coordinated "**with architectural elements and seating groups**" (Module 2 2A.1.D, Lobby Areas 2A.1.B, LP-18). Recurrence: 2 operator documents + the T2 review's "The furnishing and **high ceiling are associated with spaciousness, spatial movement, and cosiness** (Lin, 2004)". Our grid has no heights, so today this is the single largest unmeasured driver of "dead".
  2. **The day-to-night transformation, with storage.** "**Flexible design elements allow the space to transform in character from day to night**"; "**Storage: Provide ample storage to ease day to night transition**"; "there is **always a side bar for material storage** for ease of transition between day and night"; lighting on "one automated dimming system to support reading, work and social interaction and day to night transition"; "Do not include desktop computers in the [lounge]". Recurrence: 4+ clauses across 3 documents; **no area index for the storage is given** (no source found).
  3. **Acoustic and olfactory zoning of the lobby volume.** "**Music: Separately zone from the porte cochere and the Lobby**" (Module 2A.4.C.5), "An upgrade quality sound system with **volume controlled zones**", "Scent, botanicals, light and sound enhance the hotel's brand narrative", "Appropriate background music played in the lobby/reception area" (Abu Dhabi, T1), "**Acoustic Control: … Do not locate Laundry under, over or adjacent to guestrooms and public spaces**", "Configure walls enclosing wet service stations to conceal food service equipment **while permitting servers to maintain visual contact**", "Locate [wet stations] to **minimize noise at adjacent dining areas**". The lobby is zoned by *sound fields*, which is a plan property (which seats are in which acoustic zone) even though our grid cannot hear it.
  4. **The furniture-electrical plan as the test of a real seat group.** "Provide multiple electrical outlets with easy access in seating areas (**built into soft seating, bar dies, banquettes**, etc.) … Typically place outlets at a **minimum of 1 for every 3 seating areas**. **Outlets in light fixtures are not allowed**"; "Furniture Outlets: Provide power outlets distributed **within 50% of seating**"; "Power Outlets: Provide for guest use **in close proximity to seating groups**". Recurrence: 3 clauses, 2 documents. Read as a *design* statement it says: a seating group is only a group if it is wired — which is exactly the evidence a measurement harness can use to tell "designed vignette" from "chairs left in the room".
  5. **Finish-class as the public/private test.** "**At transitional areas (public corridors, staircases, elevator lobbies, etc.), extend finishes, furnishings and design treatment similar to the Lobby**"; elevator foyer "Extend finishes and millwork details from the Public Space Lobby … Ceiling: Smooth painted substrate; **no ceiling tiles**"; lobby "Ceilings: Gypsum board or plaster … **Do not use ceiling tiles**"; luggage room "Floors: **Vinyl composition tile** … Ceiling: **Exposed structure**"; laundry corridors/services likewise utilitarian; janitor closet walls "tile up to 1.2 m AFF". The corpus encodes publicness **materially**: the ceiling/floor finish grade *is* the zone label. Our grid already stores tags, so this is directly implementable — and it is the cheapest available proxy for "does this corridor feel like part of the lobby or like a back passage".

- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE (1-3), BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION (4-5)
- Scope: the whole file — these are cross-cutting, not additional placements.
- Confidence: High that each recurs inside the operator corpus (multiple clauses each); **Low on recurrence outside it, because all five come substantially from one origin** — see `## Weak or contested`.
- Checkable as: (1) `ceiling_class` per tile band as a project input, with `arrival_band_height > neighbours` — currently **unmeasurable, so declare it as a gap, do not fake it**; (2) `transform_storage_tiles ≥ 4 m² (16 tiles) within 15 m of every lounge cluster`; (3) `acoustic_zone_id` per seat cluster with `wet_station_noise_source ≥ 6 m` from any seat tile (proxy for the "minimize noise at adjacent dining areas" clause); (4) `wired_cluster_share = clusters with ≥ 1 outlet tile / all clusters ≥ 0.5`, from the "within 50% of seating" clause; (5) `finish_grade(tiles)` with the test `elevator_foyer.finish_grade == lobby.finish_grade` and `service_room.finish_grade < lobby.finish_grade`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: All five are cheap to satisfy *cosmetically* (an outlet in the middle of an aisle is not a seating group). The general guard is to score them **per cluster, never per floor** — every one of the five is a property of a group of tiles, which is also why our current floor-level metrics keep passing dead plans.

## Weak or contested

1. **Concentration.** Six of the nine origins are one operator (M), and both government manuals (AD, IN) are
   *facility-existence* rules rather than parti rules. The parti itself — LP-16/LP-17 — therefore rests on
   **one example diagram in one leaked standard**. It is an unusually good artefact (a client drawing the
   arrangement it wants), but it is one drawing, mirrored or not, and it is an "Example", not a requirement.
   Every confidence in this file is capped accordingly; nothing here should be presented to the user as
   "what the industry does" until a second brand's standard or a second published adjacency diagram lands.
2. **Provenance of the leaked standards.** These are marked "Marriott Confidential & Proprietary … may not be
   reproduced, disclosed, distributed or used without express permission". They are cited because they are
   the only dimensioned evidence in the corpus, but they are not public documents: do not quote them into a
   design brief or a UI string, and re-verify any figure before shipping it as a rule (also: they are
   *franchised* brand standards for the Caribbean & Latin America region, so metric/imperial pairs and
   climate clauses — snow-melt, radiant heating — carry that geography).
3. **Conflicts preserved, never averaged** (each printed at its rule, listed here so a future pass resolves
   them with new sources rather than with a compromise): lobby area **0.6-0.9 m²/key (DH)** vs **500 m²
   absolute (AD)** vs "**see the project Facilities Program**" (M) — LP-24; desk queue **3.7 m (M)** vs
   **6.1 m (DH)** — LP-04; station length **1.2 m (M)** vs **1.8 m (DH)** — LP-04; station count
   **1/75-100 keys (M)** vs **2 + 1 per 100 after 150 (DH)** — the two *agree* at 250 keys, which is the only
   convergence in this list; restaurant seats **40% of keys (M)** vs **75% (DH)**, lounge/bar seats **≥ 40
   absolute (M)** vs **0.5 × keys (DH)** — LP-19; desk position **off-axis, not central (M)** vs
   "**centralized reception placement**" as the recommended fix (SS) vs "corners **or** focal point" recorded
   as both being practice (Vallen & Vallen via SR) — LP-03/LP-25.
4. **Fabricated band, formally excluded.** The "12-20% circulation band" previously circulating in this
   project is **office data**; no hotel source in this survey states any circulation share for a lobby. It
   appears in no rule and LP-26 exists to keep it out.
5. **Undated instruments.** AD's text layer carries no edition year; M's chapters disagree internally on
   date (May 2014 footers, July 2024 cover). SR's own page did not surface a volume/year in the text read
   here. Treat every band as "as of ≤2024, geography as stated, currency unverified".
6. **Small-n measurement.** SS measures **three** hotels, digitised by the author from published plans
   ("secondary architectural floor plan data"), at 4-star, in Nigeria/Indonesia; its integration values
   (0.754-8.835) and articulation shares (7.1-50.0%) are *reported findings*, and this file deliberately
   converts none of them into a threshold. The metric *family* (betweenness, articulation) is adopted;
   the numbers are not.
7. **What the corpus simply does not answer.** No source found for: entrance→desk distance in metres;
   door→lift walking distance; number or area of lounge clusters; m² per lobby lounge seat; lobby bar area or
   bar-seat count beyond "40 seats minimum"; reception counter height; a walk-in/visitor peak factor (only
   the **+20% for visitors** uplift, M); a check-in arrival distribution; any hotel-lobby occupancy density
   (only borrowed ballroom/meeting indices); any lobby circulation share; the *m²* of the "seating/waiting in
   close proximity to entry"; the exact boundary of "lobby" in either classification scheme; and daylight
   depth measured *on hotel lobbies* (LB's ratios are generic room physics, not hotel observations).
8. **The `0.5 m` wall and the missing section.** As recorded in `floor-plans.md`, our 0.5 m wall inflates
   every perimeter/wall-derived number versus a 100-200 mm partition, and the grid has no heights — so LP-18
   (volume), LP-13's head-height coupling and LP-29 item 1 are **inputs the harness must be given**, not
   measurements it can take. Two of the strongest recurring decisions in this survey are therefore currently
   invisible to the harness; that is a finding about the tool, not about hotels.

1. **Where the desk stands: off-axis beside the path, nudged into a corner, or as the room's focal axis.** The
   operator says off-axis and off-centre; the space-syntax study recommends centralising reception; the
   2009 hospitality text records *both* corner and focal-point schemes as current. This is the single most
   parti-defining free choice on the plate. (LP-03, LP-25)
2. **Standing bank of pods vs seated/kiosk/host check-in.** City-hotel standing counters, resort-style seated
   desks (SAVHE states the swap as a deliberate difference from "urban hotels"), in-room check-in, self-serve
   kiosks placed between door and lifts, and post-pandemic contactless are all live options with different
   floor geometry: a kiosk band needs 3 × 1.2 m plus a 3.7 m queue, a seated host needs a table plus lounge
   chairs and no queue band at all. (LP-04, LP-28)
3. **Arrival plate at street or above it.** Ground lobby vs the sky-lobby pattern (Aman Tokyo's "top floors"
   of its tower; the operator's own shuttle-elevator and "street entry not on the same floor as the Lobby"
   clauses; escalators "not as the area focal point"). Changes which floor carries the arrival queue.
   (LP-28, LP-12)
4. **One lift hall opposite the door, or banks at the wings.** The default is central-and-visible; the same
   standard tells you to put remote banks at long wings and amenity floors to save guests from returning to
   the central lobby. A 50 m plate has both solutions. (LP-12)
5. **Axial vs diagonal parti.** The published adjacency diagram is diagonal (door at one end, lounge and
   "Views" at the other, lifts beyond the lounge, restaurant and ballroom doors on the rear edge); the
   grand-axial hotel is real and older. If axial, the axis must be ≥ 6 m wide so queue and through-flow
   coexist (LP-25's derived mitigation). (LP-17)
6. **How the depth problem is bought out: courtyard, skylight void, second frontage, or accepting an
   artificially-lit rear.** SAVHE answers with 天光 over a screen wall; the operator answers with
   two-storey arrival volume; LBNL answers with light shelves and glazed internal partitions. All three are
   defensible, and each implies a different plan. (LP-13, LP-27)
7. **Which seat index governs** (40% of keys for the restaurant + a 40-seat bar minimum, vs 75% of keys and
   50% of keys for the lounge) — a factor-of-two difference in programme that no source reconciles; and the
   same unreconciled choice between the per-key lobby index and the absolute 500 m². (LP-19, LP-24)
8. **Screen vs reveal.** Permeable screen (bends movement, keeps sight and light) vs full glass legibility vs
   the operator's purely negative "screen the BOH views". Only the first is a *compositional* move. (LP-27)
9. **Lounge as one great room vs a field of small rooms.** "Vignettes", "varied groupings", "2+ sections that
   close during slow periods", and "the seating is the centre of the lounge" all point to the field; the
   single-hall resort lobby remains built and praised. Our checker should accept either only if the *cluster*
   test (LP-06) is satisfied, not the area test. (LP-06, LP-19, LP-29)

### The one-line brief for the generator, from the above

Land the arrival at one end of the frontage under a canopy with a 2.5 m vestibule and a luggage door 15 m
away; put 3-4 pods 8-20 m from the door, beside the path, backed by walled admin; make the desk's rear wall
the focal element and the first sight line a *screened* one; lay the lounge as 3+ unequal clusters in the
daylit band, with the bar at the cluster field's edge and visible from the pods; put the lift hall beyond the
lounge as its own seated, fully-finished room; keep every service route off the arrival band and out of the
entry sight lines; hold a public WC within 40 m of every tile; and reserve the rear third of the plate for
rectangular BOH on a 2.4 m corridor. Then measure: `articulation points inside the lobby`,
`daylit seat share`, `cluster count and size spread`, `service crossings`, `queue band kept clear`,
`lift hall seats`, `first-view target`. A plan that satisfies the rules *and* scores those seven is no longer
rule-satisfying — it is composed.
