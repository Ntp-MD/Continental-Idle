# Research: operations & maintenance — the building as it is actually run, cleaned, emptied, supplied and repaired

Scope: this file covers how a building is *operated* — cleaning, waste, deliveries and loading, housekeeping,
plant and equipment replacement, utility access, staff circulation, security zoning and back-of-house sizing —
for hotels, hospitals, offices, retail and large public buildings. The host grid has no time, no staff rosters
and no cost model, so every operational requirement below is converted into a geometric proxy: route width in
tiles, turning clearance, catchment radius, queue length, crossing counts and access tiles. Findings already
owned by `professional-practice.md` (PP-01 flow crossings, PP-02 BOH share, PP-03 pantry catchment, PP-05 waste
room/chute, PP-06 staff day, PP-17 wet stack, PP-18 services band, PP-19 replacement path) are **extended with
numbers and tests here, never restated**.

## Rules

### OM-01 Give every servicing trolley a working bay, not just a corridor

- Rule: A route is not usable by a loaded trolley at the width of the trolley. Wherever a trolley must stop and work (guest-room door, bed space, retail shelf line, WC cabin), provision a clear **parking bay of 2.0 m² minimum adjacent to the served door**, plus a 3-tile (1.5 m) passing/reversal space in the route behind it, so the trolley never blocks the only path.
- Evidence: NHS England's adult in-patient design standard allocates exactly this: "Parking bay for food trolley … 2.0 m² … 1 per 24 beds", plus separate 2.0 m² bays for resuscitation equipment and for a mobile hoist (HBN 04-01, example schedules of accommodation, T1). The same document warns that mobile hoists "require sufficient space within the ward to store them" and that patient-handling tasks cause the greatest proportion of musculoskeletal injury in health services (§4.56–4.57, T1). Material-handling practice fixes the working width independently: pedestrian pallet-truck aisles are 2.1–2.4 m, walkie stackers 2.3–2.8 m, counterbalance trucks 3.5–4.2 m, "taking the Ast figure, adding 200 mm total clearance" (Toyota Material Handling aisle-width guide + AWE truck/racking guide, T3/T4).
- Source: NHS HBN 04-01 https://www.england.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2009/12/HBN_04-01_Final.pdf — T1; AWE "Warehouse Aisle Widths & Racking" https://awe.ie/blog/warehouse-racking-and-aisle-widths — T4; Toyota Material Handling "Aisle width guide" https://toyota-forklifts.eu/guides/aisle-width-guide/ — T3
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: hospitality, healthcare, retail, logistics; any served floor
- Confidence: High (NHS bay allocation is prescriptive); Medium (aisle figures are vendor guidance, single region)
- Grid translation: for each fixture tagged `trolley | hoist | food_trolley | cleaning_cart`, require ≥8 free tiles (2.0 m²) in the same room or in the corridor tile-band directly outside its door; require the serving route to be ≥5 tiles wide (2.5 m) at that bay, or ≥3 tiles (1.5 m) with a 3×3-tile reversal pocket every 20 tiles. Metric: `bays_missing`, `routes_below_3_tiles`, `pocket_spacing_max`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Corridor hotels with 1.2 m (2-tile) corridors can't give 5 tiles — then the room door must be recessed to form the bay, or the floor is served in two passes. Symptom of the failure (named in T4 hotel practice literature and consistent with the NHS bay allocation): trolley parked in the guest corridor blocking the only route, cart parked outside a room mid-shift.

### OM-02 Bound the cleaning catchment by area *and* by storey, and put a sink in it

- Rule: Custodial service is a room-coverage problem: plan **one janitor's closet per 1,400 m² of floor area and never fewer than one per storey**, minimum interior 1.5 m × 1.8 m (prefer 1.8 m × 2.1 m), door ≥915 mm, directly off a corridor, near a washroom, containing a floor-mounted mop sink with hot and cold water, a basket floor drain, ~3 m of shelving and chemical storage.
- Evidence: McGill University Building Design Standards, "Custodial Areas" (Mar 2023) §1.2: "Provide a minimum of one closet for every 1400 m2 (15,000 square feet) of floor area with a minimum of one janitorial room per floor. The location shall be accessible directly from a corridor, and whenever possible, near washrooms … minimum dimension of 1500 mm x 1800 mm … door with minimum width of 915 mm … one floor mount mop sink … floor drain with basket" — T1/T3 institutional operator standard. Independent code confirmation for institutional occupancy: Florida Building Code 2014 (Building) §450.3.9.1: housekeeping rooms/janitor's closets "not less than one … for each floor … A service sink shall be provided in at least one housekeeping room or janitor's closet on each floor" — T1. NHS allocates "Cleaners' room 8.0 m² … 1 per 24 beds" (HBN 04-01 schedules, T1) and defines it as "the base from which domestic service staff provide the immediate day-to-day cleaning service" (§5.38, T1).
- Source: McGill Facilities, *Custodial Areas* https://www.mcgill.ca/buildings/sites/buildings/files/custodial_areas.pdf — T1/T3; 2014 FBC-Building §450.3.9.1 via UpCodes https://up.codes/s/housekeeping-rooms-janitor-s-closets — T1; NHS HBN 04-01 — T1
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT
- Scope: universal for staffed buildings; ratios are for institutional/office-type stock
- Confidence: High (two independent T1/T3 instruments agree on per-floor minimum + sink); Medium (the 1,400 m² density is one operator's standard)
- Grid translation: `cleaning_catchment_tiles = floor_tiles / n_closets` must be ≤5,600 tiles (1,400 m²) and every storey containing `walkable` tiles must own ≥1 `cleaners_room` fixture cluster of ≥12 interior tiles (10.8 tiles = 1.5×1.8 m), with a 2-tile door run on a corridor, and ≥1 `sink` fixture inside. Emit `floors_without_closet`, `closet_tiles_below_min`, `closets_without_sink`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Very small floors (<150 m²) can share one closet across two storeys only if a service lift or stair is at the closet door — on the host grid a shared closet across a portal costs a lift trip per replenishment (see OM-16), so the arithmetic favours one small closet per floor. Symptom: cleaning equipment and chemicals stored in the plant room or under the stairs (McGill's standard exists because that is the observed default).

### OM-03 Count the rooms the cleaning *method* requires, not the room the cleaner requires

- Rule: A cleaning system is a set of spaces, not a cupboard. Before sizing housekeeping storage, state the method and allocate its full triad: **clean-supplies store, dirty-materials holding, and laundering/drying route** — plus a central store for the deep-clean machinery the floor closets cannot hold.
- Evidence: NHS HBN 04-01 §4.39–4.42 is explicit: "a microfibre system requires: a. space for storing the microfibre cleaning trolley and clean microfibre cloths and mops (i.e. a cleaners' room); b. space for holding dirty microfibre cloths (i.e. a disposal hold); c. laundry facilities for washing and drying used microfibre cloths", and "This manual assumes that steam cleaning equipment for periodic deep cleaning will be stored centrally and brought to the ward as required" (T1). It also requires a supply of disposables held separately in the dirty utility (§4.41). HBN 00-09 §3.89–3.90 repeats the triad for the cleaners' room: sink + slop-hopper/janitorial unit + wash-hand basin with "unrestricted access", plus "space … for mops, buckets, a vacuum cleaner, scrubbing/polishing machine (for hard floors) and other appropriate cleaning equipment" (T1).
- Source: NHS HBN 04-01 — T1; NHS HBN 00-09 *Infection control in the built environment* https://www.england.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/HBN_00-09_infection_control.pdf — T1
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: healthcare, hospitality, large public buildings; any building with contracted cleaning
- Confidence: High (two T1 documents with mechanism)
- Grid translation: require three distinct fixture clusters per served zone — `cleaners_room` (≥12 tiles), `disposal_hold` (≥1 tile per bed, see OM-06), and a route to a `laundry`/`utility_wash` cluster with a sink tile; assert the sink tile has ≥2 free tiles of approach (unrestricted access proxy). Validate `deep_clean_store` exists once per building, not per floor.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Where laundering is contracted out (common; HBN itself says "Project teams should decide locally whether laundry facilities are provided in-house or contracted out"), the third element leaves the building — then the disposal hold must grow, because dirty cloths wait on-site for collection. Symptom: wet mops stored in a sealed closet (odour/microbial growth — unmodellable, flag as such).

### OM-04 Size housekeeping labour to the wet cell: the bathroom is where the minutes go

- Rule: Plan the servicing geography around the bathroom, not the bedroom. In measured room cleaning, wet-area tasks (basin/mirror/shower screen, bathroom floor, bathtub, toilet/bidet, towel replacement, clearing bathroom items) consume roughly **41 % of the total task time**, versus ~34 % for all bed tasks; cleaning time rises with room area (suite 21.6 min vs single 15.5 min average across cleaning types). Therefore the binding constraints are the count of wet cells per attendant, the reach distance inside the wet cell, and the linen volume they generate.
- Evidence: Stopwatch time study of 74 room cleans in a 4-star hotel (74 rooms on 6 floors, Seville, Spain): total average cleaning time without extras **17.85 min** (stayover 15.24, stayover-with-changes 18.18, checkout 20.35); task shares — making the bed 22.97 %, bedside/tables 13.72 %, "washbasin, mirror and shower screen" 13.29 %, scrubbing bathroom floor 9.73 %, hoovering 8.55 %, gathering bed linen 7.06 %, replacing towels 5.55 %, cleaning bathtub 5.45 %, removing used bathroom items 3.84 %, soaping/rinsing toilet and bidet 3.60 %, stripping the bed 2.85 %, removing dirty bed linen 0.93 %. Room sizes: suite 37 m², superior 27 m², standard 18 m², single 12–15 m² (Aguilar-Escobar, Garrido-Vega, Majado-Márquez & Camuñez-Ruiz, *JIEM* 2021, 14(3):645–660 — T2). Corroborating workload figures from the same literature review: 20–30 min/room and "approx. 17 rooms in a 7–7.5-hour shift" (Onsøyen & Mykletun, cited there), 15.3 rooms/cleaner (Krause et al. 2005, cited there).
- Source: Journal of Industrial Engineering and Management https://www.jiem.org/index.php/jiem/article/download/3441/983 — T2
- Class: FACT
- Scope: hospitality primarily; healthcare and student/residential cleaning by analogy
- Confidence: High for the within-room ranking; Medium for cross-property generalisation (single 74-room property)
- Grid translation: use `wet_cells_per_attendant_post = ≤14` alongside PP-03's room count; require every `shower | bathtub | basin | wc` fixture to be within 2 tiles of a walkable tile that is also within 1 tile of the room door (reach proxy — no fixture in a room whose only access is diagonally across furniture tiles). Compute `cleanable_tiles = floor_tiles / wet_cell_tiles` and report the wet-cell share; a plan with a large bedroom and a cramped wet cell is predicted to be slow and poorly cleaned.
- Exceptions / failure mode: "Extras" (spills, guest belongings) add ~0.67 min/room average but are unbounded; open-plan suites with separate WC rooms add door passes rather than area. Symptom: quality collapse concentrated in bathrooms, inspectors' reports citing wet areas, and the classic 16→18-rooms-per-shift productivity push that T4 hotel management literature sells.

### OM-05 Cleanability is a geometry property: design out the surfaces that collect soil

- Rule: In clinical and spillage-prone areas the plan must present **no uncleanable geometry**: seamless continuous flooring, no carpet in clinical areas, no window sills or redundant horizontal surfaces, no fixed equipment above benches or in shallow voids, floor finishes able to take wet cleaning, and slip resistance where floors get wet. Every added change of level, recess or upstand is a recurring minute-cost and a soil trap.
- Evidence: HBN 00-09 T1: "Design-out unnecessary horizontal surfaces (for example, window sills) in order to discourage clutter"; flooring in clinical areas "should be seamless and smooth, slip-resistant, easily cleaned"; "Carpets should not be used in clinical areas. This includes all areas where frequent spillage is anticipated … Smell and staining have been responsible for the removal of carpets in many clinical areas" (§3.115); floors subject to frequent wet cleaning must be "unaffected by the agents likely to be used" (§3.113); floors "particularly subject to traffic when wet (bathrooms, kitchens) should have a slip-resistant surface, but be easily cleaned" (§3.114). The clutter/segregation logic appears in the same document's design-principles list ("provide sufficient storage … to discourage clutter"). Independent operator standard: McGill custodial walls must be "water-resistant and impact-resistant … painted; they cannot be left unfinished, nor simply primed", flooring "ceramic tile with cove base with minimum height of 150 mm" (§1.2.1.4–.5).
- Source: NHS HBN 00-09 — T1; McGill *Custodial Areas* — T1/T3
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: healthcare, hospitality wet areas, food service, education, any building with wet cleaning
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: emit `wet_area_adjacent_finish_conflict` = count of `bed | treatment | food_prep | storage` fixture clusters sharing a door run with a `shower | wc | sluice | mop_sink` cluster where the route between them is <2 tiles; require `floor_sink` or trapped drainage fixture in every room where OM-02's closet or OM-08's waste hold sits (proxy for "wash-down and drain to a system suitable for polluted effluent"); penalise every internal step tile in a cleaning route.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Acoustic and thermal requirements push toward carpet and thick soft finishes, which HBN explicitly overrides in clinical space; on this grid sound is unmodellable, so the agent has no counter-signal and defaults to carpet-thinking — flag "material decision unmodellable; cleanability rule wins in clinical wet areas". Symptom: replaced carpet, sealed sills, and a "non-slip" retrofit strip.

### OM-06 Waste provision is streams × containers × collection frequency — compute the room, don't guess it

- Rule: Size the waste store as **(number of segregated streams) × (container footprint) × (days between collections)**, then add a 1-tile clearance ring around every container and a minimum 2.0 m of headroom-equivalent in the enclosure. In healthcare, the rule is per-bed and per-stream: 0.25 m² of disposal-hold allowance per bed, 0.75 m²/bed of ward storage, and domestic waste never stored in the same area as clinical waste.
- Evidence: UK Approved Document H, Requirement H6 (consultation draft, Sept 2014) — government building regulation guidance — T1: domestic developments need separated-waste capacity of "0.25 m³ per dwelling", a dedicated store "1.2 m x 1.2 m" per dwelling where separate areas are provided, communal storage "determined in consultation with the waste collection authority", "a clear space of 150 mm between and around the containers", communal enclosures "a minimum of 2 m high", "permanently ventilated at the top and bottom", "paved impervious floor", and — critically — "Where storage rooms are provided, separate rooms should be provided for the storage of waste which cannot be recycled, and waste which can be recycled" (§1.14, §1.17). Capacity "needs to match the proposed frequency of collection" (HBN 00-09 §3.166, T1). NHS space numbers: "Disposal hold allowance … 0.25 sqm per bed", "Ward storage allowance … 0.75 sqm per bed", "Clean supply room allowance … 0.34 sqm per bed" (HBN 04-01 schedules, T1). Segregation classes are prescribed by HTM 07-01 and the UK regulator guidance: infectious waste in "orange bags, or orange-lidded rigid yellow containers", offensive hygiene waste in "yellow bags with a black stripe … 'tiger' bags", waste requiring high-temperature incineration (>1,000 °C) handled separately, and transfer in bulk containers such as "770 litre wheeled carts" (GOV.UK healthcare-waste guidance, T1). Operator standard: "Garbage rooms in buildings shall be large enough to store both standard waste containers, recycling containers, and composting containers … Standard waste containers are 90 gallons … Some buildings may require garbage tilt trucks" (McGill §1.2.2, T1/T3).
- Source: Approved Document H (Requirement H6) https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7e24d9e5274a2e87dafd5b/140910_HSR_Supporting_Doc5_H6_Waste.pdf — T1; GOV.UK *Healthcare waste: appropriate measures for permitted facilities* https://www.gov.uk/guidance/healthcare-waste-appropriate-measures-for-permitted-facilities/managing-healthcare-wastes — T1; PHS, "What is the Health Technical Memorandum 07-01" https://www.phs.co.uk/resources/what-is-the-health-technical-memorandum-07-01/ — T1/T3; NHS HBN 04-01 and HBN 00-09 — T1; McGill — T1/T3
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT
- Scope: universal (ratios type-specific)
- Confidence: High for "streams multiply the room"; High for the per-bed NHS figures; Medium for the 0.25 m³/dwelling (draft guidance, England only)
- Grid translation: `waste_store_tiles ≥ streams × 6 tiles × collection_days`, +1 tile ring on every side of each container cluster (so a 2×2 bin needs a 4×4 zone), and assert `domestic_waste_cluster` shares no wall tile with `clinical_waste_cluster` and no tile with a `recycling_cluster` (AD H §1.17 requires *separate rooms*). Report `streams_provided`, `container_zones_overlapping`, `hold_tiles_per_bed` (target ≥1 tile/bed ≈0.25 m²).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Daily-collection cities genuinely shrink this; the failure appears when a new stream (compost, soft plastics, e-waste, clinical) is added after handover and the room no longer exists — corridors and stair landings absorb it (the drift PP-05 predicts). Note the 150 mm clearance ring is *below* the grid resolution: on a 0.5 m grid it must be rounded up to one whole tile, which over-provisions slightly — accept and record.

### OM-07 Cap the carry distance to the waste store and keep it out of the building

- Rule: Waste storage must sit where the route is short, level and direct to the kerb: **householders/staff should not carry refuse more than 30 m** (horizontal) to the store, the store must be **within 25 m of the collection point**, the route should avoid steps and never exceed 3, and containers must not be taken *through* the building to reach the collection point "unless it is a porch or garage, or a car port or other open covered space".
- Evidence: Approved Document H, Requirement H6 guidance §1.8–1.10, T1 (verbatim figures above; §1.10 also: slopes "should not exceed 1:12", steps "should be avoided" entirely where larger containers are used, and "the storage area should be relocated" if that is not possible); §1.12 — external storage "away from windows and ventilators … preferably in shade or under shelter" and must "not interfere with pedestrian or vehicle access"; §1.11 — the collection point must be "reasonably accessible to the size of waste collection vehicles typically used".
- Source: Approved Document H (H6) — T1
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT
- Scope: universal; stated for residential and extended by analogy to wards/departments
- Confidence: High (regulatory guidance text) — class kept: the `level route assumed` flag in the Grid translation marks a representability limit of the host (a 1:12 slope cannot be drawn on this grid), not an uncited source
- Grid translation: compute `max_octile_steps(room_door → waste_store)` ≤60 tiles (30 m) and `steps(waste_store → collection_door)` ≤50 tiles (25 m); count `step_tiles` on the route (vertical transitions on the host grid = level changes/proxies) and reject >3; assert the waste route's tile set ∩ occupant-lobby tile set = ∅ (PP-01's crossing test applied to the waste leg). Slope 1:12 is unmodellable — flag "level route assumed".
- Exceptions / failure mode: Basement loading rooms are the standard way to meet the "out of the building" rule and they *fail* the steps rule unless a ramp or goods lift is on the same tile column; the AD H wording ("where this is not otherwise possible, the storage area should be relocated") makes relocation — not the ramp — the expected answer. Symptom: bin bags down the stairs, torn sacks, the stairwell as the waste stream.

### OM-08 Put the ward/department waste-and-linen hold at the entrance, serviceable from both sides

- Rule: Locally, waste and used linen hold must sit **at the entrance to the ward or department, with access from both the internal (ward) side and the building corridor side**, secure, out of public view, sized to accept a wheeled bin, with used linen in a *designated area separate from waste*. The site-wide collection store is a second, external, washable, animal-proof, secure area with vehicle access away from public routes.
- Evidence: HBN 00-09 §3.151–3.158, T1: ward/unit storage "should be secure and located away from public areas"; "storage areas should be sufficient in size to allow packaged waste to be segregated and so as to avoid waste of different classifications being stored together"; "Any new capital developments should have enough space for waste receptacles to be located close to the point of waste production to avoid unnecessary handling of waste"; "Adequate secure storage areas for waste are best located at entrances to wards or departments, preferably with access from both ward and hospital corridor to facilitate collection by authorised personnel only. Waste can then be stored in these areas — instead of taking up valuable space in dirty utility rooms"; "Storage for used linen should be in a clearly designated area separate from waste … [to] minimise any risks of used linen being accidentally taken for disposal, or of waste being taken to the laundry"; "The waste and used linen storage areas should be able to be cleaned easily and efficiently. The holding area should be large enough to hold a wheeled-bin"; "A designated secure area is also necessary to hold receptacles from the whole site for collection … with good access routes away from public areas. This area should also be washable and animal-proof." Also §3.163: external holding bays must be "washable, secure and animal-proof. Only rigid lockable receptacles should be stored in external areas."
- Source: NHS HBN 00-09 — T1
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: healthcare, hospitality (linen vs refuse), residential multi-unit, labs
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: require a `disposal_hold` cluster whose tile set touches (a) ≥1 tile inside the served zone, (b) ≥1 tile of the building-level corridor, and (c) ≥1 `door` tile on each side — i.e. two door runs, a pass-through; assert `used_linen` fixture cluster does not share tiles with `waste` cluster; assert hold interior ≥8 tiles (one wheeled bin + manoeuvre); assert cluster touches the `staff`-tagged corridor network but not the `public` network.
- Exceptions / failure mode: In a single-loaded corridor plan the pass-through hold is impossible; the compensating provision is a dedicated external door within 25 m (OM-07). Symptom reported across operator literature: linen bags and clinical waste bins parked in the same utility room, then cross-contamination of the laundry stream.

### OM-09 If a chute exists, it is a shaft, a fire compartment and a blockage job

- Rule: Treat any refuse/linen chute as four obligations at once: a **shaft column repeated floor to floor** whose base room is bigger than the chute (discharge, bin change, blockage clearance); a **fire-separated enclosure** (1-hour for the garbage room, 2-hour where the room terminates a chute, sprinkler protection at the discharge); a **diameter you can pass a modern sack and a hand through** (refuse ≥450 mm; linen 610/760/915 mm); and **controlled access** at every inlet and at the base.
- Evidence: Approved Document H §1.18, T1: chutes "should be at least 450mm diameter and should have a smooth non-absorbent surface and close fitting access doors at each storey which has a dwelling and be ventilated at the top and bottom"; §1.6 T1: dwellings above the 4th storey may share a chute-fed container "with separate storage for any waste which can be recycled" and "a satisfactory management arrangement for conveying refuse to the storage area should be assured". McGill §1.2.2.6–.7, T1/T3: garbage rooms "shall have a 1-hour fire resistance rating and shall be protected with sprinklers. Garbage rooms that end with garbage chute shall have a 2-hour fire resistance rating. The chute shall be protected with sprinklers as specified by NFPA." Linen chute product data: "standard diameters ranging from 24″, 30″, and 36″", doors as "UL-labeled, 1.5-hour fire-rated assemblies", rough openings "typically 4 inches larger than the chute diameter", sprinkler protection "in the discharge room", and NFPA-driven control of the service-room entry so it "can be used only by authorized personnel" (chutes.com, T4 vendor). Infection-control warning against the vertical route for rubble: "The removal of debris by chutes is liable to produce airborne fungal spores. The use and positioning of chutes should be carefully considered" (HBN 00-09 Appendix 3, T1).
- Source: Approved Document H — T1; McGill — T1/T3; chutes.com linen chute page https://chutes.com/products/internal-chutes/linen-chutes/ — T4; NHS HBN 00-09 — T1
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: mid/high-rise residential, hotels, hospitals, large offices
- Confidence: High (diameter/fire/access clauses) / Medium (the vendor's 1.5-hr door label vs McGill's 2-hr room rating — different jurisdictions and objects)
- Grid translation: a `chute` fixture must occupy a `blocked` tile in a shaft column repeated on every served storey, with a `door` tile on each served storey; the terminal room's interior ≥16 tiles (bin + clearance ring + turning); assert the shaft tile column shares no wall with `guest_room | bed | food_prep | staff_room` (PP-05's rule, now with the shaft made explicit); assert a `sprinkler`/`vent` proxy tile and a tag-gated door at the base. Construction/refurb exception: any `debris` route crossing a `patient` tile set is rejected.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Chutes are decommissioned in practice (PP-05 cites the UK evidence) — but decommissioning leaves the shaft: the grid must record it as blocked forever or the "free" tile gets built over and the next building can't reinstate it. Symptom: blockage crews drilling from the floor below; bin store overflowing at 18:00 on a collection-strike day.

### OM-10 Size the loading bay from arrivals per hour, not from the drawing's left-over corner

- Rule: Dock positions = **peak trucks per hour × turnaround hours per truck**, rounded up, with an *extra* position for refuse collection, and a truck waiting area sized for the surplus that the calculation proves will exist. Then give each position ≥3.7 m of centre-to-centre width (4.3 m if trailer doors swing or to reduce congestion) and an apron measured from the dock face to the nearest obstruction.
- Evidence: Nova Technology International, *Dock Planning Standards* (2013), T3 engineering/vendor standard: "Number of Trucks per Hour × Turnaround Time per Truck = Number of Dock Positions" — worked example 4.5 trucks/h × 0.75 h = 3.375 → 4 positions; "If all the trucks arrive within a peak period of 4 hours, 6.75, or 7, dock positions would be required"; "NOTE: Consider providing one dock position for trash collection"; "If the number of required dock positions cannot be provided, consider providing a truck waiting area"; "The recommended center distance between dock positions is 12 ft minimum" and "Each truck bay should be at least 12 ft wide … To reduce congestion, design 14 ft wide bays. If swinging trailer doors will be opening or closing at the dock, the center distance should be at least 14 ft". Apron for a 40 ft container rig: 120 ft at 12 ft centres, 116 ft at 13 ft, 113 ft at 14 ft, 110 ft at 16 ft, 108 ft at 18 ft; "if the dock can accommodate 48 ft trailers, increase the apron space in the table by 20%" and "If the traffic pattern causes the driver to be on the outside of a turn, add 50 ft". Corroboration: dock height "48 to 52 inches above grade", doors "9' wide x 10' high", "120 feet minimum" apron (Link Logistics industrial-real-estate guide, T3).
- Source: Nova Technology *Dock Planning Standards* https://www.novalocks.com/wp-content/uploads/Dock-Planning-Standards-Guide.pdf — T3/T4; Link Logistics, "What Is a Loading Dock?" https://www.linklogistics.com/news-insights/industrial-real-estate-101/what-is-a-loading-dock-a-guide-to-types-configurations-and-key-features/ — T3
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: retail, hospitality, healthcare, distribution, large offices; not single-tenant small retail
- Confidence: High (internally consistent worked arithmetic from one vendor standard); Medium (cross-corroboration is only on apron/height)
- Grid translation: 1 dock position = a 7-tile-wide bay (3.5 m) minimum, 9 tiles (4.5 m) preferred, on a 5-tile door run (2.5 m, covering a 2.4–2.7 m door) at 1.0 m above the approach level (proxy for the 48–52 in dock height — the grid has no levels; flag it); apron depth 41 tiles (saw-tooth 60°) to 73 tiles (straight-in 40 ft rig); `staging_tiles ≥ 8 tiles per dock position` inside. Emit `peak_trucks_per_hour`, `turnaround_tiles_min`, `docks_provided`, `waiting_spaces = (arrivals − docks) at peak`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: City hotels and hospitals often have no apron at all — the answer is a kerb/square bay with timed deliveries and a *declared* outsourcing of the yard (PP-02's "state the compensating decision"). A saw-tooth plan can recover half the apron (67 ft vs 120 ft). Symptom: a truck on the public street blocking the fire lane and the guest entrance, which is exactly the failure SBD/Toronto guidance tells you to move to a rear lane (OM-25).

### OM-11 Route the trucks so the driver turns to the inside, and keep staff off that route

- Rule: The site truck circuit should be **counterclockwise (driver on the inside of every turn)**, entered driving forward, with right-angle turns at minimum 7.9 m inside / 15.2 m outside radius, one-way aisles ≥4.0 m and two-way ≥7.9 m, and **separate roads for employee traffic** from the truck circuit.
- Evidence: Nova *Dock Planning Standards*, T3: "Design the traffic patterns around the building so that the truck driver is on the inside of each turn, for best control of the truck. Where traffic is on the right side of the road and the driver's seat is on the left side of the cab, truck movement around the building should be counterclockwise"; "An entrance driveway that accommodates the turning radius of the longest truck expected and allows trucks to be driven forward onto the site, rather than backed up"; "Right-angle turns onto the site that have a minimum inside radius of 26 ft and a minimum outside radius of 50 ft"; "One-way access roads that are 13 ft wide minimum"; "Two-way access roads that are 26 ft wide minimum"; "Separate roads for employee traffic"; "Truck waiting areas near the loading docks to accommodate all waiting trucks". Also: detached-trailer parking needs a concrete landing-gear pad "designed to support two point loads of 25,000 lb each, 6 ft apart", with the gear ~10 m from the back of a 40 ft container chassis; depressed-dock approaches "do not allow the grade to exceed 10%".
- Source: Nova Technology — T3/T4
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: any building with a yard; hospitals, hotels, retail DCs, large public buildings
- Confidence: High (single prescriptive standard, mechanically self-explanatory) / not cross-checked at another tier
- Grid translation: the grid has no vehicles: proxy a truck route as a **16-tile-wide (8 m) swept corridor** for two-way, 8 tiles (4 m) one-way, with turning corners as 16×16-tile clear quarters (8 m inside radius). Assert no `staff`-tagged or `public`-tagged pedestrian tile-set crosses the truck corridor except at a controlled door. Record "load-rated slab / landing-gear pad" as an out-of-model note; 10% grade is unmodellable.
- Exceptions / failure mode: A tight urban island forces the dock into the basement with a ramp — the 10% limit is what stops a normal ramp working, so a switchback or a lift is mandatory and the queue moves onto the street (see OM-10 exception). Symptom: reversing HGVs and pedestrians sharing one lane, which both Nova and SBD treat as the design defect.

### OM-12 Goods doors: the trailer is the datum

- Rule: Size the receiving door from the biggest object that must pass, with the trailer as the datum: **trailers are 2.6 m wide and up to 3.8 m high by legal limit, so dock doors should be 2.6–2.7 m wide (3.0 m for oversized loads) and 2.4–3.0 m high**; keep doors narrow (~2.4 m) where the space is temperature-controlled; internal service doors must not be narrower than the goods route they continue.
- Evidence: Nova, T3: "The maximum trailer size limits are 8'6" wide x 13'6" high (varies by state)"; "Common dock door dimensions are 8'0" in width with 8 ft, 9 ft or 10 ft heights. Consider extending the door 13 ft to 14 ft above the driveway and a minimum of 8'6" wide if full access to the truck's interior is required"; "9 ft wide doors should be used to service 8'6" wide trailers. Side-by-side palletizing is simplified, potential for product damage is reduced, and oversized loads can be serviced. The doors should be between 8'6" and 9'0" wide to allow for trucks to park off-center. A 10 ft wide door can be designed for oversized loads. Note that wider doors require more building space"; "Keep doors as small as possible — usually narrower than 8 ft — in temperature-controlled docks, where energy is lost through doors"; freight weight causes "a variance of up to 6 in" in trailer bed height. Cross-check: doors "9' wide x 10' high" as the common industrial default (Link Logistics, T3).
- Source: Nova Technology — T3/T4; Link Logistics — T3
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: hospitality, retail, healthcare, industrial, any building receiving palletised goods
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: a goods door = a `door` run of **5 tiles (2.5 m)** minimum on any tile path from the dock to internal storage; a 4-tile (2.0 m) run fails for palletised goods but passes for bin/trolley runs (record both classes). Assert the *narrowest* door run on the goods path, not the widest — `min_door_run_on_goods_route ≥ 5 tiles`. The 150 mm bed-height variance is unmodellable: flag "leveller required".
- Exceptions / failure mode: Existing buildings and fire doors cap the run at 3 tiles; then receiving must be broken down at the door (double handling) — a permanent labour cost that should be written into the decision log. Symptom: a 900 mm staff door as the only route from the loading room to the kitchen store.

### OM-13 Prefer recessed storage on the route over rooms on the plan

- Rule: Dedicated store rooms are expensive in circulation. Where goods are exchanged on a trolley or replenished often, use **shallow cupboards or doored alcoves opening directly from circulation, recessed so an open door does not obstruct the route**, and keep a single deep central store for bulk. Do not let the store-room count grow faster than the served rooms.
- Evidence: NHS HBN 04-01 §5.31, T1: "Store rooms are a costly means of providing storage, as they require internal circulation space. Storage in relatively shallow cupboards or doored alcoves opening directly from circulation areas may be more convenient and cheaper. The latter is particularly useful for goods for which stocks are maintained by an exchange trolley service. Cupboards in corridors may need to be recessed so that the doors, when open, do [not obstruct passage]"; §5.34–5.35: the large equipment store needs "Open shelving, hanging rails and hooks as well as free-standing space for heavy equipment such as hoists and weighing machines … Sockets may be useful for equipment that needs charging" and "Design teams may decide that more than one large equipment store is required. A number of local stores adjacent to single-bed rooms or multi-bed rooms might be more efficient"; §4.64: "just-in-time" supply means "a large centralised store on each site … for regular distribution on a 'top-up' basis" and "Local policy will influence how much storage space is needed within acute wards". Retail: the floor office on the guest floor is exactly this device (OM-14).
- Source: NHS HBN 04-01 — T1
- Class: BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION
- Scope: healthcare, hospitality, retail, offices
- Confidence: High (explicit operator standard with mechanism)
- Grid translation: score each storage cluster as `recessed_alcove` (projecting 1–2 tiles into the room edge, door run on circulation, no extra corridor tiles consumed) vs `room` (enclosed, pays wall tax + access door + its share of circulation). Validate that the alcove door swing does not reduce the route below OM-01's width; report `storage_tiles_per_served_room` and `circulation_tiles_added_by_storage`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Security, odour, moisture and infection-control reasons pull storage back into real rooms (linen must be a *closed* store — HBN 04-01 §5.30: "clean linen should be kept in a closed store rather than on open trolleys"). Symptom: an alcove plan that cannot hold a closed linen store, or recesses cut into a structural/fire wall.

### OM-14 Put the housekeeping office on the floor it serves, and cap the walk from the vertical portal

- Rule: Every guest floor needs a **floor-level housekeeping office storing materials and cleaning products on that floor**, feeding the trolleys that never leave the floor, plus an on-floor soiled-linen drop (chute or dedicated service lift); and no served room door may be more than **~46 m of walking from the lift lobby**.
- Evidence: The measured 4-star hotel study states the setup explicitly: times were recorded including "the time required to load the cleaning trolley with the materials, maintenance of the 'office' (room located on the same floor in which the materials and cleaning products are stored), and to throw the dirty linen into the laundry chute", with 74 rooms distributed over 6 floors (~12 rooms/floor) and "a fixed housekeeper assigned to each floor" (JIEM T2). Hospitality lift consultancy states the walk cap and the keyed service split: "Walking distances from the elevator lobby to any room should not exceed 150'" and "Service elevators should open directly to back-of-house areas with keyed access to service housekeeping and laundry services" (BASE4, T3). NHS states the same principle for beds: rooms should be serviced by trolley "so that staff do not need to walk far from their bed cluster" (HBN 04-01 §4.10, T1).
- Source: JIEM hotel time study — T2; BASE4, "Select the right elevator for your next hotel" https://www.base-4.com/select-the-right-elevator-2/ — T3/T4; NHS HBN 04-01 — T1
- Class: BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION
- Scope: hospitality; healthcare via bed clusters; residential-serve
- Confidence: Medium-High (three independent types, one numeric cap from a consultancy)
- Grid translation: assert ≥1 `hk_office` cluster (≥6 tiles) on each storey containing ≥6 `guest_room` fixtures, and ≤14 rooms per office (PP-03) with `max octile steps(lift_lobby → room door) ≤ 91 tiles (45.7 m)`; `soiled_linen` route from the office to the vertical portal must not enter the lobby tile-set (PP-01).
- Exceptions / failure mode: A single-service-lift property with an on-floor pantry satisfies this rule and still fails operationally (PP-04's coupling); conversely, a floor with no chute *and* no service lift makes the office a dead end — the linen must travel down the guest route. Symptom: the housekeeping vacuum/linen queue at the service lift at 10:00.

### OM-15 A service lift is a goods portal: it must swallow the largest routine object, with the door held

- Rule: Specify the service vertical portal by the object list, not by passenger count: the car must take **a stretcher/bed 610 × 2,134 mm laid out, plus a companion**, at ≥1,600 kg (3,500 lb) for stretcher duty, with a **keyed/reserved mode, an extended-door-open button, call-and-send control, and a car floor able to take point loads from wheeled trolleys and pallets**; it must open directly onto the BOH network, never into the occupant lobby.
- Evidence: Mitsubishi's hospital bed-lift literature shows the car laid out around a "Stretcher (620 x 1880)", "Convalescent bed (720 x 2040)" and "Anesthesia dolly (550 x 550)" with a 2,500 mm car depth and 750/1,000 mm door openings, and specifies the operational features: "Reserved Operation for Emergencies … the car will not respond to other calls and exclusively transport hospital beds, medical equipment, etc.", "Extended Door-open Button … doors will remain open longer to allow safe loading and unloading … including patients in beds", with 1-car selective collective standard and 2-/3-car group control options (T3/T4 manufacturer). BASE4 (T3): stretcher lifts "must accommodate a 24" by 84" ambulance stretcher in the horizontal open position", minimum capacity 3,500 lb, typical guest cab 6'-8" × 5'-5" at 3,000–4,000 lb, service lifts needing "a slightly wider than normal elevator shaft" and "keyed access". Oxford University Building Services Design Guide, C1.10 (T1/T3 operator standard): "Transportation of heavy goods, including dangerous & hazardous materials, within any lift must be a managed process. The lift's operation and finishes must be designed to meet its intended use, particularly where point loading is required (using wheeled trollies or pallets), together with the provision of call & send controls. An isolation key switch must also be provided to prevent lift use…" and G1.1: "Where service lifts are part of the plant replacement strategy, they must be designed as 'goods lifts' … and must be capable of fitting and carrying the largest and heaviest piece of regularly maintained equipment in the plant room." Vandal resistance is a code-referenced duty: BS EN 81-71:2022 "refers to the vandal resistance qualities of passenger lifts and passenger goods lifts. … Each lift car fixture, part and supply must remain functional after typical acts of vandalism" (Secured by Design Commercial Guide 2023 §52, T1).
- Source: Mitsubishi *Hospital Bed Elevators* https://www.melsa.com.sa/front_assets/download/elevators-cat/Nexies-MR/hospital.pdf — T3/T4; BASE4 — T3/T4; Oxford University Estates *Building Services Design Guide* v1.1 https://assets-oxweb.admin.ox.ac.uk/2026-04/Building-Services-Design-Guide.pdf — T1/T3; Secured by Design Commercial Guide 2023 §52 https://www.eppingforestdc.gov.uk/app/uploads/2025/07/SBD-COMMERCIAL_GUIDE_2023_v4.pdf — T1
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: any stacked, staff-served building; hospitals and hotels acutely
- Confidence: High (four independent instruments) / Medium on any specific car size (manufacturer options, not regulation)
- Grid translation: `portal_service` approach queue ≥3 tiles; car footprint proxy ≥4×5 tiles (2.0 × 2.5 m) to fit a 2.13 m bed with a companion; door run ≥3 tiles (1.5 m) for a bed portal, ≥2 tiles for a goods/trolley portal; tag the portal `staff|service` and assert `portal_service` never opens into the `public` tile-set (PP-04). Model the reserved mode as a portal that can be closed to other agents (an `isolation` flag); model the hold-open as a longer cooldown.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Where only one lift exists, the isolation key is the design — out-of-hours and delivery windows are time-shared (PP-04). Symptom: a hospital trolley that will not turn in the car, so staff reverse it into the lobby; a hotel service lift used by guests because nothing was keyed.

### OM-16 Do not size vertical or queue capacity on the average day — queues are nonlinear

- Rule: Capacity planning for portals, docks and pantries must be done against the **peak window**, because wait time is a step function: in a worked lift simulation, a system that met 14 % of the population in five minutes produced a 20.6 s average wait; the same system at 15 % produced 38.9 s; at 16 % it produced 85.8 s, while the departure interval barely moved (33.3 → 34.3 s).
- Evidence: R. D. Peters, *The Application of Simulation to Traffic Design and Dispatcher Testing*, 3rd Symposium on Lift and Escalator Technologies (Peters Research Ltd.), T3: "A lift group saturates when the demand exceeds the handling capacity. As the lifts cannot cope with the traffic, the longer the simulation runs, the longer the passenger waiting times become. Increasing queue lengths develop as the simulation progresses", with the 14/15/16 % → 20.6/38.9/85.8 s table and the warning that round-trip-time design "will be near or at the saturation point". Same logic in the dock standard (OM-10): daily mean 4 positions, 4-hour peak 7 positions.
- Source: Peters Research symposium paper https://peters-research.com/index.php/papers/the-application-of-simulation-to-traffic-design-and-dispatcher-testing/ — T3
- Class: FACT
- Scope: universal (any queue: lifts, docks, security, linen, waste, food servery)
- Confidence: High for the shape of the curve; Medium for the absolute percentages (a worked example, not a survey)
- Grid translation: for each portal compute `demand_tiles_per_min` over the worst 30-minute window and `service_rate` from RTT; report `utilisation = demand/capacity` and refuse `utilisation > 0.9`. Convert waiting people to tiles at 1–2 tiles/person standing (PP grid dictionary) and require the queue to fit in the approach band without reducing that corridor below OM-01's width. Flag "time not modelled — demand window is an assumption; state it".
- Exceptions / failure mode: Buildings with a strong time-of-day pulse (theatre, stadium, hospital visiting hour) need portal capacity set by the pulse, not by headcount. Symptom: the 4th floor walking to the stairs because the lift queue is longer than the stair climb — an overflow that then appears as unexplained staff traffic in guest areas (PP-01).

### OM-17 Plant rooms are entered like rooms, at ground level, through double doors sized to the biggest component

- Rule: A plant room must have: a ground-level location (or a ramped lower-ground location reached through double doors from a well), **no ladder access anywhere**, doors "at least wide enough to allow removal of the largest replaceable components", ≥900 mm clear around equipment, ≥2 m headroom on access routes, ramps at floor-level changes, mezzanines accessible above and below, plant raised on plinths, floors tamped/bunded and laid to falls with drains where wet services sit above occupied space, and lifting beams positioned over the heavy items.
- Evidence: Oxford University *Building Services Design Guide* v1.1, Appendix G (T1/T3 operator standard): G2.1 "Main plant rooms must be at ground level if possible and must be separate from any utilities intake room"; G2.2 "All plant rooms must have safe, easy, secure access. Where there are floor-level changes at plant-room entrances, ramps must be provided … Ladders are not acceptable as a means of gaining access to any plant room or rooftop plant area. Access to roof plant areas must be by a staircase with a clear width of at least 800mm"; G2.3 "Doors must double and at least wide enough to allow removal of the largest replaceable components, as defined in the Access & Maintenance Strategy"; G2.4 "Along access routes, there must be at least 2000mm of headroom under plant, pipes, conduit, trays, ducting etc. Access space around plant must be at least 900mm, or more if recommended by the equipment manufacturer … Appropriately positioned lifting beams must be provided to enable large items of plant such as pump motors to be safely replaced … Tripping hazards must be avoided – particularly low-level pipework discharging over floor drains located on access routes"; G2.5 requires tanking/bunding, floors "laid to fall towards drains", "a minimum of 100mm upstands" at penetrations, a flood test before handover, and "All main plant must be raised above the plant room floor on a concrete plinth"; G2.6 requires ventilation, floor drainage, uniform + emergency lighting, "an adequate number of power sockets, a WiFi access point", fire detection and frost protection. Independent engineering guidance repeats the same clearance numbers at 42 in / 6 ft and 1,000–1,500 mm (PP-19, T3/T4).
- Source: Oxford BSDG — T1/T3
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT
- Scope: universal for buildings with mechanical plant (the instrument is an estate's contractual standard, not statute)
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: `plant` cluster must touch a `door` tile on the perimeter or a ground-level tile column reachable from the site edge without crossing an occupant tile-set; door run ≥3 tiles (1.5 m) where the strategy lists a component >1.2 m; a **2-tile (1.0 m) clear ring** around every equipment fixture (900 mm rounded up) with a 5-tile (2.5 m) aisle to the door; headroom 2 m is unmodellable — flag. Report `plant_access_tiles_blocked_by_other_trades`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Rooftop plant is unavoidable on dense urban plates; the standard's answer is not a ladder but a compliant stair ≥800 mm clear plus a level non-slip pathway wide enough for removal, with lighting and edge protection (G1.6). Symptom: the chiller that gets installed by a tower crane and replaced by a wall.

### OM-18 Draw the replacement path before the layout: one document, three numbers per object

- Rule: Before finalising any plan, produce an access-and-maintenance record for every item that will be replaced in the building's life: **route from the outside world (with the narrowest door and the tightest corner in tiles), the object's size, and the object's weight**, plus the lifting/movement means. Where the route uses a service lift, that lift is a goods lift by definition.
- Evidence: Oxford BSDG G1.1, T1/T3: the designer "must produce an Access & Maintenance Strategy … This document must include access routes from the plant locations to external drop-off points, sizes and weights of replacement components and an equipment movement plan, and plans for plant replacement and maintenance/replacement of key components"; and "The designer must ensure the agreed Access & Maintenance Strategy is carried forward to the contract". For a heavy installed medical device, the numbers to enter in that record are large and specific: scan rooms "typically 30–40 sq m", floor loading "a minimum of 500 lbs/sq ft in the magnet area", "magnet weight (8,800 to 15,400+ lbs depending on field strength)" and a quench "pipe is typically 8 to 12 inches in diameter" (MRI shielding contractor guidance, T3/T4) — i.e. 4,000–7,000 kg on a route that must be planned, not a doorway problem only.
- Source: Oxford BSDG — T1/T3; Quality Shielding, *MRI Room Construction Guide* https://www.qualityshielding.com/knowledge-base/mri-room-construction-guide — T3/T4
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal; heaviest consequences in healthcare, labs, industrial kitchens, data rooms
- Confidence: High (the record is mandated in operator standards); Medium (MRI figures are contractor guidance, and 500 psf ≈ 24 kPa is a stated minimum not a measured norm)
- Grid translation: emit a table `object | footprint_tiles | weight_class | min_door_run | min_route_width | turns | lifting_provision`; validate each route as an A* run constrained to `min_door_run` and `min_route_width`, i.e. a *clearance-aware* pathfinding pass, and reject plans where the route exists only if the object is disassembled. Weight/load-bearing is out-of-model — flag "structural check required".
- Exceptions / failure mode: Items designed as permanent (cast-in pipework, embedded lifts) legitimately have no route; declare them and their design life. Symptom (PP-19 already prices this): "future replacement will cost thousands in structural modifications".

### OM-19 Riser cupboards belong on the public/service corridor, never inside a room someone must be invited into

- Rule: Every vertical services route must be reachable from general circulation with **a clear working area of at least 750 × 750 mm per item needing operation or maintenance**, solid floors, level thresholds, keyed control, mechanical and electrical risers kept apart, valve charts inside the cupboard, and the designer — not the installer — responsible for the internal layout. Extend PP-18's service band with this: the band must be *reachable*, and reach must not be granted by walking through a private room.
- Evidence: Oxford BSDG G1.4, T1/T3: "Risers must be accessible from corridor or circulation areas and must not be located in areas with restricted access, such as laboratories or private offices. Mechanical and electrical risers must be separate from each other. Riser cupboards must provide for a minimum clear working area of 750mm x 750mm for each item of equipment that requires access for operation and maintenance. All riser cupboards must have solid floors, a level threshold and doors secured with [a keyed suit] … Where branch valves are located in a mechanical riser, or in the ceiling next to it, a valve chart must be fixed in the riser with numbers corresponding to tags on the valves, identifying the sub-systems or areas of the building served … The layout of the equipment in all riser cupboards is the responsibility of the designer". Keyed segregation is prescribed by suite (electrical / mechanical / lift motor rooms, with a grand master) — the operator's answer to "which key opens what".
- Source: Oxford BSDG — T1/T3
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT
- Scope: universal for multi-storey served buildings (issued as an estate standard, not statute)
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: every `shaft` column must have a `riser_cupboard` on each storey whose tiles touch the `walkable` corridor set with a 2×2-tile (1.0 × 1.0 m, rounding up 750 mm) clear interior *in front of* the services, and whose access path for a maintenance agent tagged `estates` does not traverse any room tagged `private|lab|tenant`. Report `shafts_not_corridor_served`, `riser_working_area_tiles`, `keysuit_mismatch`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: On small footprints, the riser and the corridor compete for the same tiles — that is a genuine design conflict to log (the honest resolution is a furred service zone, which costs room area, not a private-room access). Symptom: the maintenance operative escorted through an occupied office, or the 02:00 alarm investigated from a tenant's bedroom.

### OM-20 Metering and isolation must be readable and reachable without entering, dismantling or waiting

- Rule: Every meter is **readable from standing on the floor, without mirrors or access equipment**; every piped system is zoned so a failure drains the smallest section ("as a minimum, each floor must be zoned"); every switchboard keeps 1,500 mm in front (1,000 mm rear access) with no unrelated services in the room; and meters and isolation points sit in a secure location that lets a reader or an operative work from outside the occupied building.
- Evidence: Oxford BSDG, E1 (T1/T3): "All meters need to be readable from the floor without the use of mirrors or access equipment. If this is impossible, a permanent access platform must be installed" and "All meters must be capable of resolving half-hourly consumption of the system they are monitoring"; E2.3/A1.4: "All piped services must have adequate numbers of isolation valves fitted for future maintenance requirements to minimise drain down. As a minimum, each floor must be zoned"; switchgear: "All switchboards must have a minimum of 1500mm front and rear clearance … No pipework of any kind, or any other unrelated equipment (such as emergency lighting inverters), may be installed within the switch room … Access to the switch room must be either direct from outside the building, or from the adjacent circulation space"; current transformers "must be installed on outgoing circuits such that they can be replaced without disrupting other circuits". Security overlay (SBD Commercial Guide 2023 §96.3, T1): "Where individual utility meters are still to be used, they should be located in a secure building such as a plant room and allow for meter reading without having to enter the main building … Smart meters that automatically send readings along a data line and read remotely are the much preferred option." Redundancy: "Two separate cold water storage tanks must be provided, so that supplies can be maintained while one tank is taken out of service for inspection or cleaning … It must be possible to clean the tank from outside to avoid confined space entries" (Oxford A3.6, T1/T3).
- Source: Oxford BSDG — T1/T3; Secured by Design Commercial Guide 2023 — T1
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT
- Scope: universal (readability, zoning, switchboard geometry); the security wording is UK commercial
- Confidence: High (readability, zoning, switchboard clearance) / Medium (the 1,500/1,000 mm pair is one estate's number, though it matches common practice)
- Grid translation: `meter` fixture must be adjacent (≤1 tile) to a `walkable` tile that is itself on a path from a perimeter door without crossing an `occupant`-tagged room — this is the "read without entering" test; every storey must own ≥1 `isolation_valve` tile per service; switchgear tile-set needs a 3-tile front band and a 2-tile rear band and shares no tile with `pipe`; plant/electrical rooms must have a door on the perimeter *or* on general circulation. Emit `meters_not_floor_readable`, `storeys_without_isolation`, `switchroom_service_conflicts`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Metering a leased tenancy from the public side means the riser is shared, which reintroduces the security question (SBD §33 requires secure, visible access covers rated LPS 1175 SR2/SR3). Symptom: a meter board behind a reception desk, or a drain-down of the whole building to fix one WC float.

### OM-21 Nothing maintainable goes above a ceiling, a bench or a fixed unit

- Rule: Servicing happens at a hatch, from the floor, by one person. Do not place regularly-serviced equipment in ceiling voids or roof spaces; where unavoidable, provide full-size hinged panels or boarded walkways; hatches must be **≤1,200 × 600 mm and removable by one person**; dismantling a ceiling to reach equipment is not acceptable; maintainable items must not sit above benches, IT or fixed furniture, in confined spaces, or behind a wall built later.
- Evidence: Oxford BSDG G1.2–G1.3, T1/T3: "Wherever possible, the designer must avoid positioning equipment, such as fan coil units, which require regular servicing and maintenance, in ceiling voids and roof spaces. Where this is entirely unavoidable, a safe, easy means of access must be provided. This could involve full-sized hinged panels, boarded-out walkways … Equipment within ceiling voids must be accessible via a removable ceiling tiles or purpose-made hatches that are no larger than 1200 x 600 and can be demonstrated as removable by a single person. Dismantling of a ceiling system to access equipment for maintenance is not accepted … Equipment which needs to be serviced or regularly accessed must not be located above laboratory benches, computer equipment or fixed room furniture … Where equipment is installed before internal walls are completed, ensure that these walls will not impair its serviceability once they are built … Do not install maintainable components in areas that could become confined spaces." The same guide sets the fan-coil rule at plan level: units "must be directly above the ceiling finish and must be accessible from below the finished ceiling level. Fan-coil units must not be installed above fixed furniture or laboratory benching" (A2.7). Cost of the omission: access panels "cost $50–100 each, 'not having them costs $500–1,000 per damper in ceiling removal and reinstatement during maintenance'" (PP-19, T3).
- Source: Oxford BSDG — T1/T3
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: universal (proxied in plan — the grid has no section)
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: since the grid has no section, use the plan proxy: any `maintainable` fixture must be within 1 tile of a `walkable` tile *in the same room*, and must not be inside the tile-set of any `fixed_furniture | bench | storage` footprint; every services tile adjacent to a room gets a `hatch` door tile counted at ≤4 tiles (1,200 × 600 mm = 2.4 × 1.2 m outer — i.e. a 2-tile hatch run, rounding up). Emit `maintainable_above_fixed_tile_count`, `fixtures_without_hatch_access`. "Confined space" and "single-person removal" are unmodellable — flag.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Long-span hotels/offices genuinely route air through corridors above ceilings; the honest output is "requires section + hatch strategy", not a silent pass. Symptom (named in practitioner forums per PP's access-limits note): an access panel cut into the neighbour's room.

### OM-22 Lay the staff route with the welfare arithmetic, and keep the two suites apart

- Rule: Provide the staff circuit entrance → access control → lockers → changing → WC/shower → rest/canteen → post with these unit allowances: **locker bay 1.5 m² per 12 lockers; communal changing 1.4 m² per locker, sized at twice the staff on shift +10 %; staff rest/mini-kitchen 1.8 m² per seat for the maximum staff on shift; staff WC 1 per 24 beds (or 1 per 20 lockers); ward locker bay 1 per 12 beds** — and keep men and women changing separately, with seats and tables for all shift staff at once, and lockers where contaminated clothing must be separated.
- Evidence: NHS HBN 04-01 schedules (T1) give every unit figure above verbatim, including "Communal changing area (size based on number of lockers) 1.4 … Twice number of lockers as staff on shift plus 10% contingency … Space allowance should be combined with neighbouring department to create viable male and female changing rooms" and "Staff rest and mini kitchen … 1.8 m² per seat … 3 for 8 staff (maximum staff on shift) … Space allowance should be combined with neighbouring wards to create a viable staff rest room", plus "WC: ambulant 2.0 … 1 for up to 20 lockers". Legal duties: OSHA 29 CFR 1910.141(e) requires change rooms where "the wearing of protective clothing and equipment in their work activities would result in toxic materials … being carried home on the personal clothes of the employee" and toilet facilities per Table J-1 (1 water closet for 1–15 persons, 2 for 16–35) (T1); HSE construction-welfare guidance: "Workers need changing areas if special clothing is worn", "Men and women should be able to change separately", "Lockers needed for safe storage of clothing and effects", "Tables and seats with backs for all workers at once", "Facilities to prepare food and boil water required" (T1). Access-control duty: multiple staff entrances are legitimate but "all such doorsets must be SBD compliant. Criminal opportunities are reduced by controlling these doorsets … An integrated access control system of this type will maintain a record of attendance" (SBD §46.1–46.2, T1) — and "None of these arrangements should be allowed to prevent emergency egress" (§46.4).
- Source: NHS HBN 04-01 — T1; 29 CFR 1910.141 via Cornell LII https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/29/1910.141 — T1; HSE construction welfare https://www.hse.gov.uk/construction/healthrisks/welfare/changing-rooms-and-lockers.htm — T1; Secured by Design Commercial Guide 2023 §46 — T1
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: any staffed building; hospitality and healthcare most acute
- Confidence: High (T1 arithmetic + T1 legal duties) / Medium on transferring NHS unit rates to hotels and offices
- Grid translation: `lockers = 2 × ceil(staff_on_shift × 1.1)`; `changing_tiles ≥ 5.6 × lockers` (1.4 m²), `rest_tiles ≥ 7.2 × seats`, `locker_bay` at 6 tiles per 12 lockers; assert a `staff`-tagged walkable path from the perimeter door through `access_control → locker → changing → wc → break_room → post` that uses no `public`-tagged room tile and no `portal_guest` (extends PP-06 with numbers); assert WC count against the headcount table.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Small buildings combine: male/female changing in one room on a time split, rest room shared with the canteen across departments — the HBN schedule itself instructs combining with neighbouring wards. Symptom (PP-06 evidence): staff eating in a basement corridor; a missing time-split that makes the "combined" provision unusable for one group at all times.

### OM-23 Make the service edge visible: security zoning as a sightline test on the grid

- Rule: Design the operational edge so it cannot be abused unseen: **one controlled public entrance with reception view of the approaches; avoid recessed doorways; secure utility access covers "positioned in highly visible locations"; lockable, roofed bin stores set apart from the building with combustible waste ≥10 m from any façade; a written CCTV operational requirement before any camera, installed to BS EN 62676 with vandal-resistant housings in high-risk places; and access control with data logging on doors to communal areas.**
- Evidence: Secured by Design Commercial Guide 2023 (Association of Chief Police Officers' scheme, hosted by a UK local authority) — T1: §45.1–45.3 public access "should be restricted to one main visitor entrance", electronically controlled from a reception or security office, "Reception staff should be afforded a clear view of the approaches to the entrance"; §47.1/47.3/47.5 visitor control by staff "preferably on a raised dais using automatic locking doors or barriers", escape route to a lockable place of safety with "a 'slam to lock' door with a facility to view the public area from within"; "24 Avoiding door recesses — Recessed doorways should, where possible, be avoided as they provide venues where crimes such as graffiti, arson and burglary are committed"; §33.1 telecoms/other utility access covers "certificated to a minimum of LPS 1175 Security Rating 2 … and be positioned in highly visible locations … This will help to delay or prevent the occurrence of burglaries where the perpetrators cut the CCTV or alarm signal prior to committing an offence"; §93.1–93.4 bins "should be retained in a lockable roofed store set apart from the main buildings and the containers themselves fitted with lockable lids … In general, combustible materials should be stored within [not] 10 metres of the building … Bin stores for kitchen waste in large catering and other business operations should be located away from the building"; §92.1 heavy plant and building equipment protected "by using secure storage areas on site, within the main building where possible"; §95.1 "A secure store, or stores, inside the building must be provided for the safe storage of cleaning equipment and cleaning fluids as many of these materials are flammable and toxic"; §96.1–96.3 utility access covers secured, meters in a secure plant room readable from outside; §35 CCTV "must have a recording format that is acceptable to the police", "must be installed to BS EN 62676", "In high crime areas CCTV installations should be protected with vandal-resistant housings"; §48.3 "Access control systems should have a data logging facility"; §52 lift vandal resistance per BS EN 81-71.
- Source: Secured by Design Commercial Guide 2023, PDF https://www.eppingforestdc.gov.uk/app/uploads/2025/07/SBD-COMMERCIAL_GUIDE_2023_v4.pdf — T1
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT
- Scope: offices, retail, hotels, hospitals, large public buildings, mixed-use (SBD criteria become mandatory only where accreditation is a planning condition)
- Confidence: High for the criteria as written; Medium for transfer outside England/Wales
- Grid translation: (a) *visible-zone test*: from each `reception | cctv | guard` fixture compute the tile set reachable by straight and 45° lines through `door` and `walkable` tiles without crossing a `blocked` tile; require every `dock_door`, `waste_store`, `riser_cupboard`, `utility_cover` and `staff_entrance` tile to be in that set, or in an explicitly `secure`-tagged cluster; report `blind_service_tiles`. (b) `recess` = any door tile with 2 blocked tiles on both flanks and no adjacent monitored tile → count `recessed_doorways`. (c) bin store: ≥1 blocked-tile enclosure, lockable door tag, and ≥20 tiles (10 m) from any `habitable` façade tile. (d) cleaning/chemical store: a single `secure` cluster per building in addition to the per-floor closet.
- Exceptions / failure mode: SBD itself demands balance with accessibility and inclusive design: locking systems and access control must not disadvantage users, and "None of these arrangements should be allowed to prevent emergency egress" (§46.4, and the guide's inclusive-design caveat in §1/§45). The 10 m standoff is often physically impossible on an urban plot — the compensating provision is the lockable roofed store plus fire detection and consultation with the fire authority, which the guide also names. Symptom: a bin cage under the guest windows; a fire door propped because the access-control log made egress worse.

### OM-24 Express the operational tax as four separate ratios, not one "BOH %"

- Rule: Extend PP-02 by measuring four shares independently, because they are bought and sold separately in value engineering: **circulation share, communication (services shaft/riser) share, engineering/plant space share, and served-room share.** A published, prescriptive reference set exists for hospital wards: circulation allowance 25–31 % of NIA (rising with single-room percentage), communication allowance 10 %, engineering space allowance 25–28 % — i.e. the operational tax exceeds the clinical area itself, and grows as rooms get more private.
- Evidence: NHS HBN 04-01, "Example schedules of accommodation for Health Building Note 04-01 — Adult in-patient facilities" (T1), 24-bed ward at 50 % / 83 % / 100 % single-bed rooms: NIA 665.5 / 708.5 / 714.0 m²; "Circulation allowance 25.0 % / 27.5 % / 31.0 %"; "Communication allowance 10.0 % (all three)"; "Engineering space allowance 25.0 % / 27.0 % / 28.0 %"; GIA 1,064.8 / 1,165.5 / 1,206.7 m². Note the direction of travel: privacy (more single rooms) *increases* circulation, which is why "bed cluster" organisation, not room count, drives service demand (§4.7–4.10, T1: clusters of beds that can be combined, each cluster served by its own staff and support facilities). Engineering allowance is also explicitly scale-dependent: "generally downwards as GIA increases" for a site assumed at 25,000 m² GIA (§7.6–7.7, T1).
- Source: NHS HBN 04-01 — T1
- Class: FACT
- Scope: measurement method universal; the numbers are NHS acute in-patient
- Confidence: High as an arithmetic demonstration that the tax is large and correlated with privacy; Low transferring the percentages to hotels/offices/retail
- Grid translation: report per storey and per building: `circulation_tiles / walkable_tiles`, `services_band+shaft_tiles / total_tiles` (PP-18's band, now metered), `plant_tiles / total_tiles`, and `service_room_tiles / served_room_tiles`; alert when circulation share rises but service rooms did not (a plan that bought corridor instead of pantry). Track the *coupling*: if single-room/pod proportion rises and `circulation_share` does not, the plan is lying about its door count.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Open-plan offices and big-box retail invert the ratio (low circulation, low plant share per m²); the same four metrics still apply but the bands don't. Symptom of not metering: an "efficient" plan with 8 % circulation and no disposal holds, discovered at week 40 of the programme.

### OM-25 Keep the servicing moment out of the occupants' senses — sightlines, noise, odour, hours

- Rule: Servicing must be invisible, silent-adjacent and time-separated: **no direct sightline from the public street or occupant face to the dock, waste or plant; rear-lane or lower-level servicing in mixed-use blocks; no waste/shipping function adjacent to habitable rooms; and out-of-hours delivery windows agreed where the yard cannot be enlarged.**
- Evidence: City of Toronto *Retail Design Manual* (2019) §3.3, T1 municipal guidance: "Avoid direct sightlines from the public street to the loading dock and servicing areas. When these areas are at grade on a back street or laneway, provide ample lighting for public safety, landscaping for screening, and well-integrated back doors"; "Consider fully concealed 'back-of-house' areas in larger scale and mixed-use projects whenever feasible. These areas can sometimes be integrated on a lower level (below grade parking garage) or on the second floor of mixed-use buildings"; "Protect the residential units in mixed-use buildings from noise and odours associated with shipping and waste disposal" — with soundproofing and acoustic glazing named; and on the street side, "Providing servicing, loading and parking access from a rear public lane and/or shared driveways" (§1 best practices). Also §2.2: "Most retail operators require wall space for merchandise display and back-office functions, storage, kitchen areas, waste collection, etc. It is usually difficult for them to visually connect the store interior with the exterior on more than one side of the unit." Operator practice from the hotel side: time separation is the standard substitute for physical separation — "resorts also need larger refuse storage areas, and in hot climates, these should be temperature controlled to reduce odours", while city hotels store less because collection is more frequent (WATG, T3, already cited in PP-05).
- Source: City of Toronto, *Retail Design Manual* https://www.toronto.ca/legdocs/mmis/2020/ph/bgrd/backgroundfile-157291.pdf — T1; WATG — T3
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: retail, mixed-use, hotels in urban plots, offices with street presence
- Confidence: High (municipal guidance + operator practice agree) / Low on any numeric standoff, none given
- Grid translation: define `public_frontage_tiles` and `service_door_tiles`; assert no straight unobstructed tile line (Bresenham over non-blocked tiles) from any public frontage tile to a service door — if one exists, require a screen tile or a turn; assert no `waste_store | dock | plant` cluster shares a wall tile with `bedroom|guest_room|residential` fixtures (this is PP-05's adjacency rule extended to the noise/odour case, with "flag: acoustic unmodellable"); represent the out-of-hours window as a tag on the portal (`service_only_between`), not as geometry, and state it.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Deep single-frontage sites have no rear lane — then the dock must be lifted (mezzanine/goods lift, OM-15) or the street is used with a permitted bay; declare which. Symptom: the 06:00 reversing alarm on a residential elevation, or the recycling placed on the public footway because the store is across the lobby.

### OM-26 Dimensional register: the objects that set the widths, and the retail grid that receives them

- Rule: Freeze a dimensional register of the objects that will move through the building — trolleys, bins, beds, stretchers, pallets, racks, deep-clean machines — and let it set minimum door runs and route widths; in retail, the same logic applies to the *structure*: a floor plate that cannot span a goods route and a column-free frontage cannot be re-let.
- Evidence: Object dimensions gathered above: ambulance stretcher 610 × 2,134 mm and convalescent bed 720 × 2,040 mm (Mitsubishi car layout, T3/T4); housekeeping cart "can weigh more than 330 lbs" and must be checked against car size (Mercura, T4 — no dimensions published); standard waste and recycling containers 90 US gal, tilt trucks possible (McGill, T1/T3); wheeled healthcare waste bulk carts 770 L (GOV.UK, T1); EUR/ISO pallet handling routes need 2.1–2.4 m for a pedestrian pallet truck, 2.3–2.8 m powered stacker, 2.7–3.2 m reach truck, 3.5–4.2 m counterbalance (AWE/Toyota, T3/T4); janitor closet 1.5 × 1.8 m min with a 915 mm door (McGill); deep-clean machine stored centrally (HBN 04-01 §4.42). Retail structure: "A typical retail column grid is 9 m (30 ft.). Usually a column grid smaller than 7 m (23 ft.) hinders tenant flexibility", and "Set back columns in the retail podium from the storefront line whenever feasible (e.g. by at least 1 m (3 ft.)" (Toronto §3.4, T1).
- Source: as listed per item above (Mitsubishi; Mercura; McGill; GOV.UK; AWE; Toyota; NHS HBN 04-01; City of Toronto)
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: hospitality, retail, healthcare, offices with mailroom/FM stores
- Confidence: High for the objects with published specs; Medium (cart width is inferred, not published)
- Grid translation: `min_route_width = ceil(object_width / 0.5) + 1 tile` for a pushed load (clearance allowance, 200 mm from the Asta convention rounded to a whole tile) and `+2 tiles` where the load may be turned; `min_door_run = ceil(object_width / 0.5)`; pallet route = 5 tiles (2.5 m) clear; retail structural rule = `column_spacing_tiles ≥ 18` (9 m), warn below 14 (7 m), and `column_setback_from_frontage ≥ 2 tiles`. Emit `objects_without_a_route`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Existing structures cap the grid at 6–7 m and the answer is a service route along the perimeter with storage in the middle — record the trade, don't pretend the register was met. Symptom: a new-fit pallet that cannot reach the stockroom, so goods are carried by hand across the sales floor.

### OM-27 Cleaning and servicing frequency is a load on the plan: convert each frequency into a daily tile-flow

- Rule: Take the cleaning/waste/supply specification's frequencies and convert them into a daily route count, then check the plan absorbs it: high-frequency tasks need short routes and nearby storage; event-driven deep cleaning needs a machine route and a water point; contract cleaning needs a signed-in, controlled, out-of-hours route that does not depend on occupant cooperation.
- Evidence: Large public building cleaning contract, Maryland Stadium Authority *Cleaning Specifications* (T1/T3 public-sector procurement document): the operator "will provide sufficient staging areas and storage space for the use of the cleaning contractor's personnel and for the on-site storage of the contractor's equipment and supplies. Both buildings must have its own equipment", with "a complete inventory of equipment once a month"; contractor staff "shall sign in and out of the facility and shall display an identification badge at all times"; the "quality standards of cleanliness shall be measured" visibly (e.g. "No debris, soil, or dust will be visible around trash receptacles. Black bags are for trash and clear bags are for recycling"); and mechanical floor cleaning is tied to events, not calendars: "Utilizing mechanical equipment to uniformly clean entire [epoxy/tiled] floor surface removing all visible soil and liquid stains. Frequency of mechanical cleaning shall be every game", with daily/weekly cycles for corridors, tunnels, concourses, "Trash and Loading Docks" and infrequently-used spaces that "shall be inspected daily to ensure requirements are met". Interior window cleaning is bounded by reach: "squeegee washed by the contractor … up to a height of 12 feet or the reasonable reach of the appropriate squeegee".
- Source: Maryland Stadium Authority, *Cleaning Specifications* (Attachment A) https://mdstad.com/sites/default/files/2020-08/Attachment%20A.Cleaning%20Specifications.FINAL_.pdf — T1/T3
- Class: FACT
- Scope: large public buildings, stadiums, airports, convention centres; transfers to hospitals/hotels as an operator-load argument rather than as cited numbers
- Confidence: High for the existence of the daily load; Medium for transferability of event-frequency numbers
- Grid translation: for each specified frequency, `daily_tile_flow = trips/day × route_tiles`; sum flows per tile and colour the plan by load band; require a `water_point` (sink/floor drain fixture) within 40 tiles (20 m) of every tile in the top load band, and a staging cluster sized to the contractor-equipment count; require the out-of-hours route from a controlled door to the top-load tiles to be tag-permitted for `contractor`. Reach height (12 ft) is out-of-model — record "high-level cleaning requires a ladder route; keep ≥2 tiles clear below high glazing".
- Exceptions / failure mode: Frequency specs written per *area* (m² per cleaner-hour) are not spatial and cannot be verified here; the honest output is the flow map plus "labour model not represented". Symptom: a plan that passes on area standards and is cleaned by people walking 6 km a shift (OM-28).

### OM-28 Staff walking distance is the currency the plan spends

- Rule: Measure the plan by how far the people who run it walk. Measured nursing practice: mean **9,360 steps ≈ 5.79 km per shift** (day shift 9,784 steps ≈ 5,971 m; evening 9,720 ≈ 6,065 m; night 8,576 ≈ 5,321 m; emergency departments highest at 11,801 steps), with shorter unit experience associated with more walking. Treat >6 km/shift as a design signal that supply, disposal or treatment points are badly sited — the same signal housekeeping gives at 13–14 rooms/attendant (PP-03).
- Evidence: Chang & Cho, "Nurses' steps, distance traveled, and perceived physical demands…" *Human Resources for Health* 2022 (117 nurses, 351 shifts; PubMed Central PMC9548108) — T2. Supporting design logic from the operator standard: "Ideally, a dirty utility room should serve no more than 15 beds. This reduces travel distances for staff, making better use of nursing time and reducing the risk of spillages and cross-contamination" (HBN 04-01 §4.69, T1) and "rooms be serviced by trolley, like hotels, so that staff do not need to walk far from their bed cluster" (§4.10, T1).
- Source: PMC https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9548108/ — T2; NHS HBN 04-01 — T1
- Class: FACT
- Scope: healthcare, hospitality, large retail, airports — any walk-served floor
- Confidence: High for the order of magnitude (multiple countries); Medium for using it as a rejection threshold
- Grid translation: for each role, compute the *daily route length*: `sum over served fixtures of octile_steps(post → fixture) × visits_per_shift`, reported in tiles and metres; publish a soft cap (e.g. `≤12,000 tiles ≈ 6 km per shift`) and a metric `tiles_per_served_room`. Where the model can't know visits/shift, use 1 and report the assumption. This is the measurable successor to PP-03's count cap: ≤14 rooms is necessary, not sufficient — the trolley may still walk further.
- Exceptions / failure mode: A compact cluster plan that minimises steps may maximise crossings (PP-01) and minimise pantry count (PP-02) — all three metrics must be reported together, or the agent will game walking distance into a corridor of shared rooms. Symptom: a "efficient" ward where the only way to reduce steps was to remove the second disposal hold.

## Weak or contested

- **Back-of-house as a share of the hotel floor plate.** No defensible published ratio was retrieved this pass: the hotel-planning pages that should carry it returned no quantitative content, and the peer-reviewed tower-efficiency study (MDPI *Buildings* 14(7):2051) returned 403. OM-24 therefore supplies the *measurement method* and the NHS ward's prescriptive allowance set (circulation 25–31 %, communication 10 %, engineering 25–28 % of NIA), not a hotel equivalent. Confidence: **UNCITED — heuristic for non-healthcare ratios.**
- **The 1,400 m² janitor-closet density** is one Canadian university's operator standard (McGill), corroborated only qualitatively by FBC §450.3.9.1's "one per floor" and HBN's "1 per 24 beds". The three figures come from three jurisdictions and three building types. Confidence: Medium.
- **Approved Document H figures (0.25 m³/dwelling, 30 m carry, 25 m to collection point, 450 mm chute)** are quoted from the **September 2014 consultation draft** of the H6 supporting document, not necessarily the in-force text. Confidence: High for the logic, Low-Medium for exact numbers as current law.
- **Service-lift quantities and the 150 ft walk cap (BASE4)** are a consultancy's rule of thumb keyed to US "keys", not a standard; nothing at T1/T2 confirms a lift-per-room-count driver. Confidence: **Medium-Low**; use the 14/15/16 % → 20.6/38.9/85.8 s saturation shape (Peters) rather than BASE4's counts.
- **Mitsubishi car layout numbers** are from a product brochure PDF whose dimension tables extracted imperfectly; the stretcher/bed/dolly footprints are legible, the car dimensions less so. Treat "car depth ~2,500 mm, door openings 750/1,000 mm" as **cross-checked: no** — the *feature list* (reserved mode, extended door-open, isolation key, call-and-send, point loading) is corroborated by Oxford C1.10.
- **Housekeeping cart dimensions were not found** at any tier with numbers; only its weight ("more than 330 lbs" fully loaded, T4) and the instruction to check it against the lift car. OM-01's bay therefore rests on the NHS 2.0 m² trolley bay, not on a cart spec sheet. Confidence: Medium.
- **Retail backroom-to-sales-floor ratio and replenishment time standards**: the two candidate academic sources were unreachable (Emerald 403; econstor served an interstitial page); the trade sources retrieved carried no numbers. The register's retail row is therefore deliberately ratio-free. **UNCITED — heuristic.**
- **Chute-vs-no-chute.** Linen/refuse chute product specs come from chute vendors (interested parties). The infection-control argument against vertical debris routes and the fire-rating requirements are independent (HBN 00-09, McGill); the "decommission chutes" trend report remains PP-05's T4 vendor material and was not re-verified here.
- **Loading apron and radii (Nova, 2013)** are US vendor standards; the 120 ft apron corroborates at T3 (Link Logistics) but the saw-tooth table and the 26/50 ft radii are single-source. Also: nothing retrieved quantifies **truck queuing on the public highway** for city hotels/hospitals, which is the case that actually gets complained about.
- **Welfare unit rates transfer poorly.** The HBN locker/changing/rest m²-per-person figures are NHS workforces with shift patterns and uniform exchange; applying them to an office or a hotel requires local staffing assumptions the grid cannot hold. Time, rosters and workload are out-of-model throughout this file — every "per shift" or "per peak hour" figure above is a design input the agent must assume and state, never measure.
- **MRI floor loading and magnet weights** are from a shielding contractor's guide (T3/T4), not from a manufacturer's site-planning document; AAPM Report 20 and the VA MRI Design Guide were located but not machine-readable this pass. Confidence: Low-Medium; use as "expect tonnes and a load path", not as the number to sign.
- **Unmodellable, and flagged wherever it appears:** time, staff rosters, cost, noise and odour transmission, vibration, slip resistance, fire ratings as material properties, headroom, temperature/pressure cascades, cleaning chemistry, and anything about *what it is like* to push a loaded trolley up a ramp.

## Type-specificity audit

- **Universal (any building on the grid):** OM-01 (working bay), OM-02 (per-floor closet + sink), OM-04 (wet-cell weighting), OM-05 (cleanability), OM-06 (streams multiply rooms), OM-07 (carry distance), OM-12 (goods door datum), OM-17 (plant access), OM-18 (replacement path), OM-19 (risers on public side), OM-20 (metering/isolation), OM-21 (nothing above a ceiling), OM-24 (four ratios), OM-26 (register). These should be enforced even in a 20-room hotel or a low-rise office.
- **Type-bound by law or by scale:** OM-03 (cleaning-method triad) is NHS-worded but transfers to hotels; FBC §450.3.9.1 binds nursing homes specifically, not hotels. OM-08 (dual-sided ward hold) is a healthcare pattern that only makes sense with a porter service; in retail/hotel the equivalent is a single back-of-house hold. OM-10/OM-11 (dock, yard) apply to buildings with a vehicle — irrelevant to a single-frontage shop, decisive for a hospital. OM-15 (reserved-mode bed lift) is hospital/hotel; OM-16 (queue saturation) is universal but its thresholds are lift-specific. OM-22 (locker/changing arithmetic) is staffed-building only, and the exact unit rates are NHS. OM-23's CCTV/SBD criteria are English and commercial — transfer them as *design intent*, not as compliance. OM-25 (sightline/odour separation) is decisive in mixed-use and hotels, weak in standalone industrial. OM-27's event-frequency model is stadium/terminal-specific.
- **Weakly evidenced for offices and retail.** Hotels and hospitals dominate the retrievable prescriptive material (HBN, NHS schedules, lift and cleaning contractors, hospitality consultancies). Office evidence here is essentially custodial-closet standards (McGill, FBC) and lift/vandal guidance; retail evidence is municipal design guidance (Toronto) plus generic materials-handling numbers. Two rules — OM-13 (recessed storage) and OM-26 (register) — carry office/retail by *argument from adjacent domains* rather than by a source that measured an office or a shop floor. Where the skill applies these to offices or retail, mark the transfer explicitly and prefer the geometry test over the borrowed number.
- **What this file cannot decide:** any trade-off that needs time, headcount, wage cost or vehicle economics. The grid can prove a route exists, is wide enough, is short enough, is unseen or seen, and is empty at the moment the trolley arrives. Beyond that it is an assumption, and it must be logged as one.
