# Research file 0P — Economics & spatial efficiency (the money-facing side of geometry)

Scope: how a plan's geometry shows up in cost and value — measurement conventions, net-to-gross,
circulation and core as lost revenue, floor plate and façade economics, span/grid, repetition,
change cost, whole-life and operational cost, and the type-specific productivities (office rent,
retail sales density, hotel cost per key, hospital operating load). Deepens `TH-15` (movement
budget) rather than restating it, and takes the measurement/wall-tax arithmetic from `TH-02`.

> ## DISCIPLINE BOX — no prices without a stamped ratio
> - **This file produces metrics, not quotations.** The engine has no cost model, no heights and no currency. The agent's job is to compute the geometric ratios that cost and revenue actually track, and to reason about their direction. A number of the form "X per m²" may only be stated with **source + jurisdiction + edition/year + area basis** attached. If any of the four is missing, the number is a *directional illustration*, not a claim.
> - **Every cost figure below is stamped.** Example: "frame £173–£281/m² — steelconstruction.info cost comparison study, Central London, Q3 2016 prices, cost per m² of floor area". Unstamped recollections are tagged `UNCITED — heuristic` and belong in `## Weak or contested`.
> - **Benchmarks are convention-dependent, not physics.** Net-to-gross changes when the measurement line moves (BOMA vs IPMS vs GIA), so two "inefficient" buildings may simply be measured differently. Always name the convention before comparing (`EC-01`, `EC-02`).
> - **Maximum efficiency is not the objective.** Several rules below exist to stop the agent optimising a ratio into an unusable, unlettable or non-compliant building (`EC-21`, `EC-22`).

Host grid: 1 tile = 0.5 m; 1 tile = 0.25 m²; 4 tiles = 1 m²; tile states `walkable | blocked | door`
only. A wall is one blocked tile (0.5 m) — far thicker than any real partition, which biases every
area ratio in this file *downward* relative to real-world benchmarks. `## Efficiency metric register`
gives the correction. Lengths used below: 8 m = 16 tiles; 7.5 m = 15 tiles; 9 m = 18 tiles;
15 m = 30 tiles; 18 m ≈ 36 tiles; 4 m = 8 tiles.

Notation (per floor): `T` = all tiles in the floor footprint; `W` = walkable; `D` = door;
`B` = blocked, split where tagged into `B_ext` (façade ring), `B_int` (partitions),
`B_str` (columns/walls declared structural), `B_sh` (shafts/risers).

## Rules

### EC-01 Report three area numbers for every floor, never one
- Rule: Emit, per floor and per building: (1) **gross external area** (whole footprint), (2) **lettable/rentable area** (measured to the outside face of the external wall and the centre line of partitions), (3) **usable area** (measured to the inside face; structure and walls excluded). Label each with the convention it implements. A single "area" number is unreportable.
- Evidence: The measurement standards are built exactly this way. BOMA's office standard defines Usable Area as space a tenant "can actually occupy and use", measured "to the inside finished wall of the primary portions of the permanent outer building walls" and to the office side of corridor walls, with columns *not* deducted; Rentable Area is usable plus a pro-rata share of common areas, and "Gross Building Area is used within the industry primarily to determine construction costs or building value" (UBC Sauder reproduction of BOMA's Q&A — **a 1998 text reproducing ANSI/BOMA Z65.1-1996, i.e. historical, not the current standard**; NAI Keystone summary of the same standard family). RICS/IPMS draws the same three boundaries under different names (GEA to the outside of external walls; GIA to the internal face of perimeter walls; NIA excluding toilets, plant rooms, stairwells and lift wells).
- Source: BOMA Q&A via UBC Sauder https://professional.sauder.ubc.ca/re_creditprogram/course_resources/courses/content/451/boma.cfm — T3 (reproduction of a T1 standard; **the hosted text is a 1998 Q&A reproduction of ANSI/BOMA Z65.1-1996, not the current standard — see `## Weak or contested` item 14**); NAI Keystone https://naikeystone.com/standard-for-measuring-floor-areas-of-an-office-building/ — T3; Black Ac Surveyors on RICS GIA/GEA/NIA/IPMS https://blackacresurveyors.com/blog/why-international-property-measurement-standards-ipms/ — T3/T4 (summary; the RICS/IPMS PDFs themselves were not readable this session).
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: universal (labels differ: GBA/RUS in US/CA, GIA/GIFA/NIA in UK, IPMS 1/2/3 internationally)
- Confidence: High (definitions), Medium (the secondary-source wording)
- Grid translation: `GROSS = T`. `LETTABLE = W + D + 0.5·B_int + 0.5·B_str + B_ext` (centre-line rule for internal dividing construction, full external wall — the BOMA/RICS shape). `USABLE = W + D_within_unit` (inside-face rule; deduct all construction). Report all three in tiles and in m² (×0.25), plus `rentable_to_usable = LETTABLE/USABLE` (see `EC-04` for the screening band 1.10–1.18 — no published methodology, `## Weak or contested` item 6).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Industrial and retail measurement standards differ again (BOMA publishes separate office/industrial/retail/mixed-use methods — ANSI/BOMA Z65.1 family and the gross-area standard, editions 2010→2026, per https://boma.org/boma-standards/), so a mixed-use building needs the area *summed per method*, not blended. Failure symptom: an agent quoting "85 % efficiency" that is actually a rentable-to-usable ratio, or comparing its tile count with a benchmark measured to a different face of the wall.

### EC-02 Say which face of the wall you measured to — structure moves the number, not the building
- Rule: Whenever an area ratio looks bad or good, first test whether it is a boundary artefact: load-bearing structure *inside* the tenant/room boundary inflates the measured area; structure *outside* (or excluded, as under BOMA) deflates it. Report the structural thickness share separately so the reader can move the line.
- Evidence: RICS/IPMS locates the boundary at the **Internal Dominant Face** — "the inside surface that makes up more than 50 % of the lowest 2.75 m of each wall section" — and IPMS 3.2 measures to the IDF plus the "centreline of demising walls", *including* columns and internal structural walls in the occupier's exclusive area; the same source states IPMS 3.2 "typically produces an area 2–5 % larger than NIA" and that this mechanically "lowers the rate per sq ft for the same rent". BOMA takes the opposite tack: "columns are not considered in the Standard" and partitions are measured "to the center line" (Sauder/BOMA Q&A).
- Source: https://blackacresurveyors.com/blog/why-international-property-measurement-standards-ipms/ — T3/T4 summarising RICS Property Measurement / IPMS; BOMA Q&A via UBC Sauder — T3 (**1998 reproduction of ANSI/BOMA Z65.1-1996** — historical wording, not the current standard).
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT
- Scope: universal; the numbers are UK/US specific
- Confidence: Medium (standard content known, primary text not directly readable — the official IPMS residential PDF at https://ipmsc.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/ipms-residential-buildings-sept-2016.pdf returned unreadable binary, and https://www.rics.org/profession-standards/rics-standards-and-guidance/sector-standards/real-estate-standards/rics-property-measurement-2nd-edition was not opened as text)
- Grid translation: Every construction tile is 0.5 m thick, so the choice of face is a *whole tile* per wall — larger than the real-world 2–5 % effect it imitates. Report `structure_share = B_str/T`, `wall_share = B_int/T`, and the two variants `USABLE_incl_structure` vs `USABLE_excl_structure`. Also report the raw-vs-corrected pair from `EC-03`. State which convention your headline ratio uses.
- Exceptions / failure mode: In a load-bearing masonry/wall-housed scheme the "structure" *is* the wall, so the split is arbitrary; declare the allocation rule instead of hunting for a true one. Symptom: two agents report 62 % and 78 % efficiency for the same plan and argue.

### EC-03 Correct the tile-grid wall tax before comparing to any real-world benchmark
- Rule: Before quoting any real-world efficiency figure (net-to-gross 60–80 %, load factor 10–18 %, tower efficiency 55–84 % — the first two are this file's **working screening bands from a T4 vendor glossary, not benchmarks**: `## Weak or contested` items 3 and 6), re-state the plan's areas with real construction thicknesses; a 0.5 m grid wall is 2.5–5× a real partition, so uncorrected grid ratios are systematically low. Report raw and corrected.
- Evidence: The standards measure millimetres of construction: external walls and partitions are boundary elements whose faces define usable/rentable/lettable (BOMA Q&A; RICS IDF rule). The grid rule "wall = one blocked tile" fixes every such thickness at 500 mm, so the wall area share is inflated by construction alone (see `TH-02`'s wall tax, which computes `2W + 2H − 4` tiles lost per room envelope).
- Source: as `EC-01`/`EC-02`; host grid spec.
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High (arithmetic), Medium (the nominal thicknesses used for correction are declared assumptions, not measured values)
- Grid translation: Declare `k_int = τ_int/0.5` and `k_ext = τ_ext/0.5` with nominal real thicknesses (typical internal partition 0.10–0.20 m → `k_int ≈ 0.24–0.40`; typical external wall 0.30 m → `k_ext ≈ 0.60`). Then `GFA_corr = W + D + k_int·(B_int + B_str) + k_ext·B_ext`, `NTG_corr = (W + D_unit) / GFA_corr`, and report `NTG_raw`, `NTG_corr`, `k_int`, `k_ext`. The correction should *raise* the ratio; if it does not, the plan is genuinely wall-heavy, not convention-heavy.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Big-room types (assembly, industrial, retail units, gym) have few walls per m², so the correction is small and the raw number is already comparable. Symptom: agent "fixes" a hospital wing's 55 % ratio by quoting a 60–80 % band, without noticing that the building really has 22 % wall+shaft area.

### EC-04 Set the circulation + core budget as revenue loss before drawing, and report it as such
- Rule: Convert every circulation and core tile into (a) share of gross and (b) *foregone lettable area at the current programme*, then defend any overrun in writing. Movement area is not free space; it is space sold to nobody.
- Evidence: Commercial measurement already prices this: the rentable/usable "core factor (load factor)" is typically **10 % to 18 %** in US office practice, i.e. 10–18 % of the measured area is common/deduction space allocated to tenants, and "costs are charged on gross area" while tenants occupy usable (Vantage Space, *Net-to-Gross Ratio*; *Floor Plate Efficiency* gives 65–80 % usable-to-gross for typical modern office floors, with below 60 % read as a planning problem and above 80 % as circulation starvation — both bands come from one vendor's glossary with no methodology or sample, and are carried here as **working screening bands, not benchmarks**). Measured towers: core-to-GFA ratio averages 26 % (range 11–38 %) across 40 tapered super-tall towers, with space efficiency averaging 72 % (55–84 %) — and *no significant influence* of building function or load-bearing system on efficiency. A comparative skyscraper study found Asian towers averaging 68 % efficiency with 30 % core against 76 % / 21–25 % core for Middle East and North American towers.
- Source: https://naikeystone.com/standard-for-measuring-floor-areas-of-an-office-building/ — T3; https://www.vantagespace.com/gb/glossary/space-planning-layout-principles/net-to-gross-ratio — T4; https://www.vantagespace.com/sr-me/glossary/space-costing-financial-metrics/floor-plate-efficiency — T4; MDPI *Buildings* 13(11):2819 (2023), *Space Efficiency in Tapered Super-Tall Towers*, https://cris.tuni.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/103056634/buildings-13-02819-1.pdf — T2; MDPI *Buildings* 14(11):3345 (2024), comparative skyscraper study, https://cris.tuni.fi/ws/portalfiles/portal/147341324/A_comparative_analysis_of_space_efficiency_in_skyscrapers_Case_studies_from_the_Middle_East_Asia_and_North_America.pdf — T2.
- Class: HEURISTIC
- Scope: office, hotel, residential, healthcare, education (bands differ by type)
- Confidence: Medium for the tower data (T2, tall buildings only, and not retrieved at the primary DOI this pass); **Low for the 60–80 % / 65–80 % bands — kept as this file's working heuristic / screening band (single T4 vendor glossary, no methodology or sample published), not as a documented benchmark** (`## Weak or contested` items 3 and 12)
- Grid translation: `CS = (movement tiles + door tiles)/T`; `CORE = (B_sh + stair/lift/wc service rooms)/T`; `circ_cost_tiles = (CS + CORE) × T × 0.25 m² × declared_rate_per_m²` — report the tile count and, if a rate is *supplied by the user*, the currency figure; otherwise report area only. Assert `rentable_to_usable = LETTABLE/USABLE ∈ [1.05, 1.25]` for office (screening band 1.10–1.18, single T3 summary, no published methodology) and log any increase across revisions as a revenue delta.
- Exceptions / failure mode: In a tower the core is a *capacity* constraint before it is an efficiency one (lift banks, egress remoteness) — `CODE` and vertical-transport rules outrank the ratio. In an open-plan hall or a retail market floor, the "corridor" is lettable space; reclassify before judging. Symptom: an agent reports 6 % circulation and misses that the corridors are 1 tile wide.

### EC-05 Report lettable yield, not net area, as the commercial headline
- Rule: Publish `LETTABLE / GROSS` (yield) per floor and for the building, alongside the programme satisfaction figure. A design may hit every room target and still be a bad building if it yields little of its own envelope.
- Evidence: Commercial leasing is priced on rentable area and the R/U ratio is the standard commercial lever (BOMA Q&A; core factor 10–18 % — NAI Keystone). RICS cost-analysis practice normalises costs to **Gross Internal Area** and warns against comparing projects without checking the area basis, which is the same discipline from the cost side.
- Source: https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/Cost-analysis-and-benchmarking_2nd-edition.pdf (RICS, *Cost analysis and benchmarking*, 2nd ed.) — T1; `EC-01` sources.
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: universal (investor/owner-side types especially: office, hotel, retail, PRS)
- Confidence: High (method), Medium (the specific normalisation basis is UK/RICS)
- Grid translation: `yield_f = LETTABLE_f / T_f`; building `yield = Σ LETTABLE / Σ T`. Report the three-way split `USABLE : non-lettable-shared : construction` as percentages of gross, plus `yield × floorspace_demand` to show whether the site envelope was needed at all. If the user supplies a rate, `revenue_index = LETTABLE_m² × rate` — flagged as the user's number, not ours.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Public/civic, transport and healthcare buildings legitimately yield poorly — the product is capacity, not rent. Symptom: an agent trims a hospital's waiting hall to raise yield and destroys surge capacity (`EC-21`).

### EC-06 Size the floor plate from the core-to-perimeter curve, not from the maximum plot
- Rule: Choose plate size where the two curves cross: adding plate area reduces core share but increases the distance from core/façade to occupied tiles. State the plate area, its `max` and `mean` core-to-perimeter distance, and the daylit share before declaring the plate optimal.
- Evidence: Measured tower data shows core share is the plate-size signature: 40 tapered super-tall towers average 26 % core-to-GFA (11–38 %) with 72 % efficiency, and a 35-building comparative study reports an **inverse correlation between building height and spatial efficiency** (taller = more core). The daylight/quality side caps depth independently of money: the Advanced Buildings daylighting pattern guide records the design rule of "not placing any workstation more than 8 meters (24′-0″) from a window", with a project achieving 100 % access by keeping no space 19 ft from a window, versus a deep floor (Aon, 92 ft max distance) where only 39 % of space fell within 20 ft. Daylight is priced: a hedonic study of **5,145 Manhattan offices found a 5–6 % premium in rent prices for daylight**.
- Source: https://patternguide.advancedbuildings.net/pattern-slideshow/Pattern%201_%20Floor%20Plate%20Geometry.html — T3 (practitioner guide built on US-funded design guides); MIT Real Estate Innovation Lab summary of *The value of daylight in office spaces* https://realestateinnovationlab.mit.edu/research_article/the-value-of-daylight-in-office-spaces/ — T2/T3; the two tower studies cited in `EC-04` — T2.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: office, hotel, residential, institutional slabs and towers
- Confidence: High for the direction of the trade-off; Medium for the numbers; Low for transferring supertall core ratios to a 6-storey block
- Grid translation: For each plate: `plate_m² = T × 0.25`; compute `cpd(t)` = octile distance from tile `t` to the nearest façade tile (or core face for a double-loaded slab, whichever governs); report `max_cpd`, `mean_cpd`, `daylit_share = |{t : cpd ≤ 16 tiles (8 m)}| / W`, `deep_share = |{t : cpd > 24 tiles (12 m)}| / W`. Compare `core_share` and `daylit_share` as a pair on every revision — a gain in one purchased with a loss in the other is a decision, not an improvement.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Windowless uses (stores, plant, records, back-of-house, some clinical rooms) should be excluded from `daylit_share` denominators or the metric punishes correct planning. Deep plates are *rational* where the land is dear and the use is uniform. Symptom: an agent optimises a plate to 2,400 m² and reports perfect yield while half the workstations sit 20 m from glazing.

### EC-07 Make vertical circulation cost explicit: core share is a function of storeys, not of taste
- Rule: Whenever storey count rises, re-derive the core (lifts, stairs, shafts, transfer floors) and report the resulting core share change per added floor. Do not add storeys while holding the core constant.
- Evidence: The measured tower literature is unambiguous on direction: efficiency falls as height rises (40 towers: mean 72 %, core-to-GFA up to 38 %), and the comparative study states the inverse correlation between height and spatial efficiency; regional averages differ chiefly through core size (30 % core / 68 % efficiency in the Asian sample vs 21–25 % core / 76 % in Middle-East and North-American samples). On this host grid every vertical move also carries a modelled 30-second lift queue, so the operational penalty is measurable too.
- Source: `EC-04` tower studies — T2; host sim spec (lift portal queue).
- Class: FACT
- Scope: multi-storey buildings; weakly applicable to 2–4 storeys
- Confidence: High for direction; Low for transferring absolute values (these are 200 m+ towers)
- Grid translation: Report `storeys`, `core_share(f)` per floor, `core_growth = core_share(top) − core_share(base)` (tapers: expect it positive), and `lift_trips = Σ queued portal moves` from the sim. For each candidate storey count, report `GROSS`, `LETTABLE`, `core_share`, `daylit_share` — the four together, so a reviewer can see which lever produced which gain.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Podium-and-tower schemes hide core cost in the podium; count the podium's blocked tiles as core, not as leasable. Symptom: an agent claims "same core at 30 floors" because it duplicated one lift lobby design without re-sizing banks.

### EC-08 Compute plan perimeter per area and report it as an envelope index — with the daylight counter-gain attached
- Rule: Report `P/A` (façade length per floor-plate area) for every floor and the building, because perimeter is the one line item paid for twice: capital envelope and heat/cool load. Never present a low `P/A` without the daylit-share loss it caused, or a high `P/A` without the envelope premium it bought.
- Evidence: Form compactness is a quantified energy lever. GreenSpec (UK) defines the heat-loss form factor as thermal-envelope surface area over enclosed volume and works a case pair — 0.56 (compact) vs 0.72 (form-rich) — reporting that the compact form "can reduce heat loss by approximately 28 %" relative to the form-rich one, and noting the industry rule of thumb that form-rich houses (home-form factor near 4.1 vs a compact 1.0; UK Passivhaus guidance target ≤ 3) "need exponentially thicker insulation". The counter-value is also quantified: the 5–6 % Manhattan daylight rent premium (`EC-06`). The floor-to-floor channel is documented too: in the SCI office frame study, the shallower steel option (4.18 m vs 4.375 m floor-to-floor) "saves approximately 5 % on the envelope cost" and about "4 % of the total building cost".
- Source: https://www.greenspec.co.uk/building-design/heat-loss-form-factor/ — T3/T4 (Fabric First, UK); https://steelconstruction.info/topics/cost-of-structural-steelwork/cost-comparison-study/ — T3; MIT/daylight — T2/T3.
- Class: FACT
- Scope: universal; the energy weight is climate- and code-dependent (UK figures cited)
- Confidence: Medium — computing `P/A` on the grid is arithmetic (FACT); transferring 3D form-factor results to a plan-only grid is the weak link (HEURISTIC), and no floor-height figure in this file is measured
- Grid translation: `P_tiles` = boundary wall/door tiles in the outermost ring; `P_per_m = 0.5 × P_tiles`; `FI = P_per_m / (T × 0.25)` in m façade per m² of floor — examples on this grid: 40×40 tiles (20×20 m) = 0.20; 80×80 = 0.10; 160×100 = 0.065; 240×160 = 0.042. Report `FI` with `compactness = P²/A` (square → 16) and `daylit_share`. The true 3D form factor is **not computable** here (no heights): if needed, use a *declared* floor-to-floor height and label the result "assumption-derived".
- Exceptions / failure mode: Perimeter is a *resource* in hot-dry or view-critical contexts and in retail (shopfronts), where agents should raise `FI` deliberately; a deep industrial shed should have a very low `FI` and a high roof-light count instead. Symptom: agent reports "P/A improved by 22 %" for a building that now has 30 % unlit floor.

### EC-09 Keep the structural bay in the documented economical band and count the bays you use
- Rule: Declare a structural grid, place columns on it, and check that every span falls inside an economical range **you can name a source for** — the study cited here documents two case-study grids (7.5 × 9 m and 7.5 × 15 m), not a published economical-span band, so any band in use is a declared value until a band-bearing source is read. Report bay count, bay aspect, and how many spans exceed it — an "irregular" plan is a structural cost, not a stylistic note.
- Evidence: The SCI/industry office cost study (Central London, Q3 2016 prices) compares frames on two grids: a business-park building on a **7.5 × 9 m grid** (steel composite frame £177/m² of floor area, total build £1,982/m² — frame 8.9 % of total) against RC flat slab (£173/m², total £2,183/m² — 7.9 %), and a city-centre building on a **7.5 × 15 m grid** (steel cellular beam £244/m², total £2,461/m² — 9.9 %) against post-tensioned concrete (£281/m², total £2,565/m² — 11.0 %). All eight figures were re-checked at the cited page this pass and confirmed. The frame share is therefore **8–11 % of total** across the four options, not the single "≈ 9 %" this rule previously printed. Note both the *premium* and the *payback*: on the study's own printed rates the long-span steel cellular beam (£244/m²) is **38 % above** the short-span steel composite frame (£177/m²) — `244/177 = 1.38`; that ratio is **this file's arithmetic on two different buildings, not a percentage the page states**. The page prints **no** span-premium figure, and the "24 %" formerly quoted here matches no pair of its rates: `figure NOT confirmed at source - do not quote`. The *payback* side is on the page: the shallower-floor option is credited with "about a 5% lower building envelope cost" and with contributing to the lowest total building cost (`EC-10`). These are two different buildings, so the pair is a directional comparison, not a controlled span-cost curve.
- Source: https://steelconstruction.info/topics/cost-of-structural-steelwork/cost-comparison-study/ — T3 (industry technical publication; prices dated and located).
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: framed construction — office, hotel, hospital, education, residential podiums
- Confidence: High for the cited £/m² numbers (re-confirmed at source this pass); Medium for generalising the band to other systems (timber, masonry, long-span steel trusses behave differently); **Low for the span premium as a quoted benchmark** — the +38 % is this file's arithmetic across two different buildings, and the page itself states no premium percentage
- Grid translation: `bay_w × bay_h` in tiles (15×18 tiles = 7.5×9 m; 15×30 tiles = 7.5×15 m). Report `bays_per_floor = T/(bay_w·bay_h)`, `column_count`, `off_grid_columns` (structure not on the declared grid), `span_exceedance = max(span)/9 m`. Also report the derived span→space chain: room depth must be a whole number of bays, so `program_fit = |room_depth − k·bay| / room_depth`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Long span buys leased depth and re-lettable floors (and pays in deflection, upstand and floor-to-floor); the standard case for a small shop unit or a villa is a short-span wall-bearing system where "bay" is meaningless. Symptom: agent free-forms columns to fit rooms and reports no grid at all — that is an undeclared cost driver.

### EC-10 Buy ceiling height with the whole stack, and never reclaim it by thinning the plan
- Rule: Floor-to-floor height is a cost chain (structure depth → services zone → envelope length per storey → lift travel → excavation). When the design needs higher ceilings, raise the declared floor-to-floor and re-run `EC-08`/`EC-09`; when it needs cheaper floors, reduce services zone before reducing ceiling height.
- Evidence: The SCI page was re-opened once for this check; what it actually says is: "The overall floor-to-floor height for the steel option is 4.18m… for the concrete option is 4.375m", and that difference "results in about a 5% lower building envelope cost due to the smaller area of cladding", which the page also credits as contributing to that option's lowest overall total building cost. **That is the whole of the confirmed height claim.** The wording this rule previously printed — a frame choice that "reduces the overall building height by 1.5 m … (four storeys' worth of extra façade and lift travel avoided)" — is **not on the page**: `figure NOT confirmed at source - do not quote` (it is also self-inconsistent: 0.195 m/floor × 4 storeys = 0.78 m, and 1.5 m would need ~8 storeys). The 5 % envelope figure stands verbatim; the accompanying total-building-cost percentage (printed here and in `EC-08` as "about 4 %") was recorded by the adversarial audit's own read of the same page but did not reappear verbatim in this pass's retrieval, so quote it only as "the page's whole-building comparison — verify the sentence before use". Height saving on this evidence is **0.195 m per storey in one case study**, nothing more. This is why services coordination, not slab thickness, usually decides height.
- Source: https://steelconstruction.info/topics/cost-of-structural-steelwork/cost-comparison-study/ — T3.
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: multi-storey conditioned buildings
- Confidence: Medium for the ~5 % envelope effect and the 4.18 m / 4.375 m heights (single case study, UK 2016 prices, re-confirmed verbatim at source this pass); Low for any whole-building percentage (the audit's read had it, this pass's did not); nil for the removed 1.5 m / "four storeys" claim — not confirmed at source, do not quote
- Grid translation: Not directly computable (no section). Report the *declared* `floor_to_floor` and the derived quantities: `envelope_area ≈ P_per_m × floor_to_floor` per floor; `wall_per_lettable_m² = envelope_area / LETTABLE_m²`; `extra_storey_cost_index = P_per_m + Σ(core growth from EC-07)`. Present height changes as "cost of height" with these three numbers, and state explicitly that the grid cannot verify them.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Single-storey, warehouse and industrial buildings trade height for volume cheaply (the envelope is bought anyway for racking/clearance). Symptom: agent "saves" by squeezing plant rooms below equipment maintenance clearance (see the `CODE`/`PP` service-zone rules).

### EC-11 Maximise repetition of whole room modules; count the exceptions explicitly
- Rule: Design so that the same room footprint recurs. Report the number of distinct room types against the number of instances, and treat every non-repeating room as a declared premium.
- Evidence: Measurement/benchmarking discipline requires costs to be compared per unit of area on a stated basis (RICS cost analysis, GIA normalisation), and the practical reason repetition is cheaper is reuse of formwork and setting-out: published formwork suppliers quote aluminium systems at **200+ reuse cycles** and steel at **150+** against timber, with contact-area rates of $40–$200/m² — i.e. the same panel earns its cost back only if the geometry repeats. Hotel practice states the same idea for this building type: DFMA hotel design is organised around repeating standard guest-room units (300–400 sq ft) with lobbies at 10–15 % of area, explicitly to cut cost and programme. Tier warning: these are vendor/practitioner claims, not measured project data.
- Source: https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/Cost-analysis-and-benchmarking_2nd-edition.pdf — T1; https://bfs-industries.com/blog/concrete-formwork-pricing/ — T4 (vendor); https://studioanyo.com/design-principals-for-dfma-hotels/ — T4.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: hotel, residential, student/dormitory, office, prison, hospital wards, data/industrial
- Confidence: Medium for the principle; Low for the cycle counts and rates
- Grid translation: Canonicalise each room by its `(outer_W × outer_H, area_tiles, façade_side_count)` signature; report `instances`, `distinct_types`, `repetition_index = 1 − distinct/instances`, `nonstd_share = walkable tiles in singleton types / W`, `bay_repetition = tiles on the modal bay / total structural tiles`. Target ≥ 0.7 repetition for cell types, ≥ 0.5 for office floors. Report the *exception list* (each singleton with the reason).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Repetition of a bad module multiplies a bad plan (noise through a shared wall, no cross-ventilation, one-sided rooms). Also, high repetition with *large* module variety in services/M&E kills the saving — check `EC-16`. Symptom: 30 identical rooms, each 3×3 tiles (0.25 m² interior) — perfectly repeatable and unbuildable.

### EC-12 Climb the specificity ladder in order and attach a declared penalty to each rung
- Rule: Rank every element as (1) standard/repeated, (2) adapted-standard, (3) one-off/non-standard. Start from (1) and only move up when a documented functional need forces it; record which rung and why. Never put a whole floor on rung 3.
- Evidence: Cost-analysis practice separates like-for-like elements precisely so premiums are visible (RICS element breakdowns, GIA normalisation, and its warning against comparing projects without normalising). Published supplier data on formwork shows that cost is set by reuse opportunity, not by material: the same panel is cheap at 200 cycles and ruinous at one.
- Source: RICS cost analysis — T1; BFS formwork — T4.
- Class: HEURISTIC
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: Medium (the ladder is sound; the magnitude of each premium is project- and market-specific)
- Grid translation: Tag every room type and wall run with a rung; report `rung_mix = {r1: %tiles, r2: %tiles, r3: %tiles}` and `premium_elements = count(r3)`. If a rate *is* supplied by the user, express premiums as multiples of the rung-1 element; otherwise state "directional" only. Never emit a currency figure without the user's rate or a cited ratio.
- Exceptions / failure mode: In heritage-sensitive, civic or laboratory work, rung 3 is the brief. Symptom: the agent claims "cost saving through standardisation" while the plan has 40 distinct bathroom layouts.

### EC-13 Keep the element kit small: few wall types, few junctions, few openings per façade bay
- Rule: Minimise the count of distinct construction junctions (plan corners, wall thickness changes, façade setbacks, roof edges). Each new junction type is a detail, a tolerance risk and a maintenance line.
- Evidence: Form richness is measured in these terms in energy guidance — the home-form factor comparison (compact 1.0 vs form-rich 4.1, UK Passivhaus target ≤ 3) treats additional projections as an envelope penalty requiring "exponentially thicker insulation" to compensate; the UK defect literature attributes a large share of cost to avoidable error — GIRI's estimate is that error costs **10–25 % of project cost** — and junction complexity is where errors concentrate.
- Source: https://www.greenspec.co.uk/building-design/heat-loss-form-factor/ — T3/T4; https://cqic.org.uk/insights/how-much-does-error-cost/ (citing GIRI, Jan 2023) — T3.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal; heaviest weight on conditioned and high-humidity buildings
- Confidence: Medium
- Grid translation: `corners = count of convex+concave plan corners on the boundary ring`; `junctures = distinct (thickness-change, re-entrant, step) tile patterns`; `junction_density = corners/T`. Report `corners/1000 m²` and `FI` together, and flag any bay whose glazing line steps (each step = two extra junctions + a weathering detail).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Deliberate articulation for daylight, passive solar, or wayfinding can be worth it; the rule is to *declare* the purchase. Symptom: an "interesting" silhouette with 60 re-entrant corners and a 0.28 m/m² façade index.

### EC-14 Report whole-life cost, not first cost, wherever geometry changes an operating bill
- Rule: For any design decision that alters energy, cleaning, maintenance, staffing or downtime, present both capital effect and operating effect over a declared period (default 30 years), and say which one dominates for that building type.
- Evidence: RICS *Cost analysis and benchmarking* (2nd ed.) requires cost plans to be analysed "within a whole life cost framework", not first cost only. Operating load by type is measured, not assumed: US 2018 CBECS reports **health care at 144.5 MBtu/ft² and 4 % of commercial floorspace**, with inpatient buildings showing the "highest space heating and ventilation intensities" (62.6 MBtu/ft² heating alone), versus **office at 65.6 MBtu/ft² and 17 % of floorspace** — i.e. the same square metre of hospital consumes roughly 2.2× the energy of an office one. RICS's 2024 whole-life commentary likewise states that operational costs dominate a building's life cycle and that early design choices are where that is decided.
- Source: https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/Cost-analysis-and-benchmarking_2nd-edition.pdf — T1; https://www.eia.gov/consumption/commercial/pba/health-care.php — T1 (US, 2018 CBECS); https://www.eia.gov/consumption/commercial/pba/office.php — T1 (US, 2018 CBECS); https://www.rics.org/news-insights/wbef/whole-life-cost-in-real-estate-sustainability-by-design-part-two (RICS, 2024) — T1/T3 (summary only; page rendered sparsely).
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: universal; decisive in healthcare, laboratory, data, hospitality
- Confidence: High — class kept: the `assumed` token in Evidence is the sentence's own rhetoric ("measured, not assumed"), not an admitted gap; the STANDARD tier rests on the quoted RICS whole-life-cost framing duty, while the 30-year horizon is this file's declared default rather than the standard's number
- Grid translation: For each candidate scheme, report the operating-driver trio computable in 2D: `FI` (`EC-08`), `unconditioned_buffer_share` (walkable tiles inside rooms tagged as buffer: corridors, balconies, plant, storage — deducted from the conditioned denominator), and `wet_density = fixture_tags/W`. Then state the type-specific multiplier direction using the CBECS ratio (health care ≈ 2.2× office energy intensity, US 2018) as the documented anchor — as a *ratio*, never as a currency figure.
- Exceptions / failure mode: For a short-life or highly re-let building, whole-life energy may be less relevant than adaptability (`EC-17`). Symptom: agent justifies a 0.30 m/m² façade index with "daylight value" for a windowless archive.

### EC-15 Freeze the cost-critical geometry early and price late changes as blast radius
- Rule: Identify the elements that stop being cheap to change (structural bay, façade line, core location, wet walls, floor-to-floor) and lock them at the earliest phase where they can be judged. Report every later change as a blast-radius figure, not as an adjective.
- Evidence: Professional guidance is consistent on direction: value management "should start at project inception where the benefits can be greatest" (Designing Buildings Wiki, which also notes the European SPRINT programme's adoption of "value management" as the formal term), and RICS cost practice locates design influence early in the work stages. The size of the prize is documented in the UK: error/rework consumes an estimated **10–25 % of project cost** (GIRI via CQIC). The classic "cost-of-change multiplier curve" (1× in outline, 10× in construction, 100× in use) is widely repeated but **was not retrievable from a primary source this session** — treat it as folklore (`## Weak or contested`).
- Source: https://www.designingbuildings.co.uk/wiki/Value_engineering — T3/T4; https://cqic.org.uk/insights/how-much-does-error-cost/ — T3.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High (direction), Low (magnitude of the phase multipliers)
- Grid translation: Maintain a **freeze register** with five lockable layers, each with a phase stamp: `L1 grid/bay`, `L2 façade line`, `L3 core + risers`, `L4 unit boundaries`, `L5 finishes/fixture layout`. For each revision compute per layer: `Δtiles` (symmetric difference), `Δshared_walls_broken`, `façade_line_moves`, `riser_moves`, `wet_moves`, and report `blast_radius = Δtiles / T` plus the *highest layer touched*. Rule of behaviour: a change touching L1–L3 after the declared freeze is reported as "high-cost change" with its tile count.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Freezing too early on an unverified programme converts a cheap programming error into an expensive geometry error; the freeze is only valid once `TH-01`'s programme table exists. Symptom: agent happily redrags the core on revision 9 because "it is just tiles".

### EC-16 Place services and wet stacks before partitions, and report the riser index
- Rule: Cluster sanitary fixtures into the fewest vertical groups, align them floor to floor, and keep horizontal fixture runs short. Do not distribute bathrooms one per room across the plan.
- Evidence: NIA/measure practice treats "toilets, plant rooms, stairwells, lift wells" as a distinct non-usable category — i.e. wet and vertical service zones are recognised as space they cost, and BOMA counts code-required walls enclosing a major vertical penetration as part of the penetration (Sauder/BOMA Q&A). NHS-style healthcare space guidance and professional practice literature consistently group clinical and wet functions around service zones (see `PP-*`, `CODE-*` files). The physical reason is drainage fall: waste pipes need a gradient, so each metre of horizontal run consumes ceiling zone and floor-to-floor height (which is priced by `EC-10`).
- Source: BOMA Q&A via UBC Sauder — T3 (**1998 reproduction of ANSI/BOMA Z65.1-1996**); Black Ac Surveyors on NIA categories — T3/T4; host professional-practice research file.
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: any building with plumbing; decisive in hotels, hospitals, residential towers, prisons
- Confidence: High (principle), Medium (the tile-level proxies)
- Grid translation: `wet_tiles` = tiles in rooms carrying sink/shower/toilet fixture tags. Compute vertical clusters: a cluster is a set of wet rooms whose tile-sets overlap within 2 tiles across consecutive floors. Report `riser_count = |clusters|`, `stack_alignment_share = wet tiles inside a vertically aligned cluster / wet tiles`, `max_drain_run_tiles = max distance from a wet tile to its cluster's riser tile`, `wet_wall_repetition` (is the same bathroom module mirrored/rotated/identical?). Target: `stack_alignment_share ≥ 0.9`, `max_drain_run_tiles ≤ 8` (4 m), `wet_wall_repetition ≥ 0.8`. **Representation gap:** the grid has no heights, so the fall consumed by a drain run cannot be checked — it must be reported as an assumption.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Dual-aspect suites, villas and campus-style low-rise schemes legitimately raise `riser_count`; large gravity drainage runs are cheap in single-storey slabs on grade. Symptom: a hotel where every bathroom sits at a different (x, z) per floor — 30 risers for 30 keys.

### EC-17 Give adaptability an explicit value and measure the plate's conversion envelope
- Rule: Where the future use is uncertain, buy optionality with the two geometries that survive change: a shallow enough plate for daylight in the alternative use, and a grid/height that the alternative use can tolerate. Report these as measured properties of the plan, not as intentions.
- Evidence: The office-to-residential conversion literature and practice note the dimensional criteria: Neumann Monson (2023) finds "smaller floor plates provide daylighting and views to more occupants" and works with ~60-ft (≈18 m) plates as the size that suits residential reuse, while deep cores "hinder daylight". The value side is documented from the rent side (5–6 % Manhattan daylight premium) and from the measurement side (RICS: cost analysis must serve whole-life decisions, and comparing buildings requires normalising on a stated area basis — adaptability is what keeps that comparison honest across a change of use).
- Source: https://neumannmonson.com/blog/design-thinking-plates-office-adaptive-reuse — T3; MIT/daylight — T2/T3; RICS cost analysis — T1.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: speculative office, retail shells, campus and institutional, any pre-release project
- Confidence: Medium (dimensional criteria are practice statements, not code)
- Grid translation: Report the conversion checks: `max_depth_m`, `daylit_share`, `core_position_type` (central / side / offset — side cores convert better), `bay_m` vs the receiving use's tolerance (residential comfort ≈ 6–9 m; car-park-derived structures ≥7.5 m), `floor_to_floor_declared` vs the receiving use's minimum, `wet_cluster_mobility` (can stacks be extended without crossing leased area?), `party_wall_pattern` (are the walls you cannot move the expensive ones?). Emit a one-line "conversion verdict" naming the first criterion that fails.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Single-purpose buildings (theatre, laboratory, mortuary, detention) should *not* be optimised for adaptability; the specificity is the value. Symptom: agent adds a second staircase "for flexibility" where the brief is a warehouse.

### EC-18 Retail: allocate floor area by space-to-sales, and never let productivity reclaim code space
- Rule: For each sales zone/tenant, hold `selling_share` and area-to-sales proportionality as the headline metrics, and re-allocate area toward the categories that earn per m² — but treat aisle widths, accessible routes and exit capacity as untouchable.
- Evidence: Retail space productivity is defined as "total sales over a period divided by the selling area, giving revenue earned per unit of floor", and the governing allocation rule is the "space-to-sales principle: broadly aim to give a category floor space in proportion to the sales (and the strategic role) it delivers"; the same source warns that "clear aisle widths, accessible routes and exit capacity" are code-governed and "the productivity chase must never reclaim space" (STUDIO mtx, retail/store-design curriculum). The practitioner version of the formula excludes back-of-house from the denominator: `SPSF = Annual net sales ÷ Selling square footage` (retailopstoolkit, June 2025 — its category benchmarks are inconsistent with each other and are treated as contested).
- Source: https://www.studiomatrx.org/students/retail-and-store-design/space-productivity — T3/T4; https://www.retailopstoolkit.com/blog/sales-per-square-foot — T4.
- Class: BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION
- Scope: retail units, shopping centres, airport retail, mixed-use ground floors
- Confidence: Medium for method; Low for the dollar benchmarks
- Grid translation: Split unit tiles into `selling` (walkable in rooms tagged sales/display, plus aisles that are part of the sales floor), `stock`, `fitting`, `service`, `wall`. Report `selling_share = selling/W`, `sales_density_index` only if the user supplies sales (then `annual_sales / selling_m²` with the currency and year quoted), and `space_to_sales_gap = category_area_share − category_sales_share`. Keep an `untouchable_area` ledger (aisles/egress) outside the optimisation so the reported efficiency is honest.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Showroom, destination and experience retail intentionally hold low sales per m² to sell high-margin items or brand value; storage-heavy formats invert `selling_share` deliberately. Symptom: agent cuts stock area to lift `selling_share` and creates a replenishment crisis (see `PP-*` back-of-house findings).

### EC-19 Hotels: optimise cost per key and housekeeping distance, not net-to-gross
- Rule: Treat the guest room as the revenue unit and evaluate every plan change against `cost per key` and `service minutes per key`. Report non-key share separately, and never judge a hotel floor by net-to-gross alone.
- Evidence: Published US development cost data is expressed per room, which is exactly why the ratio matters: HVS's U.S. Hotel Development Cost Survey 2025 (2024 costs) reports median per-room costs of **$167,000–$169,000** for limited-service/midscale extended-stay, **$223,000** select-service, **$265,000** upscale extended-stay, **$409,000** full-service and **over $1,057,000** luxury, with an all-property median of **$219,000**; a second US source (Terrapin, 2026) gives select-service $215k–$325k and full-service $385k–$625k per key, plus $25,000–$48,000 per key FF&E for select-service. Practitioner DFMA guidance standardises the key itself (300–400 sq ft rooms; lobbies 10–15 % of area) because repetition of the key module is the cost lever (`EC-11`).
- Source: https://www.hvs.com/article/10219-HVS-US-Hotel-Development-Cost-Survey-2025 — T3 (industry survey, US, 2024 data); https://terrapincg.com/news/hotel-construction-cost-per-key-2026 and https://terrapincg.com/news/architectural-engineering-fees-soft-costs-commercial-construction-2026 — T4 (US, 2026); https://studioanyo.com/design-principals-for-dfma-hotels/ — T4.
- Class: BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION
- Scope: hotels, hostels, dormitories, hospitals' overnight wards (by analogy)
- Confidence: High for the per-key framing; Medium for cross-source agreement (HVS vs Terrapin overlap but are not identical datasets)
- Grid translation: `keys = count of rooms tagged guestroom`; `net_key_share = key room walkable / T`; `corridor_tiles_per_key = movement tiles / keys`; `mean_service_walk = A* steps from the floor's linen/riser tile to the farthest key`; `wet_wall_repetition` (`EC-16`); `cost_per_key_proxy = T/keys` (tiles consumed per revenue unit — the single most useful number on a hotel floor). Report `tiles per key` per revision; target falls as `service_minutes` (proxy) stays within the declared band. Flag any layout where `net_key_share` improves only because `mean_service_walk` grew.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Resort, all-suite and conference properties buy area for experience (`EC-21`); a residential-hotel hybrid may be judged on lettable area instead. Symptom: an agent squeezes the corridor to 1 tile and "improves" net-to-gross while housekeeping carts cannot pass.

### EC-20 Healthcare: operational performance dominates area efficiency; optimise flows and zoning first
- Rule: In clinical buildings, rank the decision criteria as (1) infection control and zoning, (2) clean/dirty and patient-flow separation, (3) capacity and surge, (4) area efficiency. Area efficiency may only be improved where the first three are already satisfied.
- Evidence: The operating-load evidence is measured: US 2018 CBECS puts health care at **144.5 MBtu/ft²** (vs office 65.6), with inpatient buildings having the **highest space heating (62.6) and ventilation intensities** of the commercial stock on 4 % of floorspace — the building type where geometry decides the energy and staffing bill, not the rent line. RICS requires whole-life treatment for exactly this reason. This file's sister research (professional practice: NHS Health Building Note-derived findings on service coordination and back-of-house pressure) supports the same ranking.
- Source: https://www.eia.gov/consumption/commercial/pba/health-care.php — T1; RICS whole-life guidance — T1/T3; local `professional-practice.md` (`PP-*`) — internal cross-reference.
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT
- Scope: hospitals, clinics, laboratories, veterinary, food processing (same logic)
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: Compute before any efficiency metric: `cross_zoning_violations` (dirty→clean path intersections on the movement graph), `clean_dirty_separation_steps`, `bed_to_escalator_lift` / `bed_to_worst_distance`, `surge_reserve_share` (tiles in rooms tagged flexible/empty-capable), and `staff_walk_index = mean A* steps from staff entrance to the farthest patient room`. Report `NTG` last, and annotate it "operational metrics dominate this type".
- Exceptions / failure mode: Non-clinical hospital real estate (offices, car parks, retail) is commercially ordinary and should be judged on yield. Symptom: agent shortens a sterile-services route to gain 3 % efficiency.

### EC-21 Do not treat a high efficiency ratio as proof of quality — run the four false-efficiency checks
- Rule: Before reporting any efficiency improvement, test whether it was bought with (a) daylit or view-deprived occupancy, (b) corridor width or accessibility minima met only by rounding down, (c) capacity/surge reserve, (d) wayfinding legibility. If any is true, report the trade, not the win.
- Evidence: Glossary guidance already states that above ~80 % usable-to-gross "limits circulation" and that below 60 % signals a planning problem — i.e. the metric has a working band, not a maximum (Vantage Space, floor plate efficiency — one T4 vendor glossary; the band is this file's screening heuristic, not a measured threshold: `## Weak or contested` items 3 and 12). Daylight and views are priced (5–6 % Manhattan premium; 8 m workstation-to-window rule). Measured tower data shows function and structure did **not** significantly predict efficiency, so a high ratio is not evidence of good design. Accessibility junction and turning requirements in the `CODE-*` file consume area by rule, not by choice.
- Source: `EC-04`/`EC-06`/`EC-14` sources.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: Emit a fixed four-line check with every efficiency claim: `daylit_share_delta`, `corridor_min_width` (tiles, rounded UP per `CODE-27`), `reserve_share` (flexible/empty-capable tiles / T), `wayfinding_score` (count of decision points per 100 tiles and share of dead ends). A "win" must show all four non-worsening, or name which one it deliberately traded.
- Exceptions / failure mode: In genuinely tight programmes (emergency shelter, minimum-code housing) the trade is forced by the brief; then state that, and quantify what is lost. Symptom: agent reports "efficiency 84 %, no issues" for a plan with 1-tile corridors and 60 % windowless desks.

### EC-22 Separate revenue area from mandated area and never value-engineer the second
- Rule: Keep three ledgers — lettable, programme-required, and code/standard-mandated (egress width, accessible routes and junctions, shaft and plant clearances, maintenance space). Only the first may be traded for cost.
- Evidence: The measurement standards themselves carve the mandated categories out (NIA excludes "toilets, plant rooms, stairwells, lift wells"; BOMA counts code-required walls around a major vertical penetration as part of the penetration — Sauder/BOMA Q&A). Retail practice states the same boundary from the commercial side: productivity "must never reclaim" aisle, accessible-route and exit-capacity space (STUDIO mtx). Egress sizing is a floor, not a target (`CODE-*` file, IBC 1005).
- Source: Sauder/BOMA Q&A — T3 (**1998 reproduction of ANSI/BOMA Z65.1-1996**; the boundary rules it states are that edition's, not the current Z65.1's); Black Ac Surveyors — T3/T4; STUDIO mtx — T3/T4; local `building-codes.md`.
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: Maintain `mandated_ledger` = corridor tiles counted for egress width, accessible route tiles, 3×3 turning junctions, shaft/plant clearance tiles, door-manoeuvre tiles. Report `mandated_share = mandated_ledger/T` as a *fixed* line, and compute `discretionary_waste = (T − LETTABLE − mandated_ledger)/T` — that last term is the only legitimate target for an efficiency improvement.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Where a code requirement is over-satisfied by rounding (a 1.2 m route delivered at 1.5 m because of the tile), the surplus is partly discretionary — say so rather than deleting the clearance. Symptom: agent "recovers" 6 % of the floor by narrowing escape corridors.

Definitions first; then the tile formula; then the commercial meaning; then ranges — **read the
fifth column's labels, not just its numbers**: an entry marked *documented* carries a source with a
stated methodology; an entry marked **internal screening band (no published methodology)** is this
file's working heuristic (vendor glossary or design call), is **not** a benchmark, and must not leave
the file under that name — see `## Weak or contested` items 3, 6, 12.

| 13 | Structural bay | declared `bay_w × bay_h` tiles; `column_count = T/(bay_w·bay_h)` | Frame, floor-to-floor, envelope chain | case study grids **7.5×9 m** (frame £173–£177/m², total £1,982–£2,183/m²) and **7.5×15 m** (frame £244–£281/m², total £2,461–£2,565/m²), Central London Q3 2016 (steelconstruction.info, T3) | Frame is only ~8–11 % of total across the four options (7.9 / 8.9 / 9.9 / 11.0 % — `EC-09`) — judging a scheme on frame rate alone is the classic error |

| 15 | Riser index / stack alignment | `riser_count`, `stack_alignment_share ≥ 0.9`, `max_drain_run_tiles ≤ 8` | Wet-area cost, ceiling zone, maintenance | no published numeric standard found — internal target (`EC-16`) | Single-storey on grade; dual-aspect suites |
| 16 | Blast radius | `Δtiles/T` + highest freeze layer touched | Change-order exposure | rework/error **10–25 % of project cost** (GIRI via CQIC, T3, UK) | Early-phase revisions (before L1/L2 freeze) are cheap by design |
| 17 | Utilisation | `occupied_area / assigned_area`, `area/person` | Public-sector and corporate occupancy cost | US federal **150 USF per person** design standard (GSA reporting guidance, T1); OMB minimum **60 % occupancy** threshold above 50k sq ft (trade press, T3) | Design standard ≠ utilisation; a well-designed floor can be under-occupied and vice versa |
| 18 | Yield per revenue unit | `T / keys` (hotel), `T / workstation` (office), `T / bed` (health), selling share (retail) | The type-specific "cost per unit of product" | hotel per-key costs: $167k → $1,057k+ by segment, US 2024 costs (HVS, T3); FF&E $25–48k/key select-service (T4) | Suites/amenity-led products intentionally raise tiles per key |
| 19 | Operating intensity | `FI` + `unconditioned_buffer_share` + `wet_density` | Whole-life cost driver | health care **144.5** vs office **65.6 MBtu/ft²** (~2.2×), US 2018 CBECS (T1); inpatient heating **62.6** | A hospital's high intensity is not a design failure; it is the type's baseline |
| 20 | Sales density | `annual_sales / selling_m²` | Retail allocation and rent structure | formula: SPSF = annual net sales ÷ selling sq ft (T4); category benchmarks are **contested** (`## Weak or contested`) | Sales are location-driven; low density in a flagship brand-experience store can be correct |

**Cost-basis rule for all of the above:** any "per m²" figure the agent quotes must state which row
(1, 2 or 3) its denominator is, in which currency, for which year and jurisdiction. RICS cost
analysis normalises on **GIA** and explicitly cautions against comparing projects without doing so
(T1). Turner's published index measures "labor, materials, and market conditions" — i.e. an index
tracks cost movement, never a project price (T3).

## Cost-driver → geometry map

| Driver | What actually causes cost | Geometric lever on the tile grid | Metric to report | Trade-off / what it damages |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Structure span** | Frame action and depth scale with span; depth drives floor-to-floor, which drives envelope, lifts and excavation | Move bay to the economical band (case-study grids 7.5×9 m and 7.5×15 m); keep all rooms on whole bays; minimise off-grid columns | `bay_w×bay_h` in tiles, `off_grid_columns`, `span_exceedance`, `column_count` | Long span buys re-lettable depth and column-free leasing but costs frame rate (frame £177 → £244/m² between the two SCI cases) and needs deeper zones; short span wastes leasing flexibility |
| **Façade complexity** | Every metre of perimeter is bought twice: as a build-up and as a heat-loss surface; each junction type is a detail and a defect risk | Reduce `P` for a given area (compact plate, fewer setbacks/steps); align wall thickness changes | `FI`, `P²/A`, `corners/1000 m²`, `junction_density` | Perimeter is the source of daylight, views, cross-ventilation, shopfronts and escape — cutting FI damages `daylit_share` (5–6 % rent anchor) and the 8 m design rule |
| **MEP complexity** | Distribution length and diversity: risers, drains needing fall, duct runs needing ceiling zone, plant needing access | Cluster wet and high-service rooms; align them vertically; put shallow services where the ceiling is tightest; keep plant adjacent to the shaft it serves | `riser_count`, `stack_alignment_share`, `max_drain_run_tiles`, `plant_access_tiles` | Tight clustering concentrates noise, odour and maintenance access onto leased space; over-clustering blocks future subdivision (adaptability) |
| **Repetition / modularity** | Setting-out, formwork and component reuse: the panel that repeats 200 times amortises; the one-off is hand-worked at every stage | Reduce distinct room signatures; make wet modules identical or mirrored; keep one bathroom family per project; standardise door runs and junctions | `RI`, `nonstd_share`, `bay_repetition`, `wet_wall_repetition` | Repetition propagates errors and monotony; it can defeat aspect, privacy gradient and wayfinding unless the *module itself* was validated first |
| **Circulation** | Movement area earns nothing but sets service time, egress capacity and wayfinding quality | Shorten total movement tiles; prefer shared/spine routes over dendritic fingers; keep `movement` off the wet-zone path | `CS`, `corridor_tiles_per_key`, `mean_service_walk`, `T/revenue unit` | Cutting movement raises queueing and walk time, can breach egress width and accessibility minima (round UP per `CODE-27`), and destroys legibility |
| **Area efficiency (as such)** | The measurement line, not the plan: convention, wall thickness and structure share move the ratio | Choose the reporting convention deliberately and report all three areas; correct the grid wall tax | `NTG_raw`, `NTG_corr`, `R/U`, `wall_share`, `structure_share` | Chasing the ratio into the 80 %+ zone starves circulation and services; ratio gains from convention changes are not design gains |
| **Operational / maintenance cost** | Energy intensity per m² per use, cleaning time, lamp/filter/lifecycle replacements, downtime of revenue space | Compact envelope, serviceable clearances, durable standard junctions, buffers that absorb plant noise, replaceable partitions | `FI`, `unconditioned_buffer_share`, `wet_density`, plus type baseline (health care ≈ 2.2× office, US 2018 CBECS) | Low-energy envelopes with high airtightness and few openings fight natural ventilation, daylight and future reconfiguration |
| **Time (schedule)** | Sequence and rework: long-lead one-offs and late changes to frozen layers | Freeze L1–L3 early; make late revisions only in L4–L5; keep exception count low so trades repeat | `blast_radius`, `freeze_register` layer breached, exception count | Aggressive freeze before the programme is verified converts programming errors into construction rework; the documented size of the prize is 10–25 % of project cost lost to error |

The primary documents (RICS *Property Measurement* 2nd ed. page; IPMSc residential PDF at ipmsc.org) returned unreadable binary content, so the wording and the 2–5 % could not be verified against the standard. |---|---|---|---|

| `EC-15`,`EC-16` | yes | wet-stack geometry binds hotels, hospitals, residential towers | industrial on grade with floor drains; all-electric dry buildings |

| `EC-19` | hospitality only | per-key costs ladder across segments (HVS, T3); resort/conference properties carry far more non-key area | residential-hotel and co-living: judge on lettable area, not per key |
| `EC-20` | healthcare/laboratory/food only | operational intensity and zoning dominate; capacity, not rent, is the product | hospital offices, retail and car parks: ordinary commercial logic applies |
