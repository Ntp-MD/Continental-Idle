# Research file 01 — Architecture theory + design method (operational core)

Scope: the decision-relevant, computable part of architecture theory — programming, adjacency,
zoning, circulation, order/hierarchy, human dimensions and modules, environment, wayfinding,
space syntax, change, and efficiency metrics. Historical and stylistic theory is deliberately
excluded (no decision value on a tile grid).

Host grid this file is written against (from the deliverable spec):
`1 tile = 0.5 m`, per-floor 2D, tile states `walkable | blocked | door` only; a wall is one
blocked tile (= 0.5 m thick); doors are runs of door tiles (2 tiles = 1.0 m); rooms are derived
by flood-fill and typed by fixture tags; movement is A\* octile with diagonals only where both
corner neighbours are open; vertical links are lift portals with queues; no section, no heights;
access control by tags.

Derived grid constants used throughout:
- 1 tile of length = 0.5 m; 1 tile of area = 0.25 m²; 4 tiles = 1 m².
- 2 tiles = 1.0 m; 3 tiles = 1.5 m; 6 tiles = 3.0 m.
- A rectangular room whose *outer* face is W×H tiles encloses `(W-2)×(H-2)` walkable tiles
  (the "wall tax": one blocked tile all round).
- Octile cost between two tiles: `max(dx,dz) + 0.41421×min(dx,dz)` tile-steps. A 1-tile-wide
  corridor forces `dx+dz` (no diagonals), so an open hall is up to ~1.41× cheaper per metre
  corner-to-corner than the same move along a single-file corridor.

## Rules

### TH-01 Write the programme before you place a single tile
- Rule: Before designing, produce a programme table with one row per space type and the columns: user group, activity performed there, required capacity (persons), required net area, equipment/fixture list, service needs (water/exhaust/waste), environmental need (daylight / quiet / dark / clean-dirty), and required relationships to other space types. No room may be drawn that has no programme row; every programme row must end up with an area sum in the plan.
- Evidence: Architectural programming is the pre-design research/decision phase whose output "outlines all the necessary rooms and appropriate relationships between the rooms", alongside site, budget, codes and efficiency targets (The Foraker Group, *Architectural Programming*). The US federal Whole Building Design Guide treats programming as a named design discipline feeding design (WBDG, *Architectural Programming*). The standard programming artefact is a matrix: "the matrix is a grid to sort and organize the information and relationships of spaces to each other space", filled with "size of spaces, if plumbing is needed in that space, do you need to have natural light in the space" (Gustin Design Services, *Programming Matrix*).
- Source: WBDG (NIBS/NIH) https://www.wbdg.org/design-disciplines/architectural-programming — T1/T3; The Foraker Group https://www.forakergroup.org/predevelopment/resources/architectural-programming/ — T4; Gustin Design Services https://gdsatx.com/2018-09-27/programming-matrix/ — T4; Rand/Parker/Zimring, *Problem Seeking: Environmental Analysis in Architecture and Planning*, 5th ed., Routledge — T3 (no stable URL retrieved; cited by edition).
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: Emit the programme table first and treat it as the test oracle. Validation checks: (a) `every programme row appears as a room tag somewhere on some floor, or is explicitly dropped with a reason`; (b) `sum(room net tiles × 0.25) ≥ sum(programme required area)`; (c) `every room has capacity = ceil(area / programme area-per-person)`; (d) flag any room tile-set with no fixtures (an untyped room cannot be validated because the host types rooms from fixture tags).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Fast-replicable types (hotel floors, standard cells, retail units) where the programme is a single repeated row — then the table degenerates to one row plus a count. Failure symptom: a plan that "looks right" but has rooms no fixture tag can identify, or total area wildly above the programme sum (dead space), or a service need (plumbing) discovered after the party wall is placed.

### TH-02 Convert programme areas into tiles and pay the wall tax explicitly
- Rule: Convert every required area to tiles (area in m² × 4), then add the enclosure penalty: a room's outer footprint must be at least 1 tile larger in each direction than its usable interior. Compute net-to-shell area per room and carry the difference in a visible budget, never silently.
- Evidence: On this host grid a wall is exactly one tile, i.e. 0.5 m thick — far thicker than a real internal partition (typically 0.10–0.20 m plasterboard/masonry leaf, per standard construction texts; Ching, *Building Construction Illustrated*, 5th ed., Wiley, ch. 5 — T3, no numeric claim needed here). Consequence, arithmetic not theory, for a footprint whose perimeter is one tile thick: a 6×5-tile outer room = 3.0 m × 2.5 m = 7.5 m² of plate, but only 4×3 = 12 tiles = 3.0 m² of room, because the ring takes `2W + 2H − 4` = 18 tiles — **60 %** of that footprint. A 12×10 outer room = 6.0 m × 5.0 m = 30 m² yields 10×8 = 80 tiles = 20 m² and loses 40 tiles = **33 %**; a 20×15 outer room loses 66 of 300 tiles = **22 %**; a 40×30 outer room loses 136 of 1200 = **11 %**. The tax falls steeply as rooms get larger, which is why this grid punishes many small rooms and rewards a few big ones — an economic argument for suites, open floors and shared party walls, not an aesthetic one. At floor level the ring is shared (two adjacent rooms pay one party wall between them), so the honest floor figure comes from actual tile counts, not from summing per-room taxes. Note also that door tiles are neither wall nor room: `deriveFloorRooms` excludes door cells from every room (`src/engine/npc/rooms.ts:2,15,33`), so a tally that lumps doors with walls overstates structure, and one that lumps them with rooms overstates occupied area.
- Source: derived from the host grid spec (1 tile = 0.5 m, wall = 1 blocked tile) — arithmetic; partition build-up thicknesses: Francis D. K. Ching, *Building Construction Illustrated*, 5th ed., 2014, Wiley — T3.
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: For each room, report `outer_W×outer_H`, `usable = (outer_W-2)(outer_H-2)` tiles, `usable_area = usable × 0.25 m²`, `wall_share = 1 − usable/(outer_W×outer_H)`, and separately `door_tiles` in that perimeter. Validation: reject any room whose usable tile count is below the programme minimum; warn when `wall_share > 0.25` (too-small rooms on a 0.5 m grid are mostly wall). A room needs `outer_W ≥ 3` and `outer_H ≥ 3` to exist at all (a 2×2 tile "room" has zero interior). Two door-specific checks: (a) a door tile placed *inside* a walkable region acts as a room boundary and **divides the region into two derived rooms when the door run spans its narrow width**, because flood-fill will not cross a door cell — so the test is the derived room count: a door run across a throat divides a room, while one or two loose door tiles in an open area do not; (b) classify every tile in the floor tally as `walkable | blocked | door` and report occupied = derived-room tiles, circulation = walkable tiles belonging to no room (plus door tiles), structure = blocked tiles; the three must sum to the grid, and any residual is an unclassified error.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Rooms that share a party wall pay it once, not twice — the naive per-room tax overstates loss when computing whole-floor area; compute the floor-level ratio from actual blocked-tile counts instead. Symptom of ignoring this: agent designs 3×3-tile "bedrooms" (0.25 m² interior) or double-counts wall area and believes the floor is 40 % inefficient.

### TH-03 Grade adjacency requirements and satisfy the top grade before anything else
- Rule: Record every required relationship as a graded closeness value, not as a boolean. Order of satisfaction: (1) must-touch, (2) should-be-close (bounded step distance), (3) may-share-a-corridor, (4) must-not-touch. Fill in the standard five-grade scale used in practice as A = absolutely necessary, E = especially desirable, I = important, O = ordinary, U = undesirable — and treat the letter meanings as a convention, not a measurement.
- Evidence: The adjacency matrix is the standard schematic-design artefact, conventionally drawn square and read against its own diagonal to obtain the pairwise relationship symbol for each space pair (Gustin Design Services, *Programming Matrix*, which describes reading "along the diagonal to the box where the columns intersect, and that specific box contains the symbol" for "mandatory or secondary relationships"; the same matrix + bubble-diagram pair is described as the "traditional adjacency matrix and space bubble diagram" in the peer-reviewed planning-computing literature — figure source on ResearchGate, see Sources). The A/E/I/O/U letter scale in use for ARE exam prep and professional space programming is `UNCITED — heuristic` at this pass: multiple retrievals failed, so the letters' wording is reported as convention (Confidence Low) while the *graded* nature of adjacency is well attested.
- Source: Gustin Design Services https://gdsatx.com/2018-09-27/programming-matrix/ — T4; adjacency matrix + bubble diagram figure https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Traditional-adjacency-matrix-and-space-bubble-diagram_fig1_30870942 — T2 figure; "space adjacency analysis; diagramming information for architectural design", ResearchGate https://www.researchgate.net/publication/284721733_space_adjacency_analysis_diagramming_information_for_architectural_design — T2; Neufert, *Architects' Data*, 3rd English ed., Wiley 2012 (room-by-room requirements and design-method chapter) https://ia600801.us.archive.org/21/items/NeufertS/Neufert-S.pdf — T3.
- Class: HEURISTIC
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High for graded adjacency as a method; Low for the specific A/E/I/O/U letters
- Grid translation: Store the matrix as an N×N integer table (4=must-touch, 3=close, 2=neutral, 1=avoid, 0=must-not-touch). Compute two measured values per pair from the finished plan: `touches` (true if the two room tile-sets are 4-neighbour-adjacent through a door run) and `steps` (A\* distance between the two rooms' nearest tiles). Validation: every grade-4 pair must have `touches = true`; every grade-3 pair `steps ≤ k` (set k per programme, typically 8–16 tiles = 4–8 m); every grade-0 pair must have `touches = false` AND `steps ≥ m` with a buffer room between. Report the matrix with measured-vs-required cells side by side.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Some grade-4 pairs conflict with grade-0 pairs (kitchen must touch service yard, nursery must not touch plant). Such pairs are not errors — they are the input to TH-05. Symptom of a bad matrix: the agent satisfies "must-touch" with a shared 0.5 m wall and no door, which on this grid reads as adjacency but delivers no access (see TH-04).

### TH-04 Test adjacency, access and separation as three separate conditions
- Rule: Never treat "next to" as "reachable". For every pair, state and check which of the three applies: adjacency = tile-sets touch (or share a façade/wall); access = a legal walkable path exists through a door the moving agent is permitted to use; separation = no direct door between them and a minimum path distance is enforced. A plan can satisfy any one while failing the other two.
- Evidence: The distinction is built into how adjacency matrices are used: the matrix records "relationships", while bubble diagrams record the movement/organisation of the same spaces, and the two are read together rather than conflated (Gustin, *Programming Matrix*; "traditional adjacency matrix and space bubble diagram", ResearchGate figure). Codes formalise the separation direction as a *path* requirement, not a touching requirement: egress width and corridor minima in IBC ch. 10 size the connection between spaces, and access control (doors, tags) determines who may traverse it.
- Source: IBC Section 1005 Means of Egress Sizing, UpCodes https://up.codes/s/means-of-egress-sizing — T1; Gustin Design Services — T4; ResearchGate adjacency-matrix figure — T2.
- Class: FACT
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: Emit three independent checks. (a) `adjacent(A,B)` = A and B own tiles that are 4-neighbours, or share a door run. (b) `reachable(A,B,role)` = A\* over `walkable ∪ door` tiles using only doors whose tag permits `role`, returning step cost or ∞. (c) `separated(A,B,n)` = NOT `reachable_via_direct_door` AND `min steps ≥ n`. Validation: for every must-not-touch pair, run (b) with the *most permissive* role — a private room reachable in 1 door from the public lobby is a zoning failure even if it is "not adjacent".
- Exceptions / failure mode: Symptom patterns: (i) rooms marked adjacent in the matrix but joined only by wall tiles — invisible failure unless access is checked separately; (ii) a "separated" service route that is technically walled off but reachable by any user because the host has only one path; (iii) on a 0.5 m grid, a single blocked tile is a full separation, so an agent can create fake separation by thickening a wall rather than by adding distance.

### TH-05 Resolve adjacency conflicts with the four levers, cheapest first
- Rule: When a required adjacency and a required separation collide, apply levers in this cost order and stop at the first that works: (1) insert a buffer room between them, (2) use circulation as the separator (put the corridor between them, so they meet the corridor rather than each other), (3) increase plan distance along the movement graph, (4) stack them on different floors. Record which lever was used and what it cost in tiles.
- Evidence: Buffer/separator strategy is the standard resolution of adjacency-matrix conflicts in space programming practice (matrix + bubble + block planning sequence; Gustin T4, WBDG T1/T3 programming discipline). The stacking option exists only because the host has lifts: on this grid, changing floor is not free — it converts a walking move into a lift trip plus a queue (see TH-29), and the absence of a section means "stack" must be paid in full at the vertical core. Ching's account of organisation of spaces by a *common space* is the formal version of lever (2): spaces are separated from each other by being linked through a shared circulation/entity space (Ching, *Architecture: Form, Space, and Order*, 4th ed., Wiley, "Organization of Spaces", spatial-relationship determinants — T3; the four determinants listed in a chapter summary are "space within a space", "interlocking spaces", "adjacent spaces", "spaces linked by a common space").
- Source: Francis D. K. Ching, *Architecture: Form, Space, and Order*, 4th ed. 2014, Wiley — full text https://archive.org/stream/francis-d.-k.-ching-architecture-form-space-and-order-4-e-2014/Francis%20D.%20K.%20Ching%20-%20Architecture_Form%2C%20Space%2C%20and%20Order%204E%20%282014%29_djvu.txt — T3; chapter summary https://beckwithhouseinteriors.wordpress.com/2020-01-30/architecture-form-space-order-by-francis-d-k-ching-chapter-four-organization/ — T4; Gustin/WBDG as above.
- Class: HEURISTIC
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: Medium
- Grid translation: Cost each lever in tiles so the agent can choose: lever 1 = `buffer room footprint tiles + 2 extra door runs`; lever 2 = `corridor tiles inserted between the two room tile-sets` (≥1 tile wide, typically 2–3); lever 3 = `Δ steps` added to the pair's path length; lever 4 = `1 floor + lift-portal demand Δ` (recompute TH-29). Validation: after resolution, re-run TH-03's three checks; a conflict is resolved only when both the adjacency grade and the separation grade pass, and the buffer room is a real room with a fixture tag, not an untagged tile void.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Lever 2 fails when the corridor is 1 tile wide — separation is achieved but the pinch point now throttles everyone (TH-11). Lever 4 fails silently on this grid because there is no section: nothing stops the agent "stacking" a noisy room directly under a quiet one, which in a real building is a floor-assembly problem, not a plan problem; on a per-floor 2D model, stacking buys graph distance only through the lift. Symptom: conflict "resolved" by stacking, and the lift queue then becomes the bottleneck.

### TH-06 Zone every floor into public, semi-public, private and service bands
- Rule: Assign each room to exactly one of four zones — public (anyone may arrive uninvited), semi-public/semi-private (arrival requires being expected), private (occupant controls entry), service (staff/logistics only) — and require that zones be contiguous tile regions crossed only at named control points. Publish the count of zone crossings per role.
- Evidence: The four-way band is the operative form of territorial thinking in environment-behaviour research and its design application: Newman's defensible-space work is built on the requirement that spaces carry an expression of who they belong to — "territoriality measures the degree to which people have a sense that a space is 'owned' or is 'private'" (Newman, *Defensible Space*, 1972, quoted p. 50 in the scholarly account cited below), and defensible space is "the physical expression of a social fabric that defends itself" (ibid., p. 3). Altman's privacy-regulation framework supplies the same idea at the level of the individual setting: privacy is a boundary-control process, and its states (intimacy, privacy, sociability, solitude) are managed by how much of which territory is open to whom.
- Source: Oscar Newman, *Defensible Space*, Macmillan 1972; HUD-era follow-up *Creating Defensible Space* https://www.huduser.gov/publications/pdf/def.pdf — T1/T3; I. Altman, *The Environment and Social Behavior: Privacy, Personal Space, Territory, and Crowding*, Brooks/Cole 1975 — T3 (no stable URL; cited by title/edition), overview at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privacy_regulation_theory — T4; "Defensible Space and Low-Rise Public Housing Design, 1966–1976", Archimaera/AMST (open access) https://amst.winter-verlag.de/article/amst/2020/2/7/display/html — T2.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal (residential, hospitality, healthcare, education, civic, detention most strongly)
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: Tag every room with a zone value; compute `zone_boundary_tiles` (edges where two adjacent rooms have different zones) and `crossings(role)` = number of doors of a different zone that `role` must pass from the public entry tile to reach its furthest permitted room. Validation: (a) each zone must be a single flood-fill region (or an intentional set of regions, each counted); (b) `crossings(role) = 0` for roles not entitled to a zone — enforce with door tags; (c) warn when zone boundaries zig-zag (high boundary length relative to area) as a sign of interleaved functions.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Mixed-use floors (hotel with lobby retail, hospital with public café ward) legitimately interleave; force the agent to declare those rooms `public` rather than inventing a fifth zone. Symptom: one tile of `private` embedded inside `public` corridor region — a room reachable without passing any control point; or a "public" room with no walkable connection to the entry tile (a zone that exists only as a tag).

### TH-07 Count the doors between arrival and the deepest room — the threshold sequence
- Rule: Design the entrance as a sequence of explicit thresholds, and verify the sequence length grows with the privacy of the destination: street → semi-public → private should pass at least one threshold per step up the gradient, and no private room should be reachable from the arrival tile in zero doors.
- Evidence: Newman's scale argument is that the number of sharers dilutes the sense of ownership and therefore the willingness to control a territory: "family's claim to a territory diminishes proportionally as the number of families who share that claim increases" (*Creating Defensible Space*, quoted p. 17 in the AMST article) — operationally: the larger the shared group, the more intermediate thresholds a space needs to stay legible as somebody's territory. Ching treats transition as a first-class organising device (spaces linked by a common space; the in-between space as the connector rather than the rooms touching each other), and Altman's privacy regulation makes the boundary, not the room, the object being managed.
- Source: Newman via https://amst.winter-verlag.de/article/amst/2020/2/7/display/html — T2 (with quoted page refs to *Defensible Space* 1972 and *Creating Defensible Space*); Ching, *Architecture: Form, Space, and Order*, 4th ed. — T3 (archive.org full text, see TH-05); Altman 1975 — T3.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal; residential / healthcare / detention / civic most sensitive
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: Build the door-graph (rooms as nodes, door runs as edges) and compute `threshold_depth(room)` = shortest number of doors from the arrival tile to the room's interior. Validation: require `threshold_depth` to be monotone non-decreasing with the room's privacy rank (public 0, semi-public 1, private 2+, service 1+ but on a disjoint route); assert no private room has depth 0; assert a suite's internal rooms have depth ≥ the suite entry's depth.
- Exceptions / failure mode: A door run of 2 tiles is still one threshold — depth counts doors, not tiles, so an agent cannot buy depth with a wide opening. Symptom: a "sequence" achieved by two parallel doors in the same wall line (a vestibule with no territory), which raises the count without raising the privacy gradient. Also: retail floors deliberately invert the rule (deepest private space = stockroom placed next to the busiest public zone for service speed).

### TH-08 Keep the service route off the public route
- Rule: Every dirty/quiet-hours-critical function (kitchen, laundry, waste, plant, linen, food) must have a route from its own service entrance to the vertical core or loading point that shares no corridor tile with the public/guest/user route at the same time. Where the two must meet, the meeting point is a named, door-controlled node.
- Evidence: This is the standard separation of "back of house" from "front of house" in building-type programming, and it is the service half of the zoning argument (Newman/Altman above; the room-by-room service requirement in the programming matrix — plumbing/natural-light columns, Gustin T4; and Neufert's *Architects' Data* organisation of planning by room type and circulation, 3rd English ed., Wiley 2012 — T3). Codes encode a weaker but hard version of the same idea: exit access routes must not be defeated by storage or by sharing with other occupancies' escape (IBC ch. 10 means-of-egress sizing and continuity requirements).
- Source: Neufert, *Architects' Data*, 3rd English ed., Wiley 2012 https://ia600801.us.archive.org/21/items/NeufertS/Neufert-S.pdf — T3; IBC ch. 10 via https://up.codes/s/means-of-egress-sizing — T1; Newman/Altman as in TH-06.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: hospitality, healthcare, commercial, industrial, detention
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: Compute `service_route` (A\* from service door to service-serviced rooms and lift portal, role = staff) and `public_route` (role = guest). Validation: assert `intersection(service_route tiles, public_route tiles) = ∅`, or, if not, assert every shared tile lies inside a declared shared node (lobby crossroads / lift hall). Secondary check: no food/linen/waste room's only access is through another occupied room (that fails the door-graph test in TH-04).
- Exceptions / failure mode: On a tight floor plate, sharing is unavoidable — then the fix is temporal (service windows) which this grid cannot represent, so the agent must either pay with a wider shared corridor (2–3 tiles, so one party can stand aside) or accept a documented exception. Symptom: a 1-tile service corridor that is also the guest escape route: a single staff member with a trolley blocks the whole floor, and the A\* step cost for guests spikes (visible as a detour index blow-up in TH-11).

### TH-09 Give every territory a legible boundary
- Rule: Each zone/territory must be bounded by a physical edge (wall/door run) or a strong spatial change, not by a change of tag alone. If two territories meet across open walkable tiles, they merge into one territory as far as the user is concerned.
- Evidence: Newman's design criteria include image, milieu, and *strong boundary* as the means by which a space is recognisably somebody's, and the critique of the towers he studied is precisely that their shared spaces had no definable edge — the "apartment tower itself [...] is the real and final villain" where territorial definition fails (*Defensible Space*, p. 25 as quoted in the AMST article). Lynch's "edge" is the same feature at urban scale: a boundary element that organises the mental map.
- Source: Newman via https://amst.winter-verlag.de/article/amst/2020/2/7/display/html — T2; Kevin Lynch, *The Image of the City*, MIT Press 1960 — T3, teaching PDF https://cus.ubt-uni.net/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Kevin-Lynch-The-Image-of-the-City.pdf — T2; https://www.architecturecourses.org/design/kevin-lynchs-5-elements-city-guide-urban-design — T4.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: For each pair of adjacent rooms with different zones, require `door_run` between them (≥2 contiguous door tiles) or a wall segment of ≥3 blocked tiles. Compute `perimeter_controlled_fraction = controlled_boundary_tiles / total_shared_boundary_tiles` and require ≥ 0.7 for any private zone. Validation: flag zone pairs that share ≥1 boundary tile with neither wall nor door — on this grid that is physically impossible (any shared tile edge is either blocked or a door), so the real failure to hunt is *open* territory boundaries: two zones that both open onto the same undifferentiated hall with no threshold between them.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Deliberately boundary-free civic/retail floors trade legibility for flow — acceptable only if a control node exists at the point where the public stream stops (TH-19). Symptom: a lift hall that is simultaneously lobby, café, thoroughfare and queue space; measured as one room node with degree ≥ 6 and no door controlling any of its edges.

### TH-10 Choose one circulation organisation and name it in the parti
- Rule: Decide the plan's movement organisation from an explicit set before designing: centralised (rooms around a hub), linear (rooms along one spine), radial (spines from a hub), clustered (rooms grouped around several small hubs), grid (orthogonal spine network). State which one, and verify the built plan matches it. Do not mix organisations within a single floor without stating which region carries which.
- Evidence: Ching's *Architecture: Form, Space, and Order* (4th ed., 2014, Wiley; ch. "Organization of Spaces") gives exactly these five forms of spatial organisation: Centralized, Linear, Radial, Clustered, Grid — verified from a chapter summary listing "Centralized Organization", "Linear organization", "Radial organization", "Clustered Organization", "Grid organization". Important correction to the brief: these are Ching's five *forms of organisation of spaces*, not a list of "five circulation organisations", and Ching's list is not "frontal, linear, radial, circular, grid, complex" — that formulation could not be traced to Ching at this pass and should not be used.
- Source: Ching, *Architecture: Form, Space, and Order*, 4th ed., Wiley 2014 — T3 (archive.org full text as in TH-05); chapter-summary check https://beckwithhouseinteriors.wordpress.com/2020-01-30/architecture-form-space-order-by-francis-d-k-ching-chapter-four-organization/ — T4.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High (for the five forms); the "frontal/circular" variant is misattributed — do not use
- Grid translation: Build the movement graph from the walkable skeleton: take the *corridor/lift-hall* tiles, flood-fill them into route segments, and derive a graph. Classify: has one node of degree ≥ 4 whose removal disconnects everything → centralised; max degree ≤ 2 → linear; several degree ≥ 4 nodes + few cycles → clustered; many cycles, degree 4 nodes on an orthogonal lattice, cycles ≥ nodes−1 substantially → grid; one hub with ≥ 3 long linear arms → radial. Validation: the classified organisation must equal the declared parti for ≥ 80 % of floor area.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Symptom of unchosen organisation: a graph that classifies as "none of the five" — typically hub-less spaghetti with many degree-2 dead turns. Grid organisation is the cheapest to compute but the most expensive in travel distance for point-to-point trips (many right-angle turns); centralised is the cheapest in area but fails when the hub saturates (see TH-29 for lift hubs).

### TH-11 Prefer fewer, wider routes; movement speed is a design variable you can measure
- Rule: For the same OD (origin-destination) demand, prefer a small number of generous routes to many narrow ones. Measure the plan's movement cost as total octile steps for the expected OD set, and reject variants that raise it without buying privacy, daylight or area.
- Evidence: On the host grid movement is A\* octile with diagonals only where both corner neighbours are open. Consequence, arithmetic not theory: a 1-tile corridor forces axis-aligned steps, so a 10 m × 10 m crossing costs 40 tile-steps; the same move across open floor costs `20 + 0.41421×20 ≈ 28.3` steps — about 29 % cheaper. Ching's treatment of circulation as an organised system (spaces linked by a common space) supports making the movement element an explicit design object rather than a remainder; space-syntax practice likewise measures routes as graph shortest paths and treats route choice as the mechanism that generates use (Space Syntax Ltd: "an accessible segment is more easily reached than a segregated one because it can be arrived at by simpler routes from other segments"; "A more accessible segment should be more likely to be selected as part of a route between other pairs of segments").
- Source: derived arithmetic from the host grid movement model — FACT; Space Syntax Ltd, *The Space Syntax Approach* https://spacesyntax.com/the-space-syntax-approach/ — T3; Ching 4th ed. — T3.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High; the octile-cost arithmetic behind it is a measured fact of the host movement model, while the preference for fewer, wider routes is the design principle the rule asserts
- Grid translation: Define an OD matrix from the programme (roles × typical trips: arrival→desk, desk→lift, lift→room, room→ amenity, service→back-of-house). Validation metrics: `mean octile steps per trip`, `detour index = octile steps / straight-line tile distance` (≈1.0 in open floor, up to 2.0 in forced L-paths), `corridor tile share` (see TH-15), and `min-cut tiles` on each route (removing which tile disconnects which pairs — a 1-tile corridor always has min-cut 1). Reject any revision where detour index or min-cut fragility worsens without a compensating zoning/daylight win.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Narrow routes are correct where the requirement is control (detention, sterile zones, hospital clean/unclean separation) or where a 1-tile passage is a deliberate pinch (defensible-space thresholds). Symptom of blindly maximising openness: the whole floor becomes one hall, room typing collapses (flood-fill yields one giant untyped room) and every privacy test in TH-06/TH-07 fails.

### TH-12 Size every route from flow, not from habit
- Rule: Compute required route width from occupant load: width = occupant load × egress capacity factor. Use the code factors as the floor of the decision, then add the practical allowances the grid forces. Record the calculation next to the corridor it justifies.
- Evidence: IBC §1005 capacity factors: stairways `0.3 in (7.6 mm) per occupant`, reduced to `0.2 in (5.1 mm) per occupant` where sprinklered; "other egress components" `0.2 in (5.1 mm)` per occupant, reduced to `0.15 in (3.8 mm)` where sprinklered. Minimum corridor/egress widths: 44 in where occupant load > 50, 36 in where ≤ 50; minimum door clear width 32 in; business occupant-load factor ≈ 150 ft² gross per occupant (≈ 14 m²/person).
- Source: IBC §1005 via UpCodes https://up.codes/s/means-of-egress-sizing — T1 (code adoption varies by jurisdiction); occupant-load factor and 32 in door value cross-checked at https://harth.build/resources/design-standards/egress-and-corridor-widths/ — T4.
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT
- Scope: universal (jurisdiction-specific in exact values)
- Confidence: High for flow-based sizing from the cited code factors; Low for the 1.1–1.2 m passing-people figure in the exception (UNCITED — heuristic) — class kept: §1005 does require width to be derived from occupant load, which is the rule's substance; note that CODE-04 could not re-find the 0.2 / 0.15 in-per-occupant reduced tiers at the cited source, so those specific factors are unverified here even though the mechanism is not
- Grid translation: 44 in = 1118 mm ≈ 3 tiles wide; 36 in = 914 mm ≈ 2 tiles; 32 in clear door = 813 mm — which the host's 2-tile (1.0 m) door run satisfies with margin, while a 1-tile (0.5 m) door fails every code. Validation: for each route, `occupants_served = sum(room capacities upstream)`; `required_tiles = ceil(occupants × 5.1 mm ÷ 500 mm)` for a sprinklered level route, `ceil(occupants × 7.6/500)` for a stair/lift-equivalent vertical route; assert `corridor width tiles ≥ max(2, required_tiles)` and `corridor width ≥ 3 tiles` wherever `occupants > 49`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: The code factors are egress (panic) minima, not comfort minima: two people passing shoulder-to-shoulder need roughly 1.1–1.2 m, i.e. 3 tiles, which is `UNCITED — heuristic` (Confidence Low) but is why a 2-tile corridor reads as cramped in simulation even when it passes egress arithmetic. Symptom: a plan that passes TH-12 everywhere yet shows queue spikes at the door runs, because doors are the pinch points even when corridors are wide.

### TH-13 Provide turning and standing space where routes change direction
- Rule: Wherever a route turns, meets another, or serves a wheelchair user, provide a turning space; wherever a user must wait (lift hall, reception, desk), provide standing/clear floor space. Do not let these fall out of leftover corridor width.
- Evidence: 2010 ADA Standards: "The turning space shall be a space of 60 inches (1525 mm) diameter minimum" (§304.3.1); the T-shaped alternative fits "within a 60 inch (1525 mm) square minimum with arms and base 36 inches (915 mm) wide minimum" (§304.3.2); clear floor space for a wheelchair user is "30 inches (760 mm) minimum by 48 inches (1220 mm) minimum" (§305.3).
- Source: 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design, Chapter 3 https://www.access-board.gov/ada/chapter/ch03/ — T1 (US federal; other jurisdictions differ, e.g. 1500 mm turning circles in metric guidance).
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT
- Scope: universal (jurisdiction-specific values)
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: 1525 mm ≈ 3.05 tiles → reserve a 3-tile-diameter (or 3×3 tile) open walkable disc at each route junction and at each lift portal landing; 1220 mm ≈ 2.44 tiles → a 2×3 tile clear patch beside every desk/counters/queue node. Validation: `walkable disc of radius 1.5 tiles free of blocked and door tiles` at every junction with turn angle ≥ 90°; count junctions passing as `turning_space_coverage`, require 100 % on accessible routes.
- Exceptions / failure mode: A 3×3 junction plus door runs at each corner starts to eat the floor plate — this is the real cost of accessibility compliance and must be reported, not hidden (see TH-15's budget). Symptom: junctions that pass the disc test only because a wall tile was deleted, producing a room whose "door" is a hole in the wrong place; or a turning disc overlapping a door swing, which the grid cannot represent as clearance.

### TH-14 Lay the desire lines down before formalising the route grid
- Rule: Before committing the corridor skeleton, draw the straight lines between the programme's high-volume OD pairs, and route the formal grid along them. Deviate only where a zone boundary, daylight frontage or structural module demands it — and record the deviation cost in extra steps.
- Evidence: "Desire line/desire path" is the established planning term for the unmarked straight line people actually walk between origin and destination, and the design practice of paving it rather than fighting it; the concept is documented in transport-planning guidance in the UK street-design tradition (Manual for Streets 2 uses the desire-line concept when locating footways and crossings) and in the general reference. Space-syntax theory predicts route choice the same way: movement concentrates on the segments that offer the simplest routes between many pairs (Space Syntax Ltd quotes in TH-11).
- Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desire_path — T4 (concept/definition only, not elevated); *Manual for Streets 2* https://tsrgd.co.uk/pdf/mfs/mfs2.pdf — T1/T3 (UK government guidance; desire-line concept for footway alignment); Space Syntax Ltd — T3.
- Class: HEURISTIC
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: Medium
- Grid translation: For each OD pair with trips/day above a threshold, compute the Bresenham/octile straight line and mark the tiles it crosses as `desire tiles`. Validation: `desire_coverage = trips on pairs whose desire line lies inside a declared route / total trips` — target ≥ 0.7. Where a desire line crosses through a room, that is a zoning conflict, not a paving instruction: escalate to TH-05 rather than punching a door.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Desire lines ignore privacy gradients — the shortest path from lift to amenity may cross the most private zone, and following it would defeat TH-06/TH-07. Symptom: a measured "efficient" plan whose only fast route threads through a suite, and a user population that in simulation cuts across guest rooms (rooms whose tile-set the A\* path crosses).

### TH-15 Budget the movement share of the floor and never let it overrun
- Rule: Decide, in the parti, what fraction of floor tiles go to movement and vertical cores, then enforce it. Report circulation share as a first-class metric alongside room count and net area.
- Evidence: Circulation is defined as the dedicated movement area — "corridors, aisles between furniture, stairways, elevator lobbies, main entry halls and other dedicated movement zones" — and typical reported office shares are "roughly 12–25 % depending on building type and layout", with 15–25 % cited as the common range (Vantage Space glossary). Tier note: this is professional-community practice data (T4), not a standard; treat the band as an order-of-magnitude check. The wall-thickness effect on this grid adds a further, non-negotiable share (TH-02).
- Source: https://www.vantagespace.com/gb/glossary/capacity-density-space-standards/circulation-area — T4; Time-Saver Standards for Building Types, 2nd ed., McGraw-Hill 2001 (per-type space-planning standards incl. efficiency factors) — T3, no stable URL retrieved; Neufert, *Architects' Data* 3rd English ed. — T3.
- Class: HEURISTIC
- Scope: universal (per-type targets differ)
- Confidence: Medium for the method, Low for the exact percentages (T4 source)
- Grid translation: Classify tiles: `movement` = walkable tiles inside rooms tagged as corridor/lobby/hall/lift-hall (i.e. rooms with no fixtures other than vertical-portal or seating), `usable` = walkable tiles inside programme rooms, `structure` = blocked. Validation: `movement_share = movement tiles / floor tiles` within a declared band (target 0.10–0.20 for repeatable-cell types, 0.15–0.25 where public gathering is the product); assert `movement_share` does not grow across revisions unless the reason is a logged zoning or accessibility win.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Open-plan floors legitimately push usable into the movement category (the hall *is* the space); then the metric must switch to "tiles in rooms with a declared function". Symptom: a plan whose circulation share reads 6 % — suspiciously efficient on this grid, and almost always means 1-tile corridors were used, which fails TH-12/TH-13 and blows up queue cost.

### TH-16 State the parti in one sentence and one diagram before placing tiles
- Rule: Produce a parti: a single sentence naming the organising idea plus a minimal diagram (organisation form from TH-10, the zone bands from TH-06, the movement skeleton). Every later revision must be re-derivable from it; if a revision cannot be drawn as the parti, it is a new parti and must be declared.
- Evidence: The parti pris is the standard device for holding a scheme's organising idea constant while its parts change; it is inseparable from Ching's organisation-of-spaces forms (centralised/linear/radial/clustered/grid, TH-10) and from the block/schematic diagrams generated out of the adjacency matrix in programming practice (Gustin T4; adjacency-matrix + bubble-diagram pair, ResearchGate T2 figure). "Parti" here is used as the design-method term, not a style claim.
- Source: Ching, *Architecture: Form, Space, and Order*, 4th ed. — T3; Colin Rowe & Fred Koetter, *Collage City*, MIT Press 1978 (figure/ground reading of urban fabric; parti/collage method) — T3, no stable URL retrieved; Gustin — T4.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: Medium
- Grid translation: Store the parti as data, not prose: `{organisation: "radial", zones: [...], spines: [...], core_position: tile, aspect_strategy: "double-frontage"}`. Validation: re-derive TH-10 classification, TH-06 contiguity, TH-11 mean cost from the plan and diff against the stored parti; report a `parti drift` score = number of declared properties the built plan violates. A drift > 0 with no logged reason is a failed revision.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Symptom: an agent that "improves" the plan locally until the movement graph no longer matches any named organisation and the zone bands fragment into several flood-fill regions — usually after fixing one adjacency conflict with lever 3 (distance).

### TH-17 Make importance visible in area, position and connectivity
- Rule: Establish a spatial hierarchy: the most important space must be the most prominent on at least two of three measurable axes — floor area, centrality (graph distance from entry), and connectivity (number of direct links). No ties may be resolved by default.
- Evidence: Hierarchy is a standard organising device in Ching's account of ordered space (the dominant/subordinate relationship among spaces, *Architecture: Form, Space, and Order*, 4th ed. — T3); space syntax supplies computable proxies for the same intuition: `connectivity` = number of immediate neighbours of a space in the graph, and integration = mean depth from a space to all others, with the standard formalisation being mean depth normalised for graph size (an axiomatic treatment gives node connectivity as `Connectivity(i) = deg(i) = Σ_j A_ij`, mean depth `ℓ_i = D_i/(N−1)`, and a normalised real-relative-asymmetry `RRA(i) = 2(ℓ_i − 1)/(D_N(N−2))`).
- Source: Ching 4th ed. — T3; arXiv preprint "Scaling and Universality in City Space Syntax" https://arxiv.org/html/0709.4375v1 — T2 (formulas as reproduced there); Hillier & Hanson, *The Social Logic of Space*, Polity 1984 — T3.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal (strongest in civic, hospitality, healthcare, education)
- Confidence: Medium
- Grid translation: For each room node compute `area_tiles`, `depth = A* steps from arrival tile`, `degree = distinct rooms joined by a door run`. Validation: assert `rank(area)`, `rank(−depth)`, `rank(degree)` agree for the declared primary space in ≥ 2 of 3; assert a monotone decrease of mean room area with depth for repeatable-cell types.
- Exceptions / failure mode: On this grid, `area` is a weak signal because walls inflate small rooms (TH-02) — compare interior usable tiles, never footprints. Symptom: the largest room is the lift hall or an untagged void, and the "primary" space is subordinate by accident. Also: some programmes want a deliberately un-hierarchical plan (open office, market hall); then declare hierarchy as "flat" and drop the check.

### TH-18 Read the plan as figure-ground before reading it as rooms
- Rule: Print the floor as solid (blocked) against void (walkable) and judge it: the solid should form coherent edges and the void should form a legible continuous figure. If the solid reads as noise, the wall layout is wrong regardless of how each room tests individually.
- Evidence: Figure-ground reading is the standard analytical device for legibility of built fabric (Rowe & Koetter, *Collage City*, MIT Press 1978 — T3; the method is universally used in urban-design teaching, e.g. the Lynch-derived material listed in TH-09). On this grid the reading is literal: blocked tiles are the figure, walkable tiles the ground, and a wall is one tile thick, so solid coherence is directly measurable.
- Source: Colin Rowe & Fred Koetter, *Collage City*, MIT Press 1978 — T3 (no stable URL retrieved); Lynch teaching PDF https://cus.ubt-uni.net/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Kevin-Lynch-The-Image-of-the-City.pdf — T2.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: Medium
- Grid translation: Compute solid metrics: `solid_share = blocked / total`; `solid_components` = number of flood-fill groups of blocked tiles (a coherent plan has few, and one should carry the boundary); `mean_component_size`; `floating_walls` = blocked components of ≤ 2 tiles (orphan stubs); `perimeter_ratio` of the largest solid body (compactness). Validation: `solid_share` typically 0.25–0.40 on a cell-repeat floor (arithmetic consequence of TH-02 — `UNCITED — heuristic`); `floating_walls = 0`; `solid_components ≤ 1 + number of intentionally detached cores`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Detached solids are legitimate (a core, a pavilion, a free-standing fixture wall). Symptom: hundreds of one-tile stubs — the classic sign of an agent walling tile-by-tile instead of drawing room envelopes, which also makes rooms un-floodfillable because door runs are placed as isolated gaps.

### TH-19 Design decision points so the plan can replace signage
- Rule: At every place where the route could continue in more than one direction, make the correct choice physically obvious — one branch must be visibly the main one. Signage is the backup for a plan that failed this test; count the signs you would need as a penalty score.
- Evidence: Lynch's five elements — paths, edges, districts, nodes, landmarks — are precisely the physical cues that make a layout traversable without instruction, and "imageability" is the property he optimises (Lynch 1960; see TH-09 URLs). Passini formalised wayfinding as a sequence of decisions (choosing a route, orienting oneself, and deciding to continue), and argued that spatial configuration carries the information that signs otherwise must supply — *Wayfinding in Architecture*, Van Nostrand Reinhold 1984, and *Wayfinding: People, Signs and Architecture* (with Cleemenses; 1992). Peer-reviewed summaries of the field treat wayfinding as information processing under uncertainty rather than sign-reading (Itard & O'Neill, "Wayfinding: a simple concept, a complex process", *Environment and Behavior* 2001; university lecture note DR-14, SUNY Buffalo IDEA).
- Source: Lynch 1960 — T3 (teaching PDF, TH-09); Paul Passini, *Wayfinding in Architecture*, 1984 — T3, catalogue record https://searchworks.stanford.edu/view/2376159 — T4; *Wayfinding: People, Signs and Architecture* https://searchworks.stanford.edu/view/2376159 — T3/T4; SUNY Buffalo IDEA lecture note DR-14 http://idea.ap.buffalo.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/110/2019-08-14.pdf — T2.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal (healthcare, hospitality, education, transit, detention strongly)
- Confidence: High for legibility, Medium for the specific decision taxonomy (Passini's decision names could not be retrieved verbatim this pass — treat "route / orientation / continue" as reported-at-second-hand, Confidence Low)
- Grid translation: Extract decision points: walkable tiles with ≥ 3 open neighbour directions, merged into junction nodes. For each, compute the *branch asymmetry* = difference in downstream integration/degree between the strongest and next-strongest branch. Validation: require `branch_asymmetry > 0` at every junction (one branch clearly dominant); require that a straight continuation exists from the arrival axis at the first junction after each lift portal; report `estimated_signs_needed = #junctions with asymmetry == 0` and drive it to zero.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Symmetric junctions are not always wrong (a deliberately bilateral plan, or a queueing manifold). Symptom: the whole floor at `asymmetry = 0` — a grid of identical crossroads, which is the classic maze failure and will also show up as a high detour index (TH-11) and flat hierarchy (TH-17).

### TH-20 Compute integration and put the public rooms at the integrated end
- Rule: Rank all rooms by how easily they are reached from everything else and place public/retail/amenity at the top of the ranking and private/service at the bottom. Do not rely on intuition about "feeling central".
- Evidence: Space syntax defines the operational claim that accessibility, not decoration, produces publicness: "an accessible segment is more easily reached than a segregated one because it can be arrived at by simpler routes from other segments" and "a more accessible segment should be more likely to be selected as part of a route between other pairs of segments" (Space Syntax Ltd, *The Space Syntax Approach*). The theoretical basis is Hillier & Hanson's argument that spatial configuration constrains co-presence, and the empirical programme that correlates integration with observed movement ("the movement economy", Hillier 1996, *Space is the Machine*). Measures: integration = reciprocal of mean topological depth from a space to all others, normalised for graph size; connectivity = degree in the space graph; representations are convex/axial/segment maps.
- Source: Space Syntax Ltd https://spacesyntax.com/the-space-syntax-approach/ — T3; Hillier & Hanson, *The Social Logic of Space*, Polity 1984 — T3; Hillier, *Space is the Machine*, Cambridge UP 1996 — T3; formulae as reproduced in https://arxiv.org/html/0709.4375v1 — T2; synopsis article "Bill Hillier's Legacy: Space Syntax — A Synopsis of Basic Concepts, Measures and Empirical Application", *Sustainability* 13(6):3394, 2021 https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/13/6/3394 — T2 (full text not retrievable this pass; cited by title).
- Class: HEURISTIC (the predictive part is contested — see ## Weak or contested)
- Scope: universal; strongest evidence in urban street grids and retail
- Confidence: High that the measure is computable and monotone with publicness by construction; Low-Medium that it *predicts* actual behaviour in an interior floor plate
- Grid translation: Build the room graph (nodes = flood-fill rooms; edges = door runs; weight = 1). Compute `degree` (connectivity) and `mean_depth = Σ shortest-path hops / (N−1)`; `integration = 1/mean_depth` (report raw and rank, not the normalised RRA, since the graph is small). Validation: `spearman(zone_publicness_rank, integration_rank) ≥ 0.6`; `max integration` belongs to a room tagged public; every room tagged `service` sits in the bottom third by integration.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Interior floors are small graphs, so mean depth is noisy and one door can invert a ranking; integration also rewards a room for being a thoroughfare, which is exactly what a private suite must not be. Symptom: the highest-integration room is a back-of-house corridor because it has 6 doors; or retail placed at the integrated end but 40 tiles from the arrival door, so the public never finds it (integration without a visible spine — combine with TH-19).

### TH-21 Design clearances for the large user and reaches for the small user
- Rule: Never dimension a clearance to the average body. Set passages, door widths, turning spaces and ceiling-independent clearances from a large-person value, and set handles, controls, shelves and reachable fixtures from a small-person value. Where both apply in one room, dimension both and let the larger envelope govern.
- Evidence: The design-to-extremes principle is the standard content of anthropometric guidance in ergonomics: design values are chosen from a percentile of the relevant population, with high percentiles governing clearances (space to pass) and low percentiles governing reaches and grasps; see HFES, *Guidelines for Using Anthropometric Data* (document chapters include "Defining the Target Population" and "Anthropometry in Design: Examples and Summary"), Pheasant & Haslegrave, *Bodily Dimensions: Ergonomics for Design*, 3rd ed., Taylor & Francis 2006, and ISO 7250-1:2017 *Basic human body measurements for workplace design* (T1). Caveat: the specific "95th percentile male / 5th percentile female" pairing could not be verified from retrievable primary text in this pass — it is reported as convention, `UNCITED — heuristic`, Confidence Low. What *is* verified is the numeric floor set by codes (TH-12/TH-13): 32 in door clear width, 30×48 in (760×1220 mm) clear floor space, 60 in (1525 mm) turning circle.
- Source: HFES guidelines PDF https://www.hfes.org/Portals/0/Publications/Guidelines_AnthropometricData.pdf — T2/T3; ISO 7250-1:2017 (T1); Pheasant & Haslegrave 3rd ed., 2006 (T3); 2010 ADA Standards ch. 3 https://www.access-board.gov/ada/chapter/ch03/ — T1.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE (the design-to-extremes strategy)
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High for the asymmetry principle and the code numbers; Low for named percentiles; the class is code-derived for the embedded clearance minima (ADA ch.3, ISO 7250-1), while the design-to-extremes percentile strategy itself is a heuristic no code prescribes — headline class was `CODE REQUIREMENT`; demoted: the Evidence says outright that the strategy is "reported as convention, UNCITED — heuristic", so code strength belongs only to the numeric minima the rule applies [classification: the rule also carries CODE REQUIREMENT for its part of the claim - the field holds one label per spec §27]
- Grid translation: Encode two constants: `clearance_min_tiles = 2` (1.0 m ≥ 32 in door) for any passage a body must fit through, and `turning_tiles = 3` for any change of direction. For reach-type fixtures (counter, control, storage), require `1 ≤ distance from a standable tile ≤ 2` tiles, i.e. within 1.0 m of an unobstructed walkable tile. Validation: sweep every fixture tile and assert it has a standable tile within the reach radius and that the standing tile itself is not in another room's required clearance.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Some rooms must be sized to the *equipment*, not the person (beds, trolleys, gym machines, laundry): use the fixture footprint plus its maintenance clearance; on this grid a fixture occupies whole tiles, so a 2 m bed = 4 tiles long and cannot be positioned off-grid. Symptom: fixtures whose only adjacent tile is a door tile, or a counter reachable only from inside a wall (no standable tile in radius).

### TH-22 Treat the 0.5 m tile as a modular-coordination grid and design to it
- Rule: Fix one coordination module for the whole job and place every dimension — room widths, door runs, core positions, fixture footprints — on multiples of it. Do not mix two modules in one floor.
- Evidence: Modular coordination in building is standardised: the ISO series on the subject takes a basic module M of 100 mm with coordination modules as multiples of it (ISO 1006:1983 *Modular coordination — Basic module*; ISO 2848:1984 *Modular coordination — Principles and rules*; both T1). Building planning conventionally uses larger coordination steps in the 300–600 mm range for dimensional coordination of components and grids; a 500 mm tile is a non-standard but perfectly workable module as long as everything is on it. The alternative tradition — a proportion module derived from the human body (Le Corbusier's *Modulor*, 1950–55) — is documented with these numbers: a "Greek man" of 1.75 m in the first version and 1.829 m in the second, a navel height of 113 cm giving the red series (113, 70, 43, 27 cm), an outstretched-arm height of 226 cm giving the blue series (226, 140, 86, 43 cm), with derived room heights such as 2,260 mm and the series built on the golden section / Fibonacci.
- Source: ISO 1006:1983 https://www.iso.org/standard/5470.html — T1; ISO 2848:1984 sample https://cdn.standards.iteh.ai/samples/7846/a86382c91b96433da03b947608e0c2d0/ISO-2848-1984.pdf — T1; Le Corbusier, *Le Modulor* — T3, numbers from the official Fondation Le Corbusier-licensed journal page https://dz.lescouleurs.ch/en/journal/posts/the-modulor-human-closeness-as-a-basic-value/ — T4, and scholarly discussion in "Exploring the Panel Exercises in the Modulor", *Journal of Open Image History* (Wiley OA) https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2475-8876.12147 — T2.
- Class: FACT
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High for "use one module"; Medium for the ISO numbers (standard titles verified, body text not retrieved); Low for Modulor's practical usefulness; the ISO modular-coordination citations are voluntary standard practice, not a code requirement, so the class rests on the grid fact (one declared module) the discipline depends on
- Grid translation: Module = 1 tile = 500 mm; declare it once. Validation: assert every room's interior dimensions and every door-run position are integer tile multiples — automatically true on this grid, so instead test the *consequences*: (a) fixture footprints must be integer tiles; (b) the core/portal must sit on a declared module intersection shared by all floors (stacking alignment); (c) room sizes drawn from a small set of module combinations (see TH-23). Note explicitly: 500 mm is *not* an ISO coordination size (ISO steps are 100-based); the simulation therefore cannot claim ISO compliance — label the plan "500 mm tile module", not "metric modular coordination".
- Exceptions / failure mode: Long-span and free-plan layouts legitimately break the module (curves, ramps, columns on a different grid) — but each break must be paid for with a named exception. Symptom: a fixture that occupies 2.5 tiles and therefore cannot be placed, or two floors whose lift portals are offset by one tile because each floor invented its own grid.

### TH-23 Design a kit of repeatable room types instead of inventing rooms
- Rule: Derive a small set of standard room types (each with fixed footprint, fixture set, door position and zone) and assemble floors by repeating them. Target ≥ 70 % of rooms belonging to ≤ 6 types. Innovation is spent on the joins between types, not on the types themselves.
- Evidence: Standardisation and repetition are core organising devices in architectural composition (repetition/rhythm as an ordering principle: Ching, *Architecture: Form, Space, and Order*, 4th ed. — T3), and the industrial-logic version is Open Building: separate the durable, collective "support" from the adaptable "infill", i.e. "walls and partitions that can be easily adapted", so that units are repeatable and the frame is not re-invented (Habraken's support/infill concept, introduced 1961, developed in *Supports: An Alternative to Mass Housing*, 1972; RE-DWELL definition of Open Building and the Open Building Institute legacy pages).
- Source: Open Building Institute, *Legacy* https://www.openbuilding.co/legacy — T3; RE-DWELL, *Open Building — definition* https://www.re-dwell.eu/concept-definition/58 — T3/T4; "Modularity in transition: reconciling open building principles with early design education", *Open House International* 51(1) https://www.emerald.com/ohi/article/51/1/176/1305084/Modularity-in-transition-reconciling-open-building — T2; Ching 4th ed. — T3.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: residential, hospitality, healthcare, education, detention, office
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: Define room types as tile templates: `W×H outer footprint`, `door run offset`, `fixture tag set`, `zone`. Validation: `type_coverage = rooms matching a template / total rooms ≥ 0.7`; `unique_types ≤ 6`; identical types must produce identical interior tile counts (a mismatch means a wall was drawn in the wrong place). Cross-check each template against TH-24 minima and TH-02 usable area.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Civic and hospitality public rooms are one-offs by nature; exempt them explicitly and cap exempt area at the programme's public share. Symptom: 25 room types for 40 rooms — every "room" is bespoke, the plan cannot be extended, and the vertical core cannot align because each floor's party walls differ.

### TH-24 Respect published minimum dimensions per room type
- Rule: Where a jurisdiction or a recognised standard publishes minimum areas/dimensions for a room type, use them as hard floors and cite which one was used. Where none exists for the type, derive a minimum from the largest fixture plus its clearance and record it as derived, not as a standard.
- Evidence: England's *Technical housing standards – nationally described space standard* (2015) gives, in its room-size table: "a single bedroom has a floor area of at least 7.5m2 and is at least 2.15m wide", a double bedroom "at least 11.5m2"; its floor-area table for dwellings runs from 39 m² for a one-bed/one-storey dwelling to 138 m² for a six-bed/three-storey dwelling, with built-in storage allowance up to 4.0 m²; and "minimum floor to ceiling height is 2.3m for at least 75% of the Gross Internal Area", while noting the 2.3 m figure "will not be adequate for wheelchair housing".
- Source: UK Government (MHCLG/DCLG), *Technical housing standards – nationally described space standard*, 2015 https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/technical-housing-standards-nationally-described-space-standard/technical-housing-standards-nationally-described-space-standard — T1 (England; guidance/adoption is jurisdiction-specific).
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT (scope: residential, England)
- Scope: residential
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: Residential table in tiles (usable, interior): 7.5 m² = 30 interior tiles (e.g. 6×5 outer → 4×3 = 12 tiles — far short, so a compliant "double" needs roughly 8×7 outer tiles → 6×5 = 30 interior tiles = 7.5 m²); 11.5 m² = 46 interior tiles; min width 2.15 m = 5 interior tiles (so ≥ 7 outer). Validation: assert each residential room type meets its tiles and width; report `headroom` as out-of-model (the host has no section) — record the 2.3 m rule as an unverifiable-by-tiles constraint so it is never silently claimed as satisfied.
- Exceptions / failure mode: The 0.5 m grid quantises badly against metric minima: 2.15 m is not a tile multiple, so the compliant answer is the *next* multiple up (5 interior tiles = 2.5 m), which over-builds by 16 %. Symptom: an agent that reports "2.15 m achieved" from a 4-tile interior (2.0 m) — always round up, never to nearest. Non-residential types (hotel, hospital ward, classroom) have no equivalent global minimum here; use the type's fixture envelope and label it derived.

### TH-25 Bound room proportions so the room is usable, not merely large enough
- Rule: After area and minimum width pass, test the proportion: reject rooms whose length:width ratio is extreme for their function, and reject long thin rooms with no route function to justify them.
- Evidence: Proportional limits on rooms are standard interior-planning advice in the dimension-driven references (Neufert, *Architects' Data*, 3rd English ed. — T3; Time-Saver Standards for Building Types, 2nd ed. — T3; Panero & Zelnik, *Human Dimensions and Interior Space*, Watson-Guptill 1979 — T3), none of which could be quoted verbatim this pass; the concrete numeric anchors verified in this file are the NDSS minimum width-to-area pairing (TH-24: 7.5 m² *and* 2.15 m wide, i.e. area alone is not accepted as adequate) and the reach/clearance geometry of TH-21. Practical consequence, arithmetic: a room of fixed area whose ratio grows loses usable floor to circulation (a 30-tile room at 10×3 interior costs a walk of 8 tiles to reach the far corner, and cannot fit a 4×5 bed without blocking its own door).
- Source: NDSS room-size table https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/technical-housing-standards-nationally-described-space-standard/technical-housing-standards-nationally-described-space-standard — T1 (evidence that width is regulated alongside area); Neufert / Time-Saver / Panero & Zelnik — T3, cited by title/edition without quoting numbers.
- Class: HEURISTIC
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: Low for numeric ratios; High for the requirement to test ratio at all [classification note: the specific ratio limits are `UNCITED — heuristic`]
- Grid translation: Compute `interior_ratio = max(W,H)/min(W,H)` on interior tiles. Validation: `ratio ≤ 3` for sleeping/ward/treatment rooms, `≤ 2.5` for living/dining/consultation, `≤ 4` for corridors and circulation rooms (that is their purpose), `≤ 3.5` for offices and classrooms; plus: assert every room's fixtures fit inside its interior rectangle in one orientation *and* leave a standable tile of ≥2 tiles' approach to each fixture (TH-21). Suggested working band: aim for 1.2–1.6 for primary rooms — this band is a heuristic, not a citation.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Galleries, service runs, ward-roof terraces and security corridors are legitimately extreme; exempt by tag. Symptom: an area-compliant room with ratio 6 where the bed occupies the entire short side and the door opens into the bed — the classic "correct on paper, unusable in place" plan.

### TH-26 Cap single-aspect depth by the daylight rule, and measure daylight in tiles
- Rule: Every room intended for long occupancy must lie within daylight reach of a façade. Set a depth cap from the assumed floor-to-floor height, and validate per tile, not per room. Where the cap cannot be met, the room must be reclassified as interior (corridor, store, plant, circulation) — not left tagged as habitable.
- Evidence: BS EN 17037:2018 *Daylight in buildings* sets daylight targets as illuminance on a reference plane: a "median target illuminance ... of at least 300 lx over 50 % of the reference plane" with "a minimum threshold illuminance of 100 lx across 95 % of the space", assessed for "more than half of the daylight hours in the year (2190 h)", with the working plane at 0.85 m above the floor; the "1 m wide strip around the perimeter" exclusion in the UK version is a national deviation, not part of the European standard. Mean daylight factor is deprecated in EN 17037 in favour of these lux-based metrics (Anstey Horne). Sidelit "daylight zone" definitions in energy codes bound the useful depth directly: depth "up to 1.0 times the height from the floor to the top of the fenestration", with the zone extending past the opening by "up to 2 feet (610 mm)" in the retrieved code text. The widely used planner's heuristic that single-aspect depth ≈ 1.5 × floor-to-floor height and double-aspect ≈ 2.5 × could NOT be traced to a retrievable primary source in this pass — report as `UNCITED — heuristic`, Confidence Low.
- Source: BS EN 17037:2018 (T1) — catalogue https://standards.iteh.ai/catalog/standards/cen/836e5b91-1eb0-4643-a2ba-7ca5a5988e64/en-17037-2018 — T1; numbers as reported in the peer-reviewed/open-access practice study https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10773465/ — T2; Anstey Horne, *Daylight Factors and BS EN 17037* https://www.ansteyhorne.co.uk/news/daylight-factors-and-bs-en-17037-understanding-internal-daylighting-standards — T3; code daylight zone via UpCodes https://up.codes/s/sidelight-daylight-zone — T1.
- Class: STANDARD (the read EN 17037 targets)
- Scope: universal (residential, education, healthcare, office most sensitive)
- Confidence: High for the EN 17037 lux targets and the 1.0×-glazing-head daylight-zone rule; Low for the 1.5×/2.5× floor-to-floor multipliers; the classification is code-derived only for the EN 17037 lux targets and the 1.0x-glazing-head daylight zone - the 1.5x/2.5x floor-to-floor multipliers stay heuristics — headline class was `CODE REQUIREMENT`; demoted to `STANDARD`: EN 17037 is a daylight standard, not an adopted code duty in the sources read, and the plan-depth cap is this file's arithmetic translation of its lux targets [classification: the rule also carries HEURISTIC for its part of the claim - the field holds one label per spec §27]
- Grid translation: The host has no window tile and no heights, so daylight must be modelled as a proxy: `façade tile` = blocked tile with a walkable tile on one side and outside/void on the other; `daylit tiles` = interior tiles within `D` tiles of a façade tile carrying glazing. With a declared assumption of 3.0 m floor-to-floor and glazing head at 2.1 m, the code-derived single-aspect depth is 2.1 m ≈ 4 tiles — very strict; the planner's 1.5 × floor-to-floor gives 4.5 m ≈ 9 tiles. Validate the middle: cap single-aspect depth at 8 tiles and require `daylit_share ≥ 0.6` of a habitable room's interior tiles; below that, re-tag the room. Report the assumed heights explicitly whenever this check is used, since the model itself carries none.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Atrium/interior-facing rooms, basements, and high-heat service rooms legitimately have no daylight — but the check must still record them as unlit so amenity counting (TH-27) does not credit them. Symptom: a floor plate 16 tiles deep with every room tagged habitable; or "daylight" credited to a room whose only façade adjacency is through a corridor's wall, i.e. the light never reaches the room's tiles.

### TH-27 Give long-occupancy rooms light from two sides where the plan can afford it
- Rule: Where two or more façade directions are reachable for a room, prefer two-sided lighting over one-sided, and record which rooms were denied it and why. Treat two-sided rooms as the quality differentiator and price them accordingly in the programme.
- Evidence: Christopher Alexander, Shara Ishikawa & Murray Silverstein, *A Pattern Language: Towns, Buildings, Construction*, Oxford University Press, 1977 — Pattern 159, "Light on Two Sides of Every Room" (verified pattern number and title; the retrieved text is qualitative: "When they have a choice, people will always gravitate to those rooms which have light on two sides, and leave the rooms..." which are lit from one side; the pattern gives no numeric depth or glazing ratios, and it cross-refers to patterns 106, 107, 109, 116, 180, 192, 209, 221, 223, 238). Note for the skill: pattern 146 is *not* this pattern (146 is a different pattern) — do not cite "146" for two-sided light.
- Source: https://patternlanguage.cc/Patterns/Light-on-Two-Sides-of-Every-Room-(159) — T3 (pattern text); Alexander et al. 1977, Oxford UP — T3.
- Class: EXPERIENCE-BASED ADVICE (Alexander's patterns are observation-based, not measured)
- Scope: universal; residential, education, healthcare, office
- Confidence: Medium
- Grid translation: For each habitable room compute `façade_directions` = distinct compass directions of its façade tiles. Validation: `two_aspect_share` over long-occupancy rooms (sleep, living, ward, classroom, work) — target ≥ 0.5 on a perimeter-resolvable floor; for every long-occupancy room with `façade_directions == 1` while the floor plate allows 2, emit a logged veto-able decision. Two-aspect also relaxes TH-26's cap: allow depth up to 12 tiles when `façade_directions ≥ 2`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Deep floor plates and site-boundary constraints make two aspects impossible without double-loaded corridors; the failure to watch for is buying two-aspect for rooms where it is worthless (store, plant, lift hall) while a bedroom stays single-aspect — check that `two_aspect_share` is computed over long-occupancy rooms only.

### TH-28 Separate what changes often from what must not
- Rule: Split the design into long-life decisions (core position, primary wall lines, service risers, module) and short-life decisions (partitions, fixture layout, room assignment). Only short-life decisions may be revised freely; a revision that moves a long-life element must be declared as a scheme change.
- Evidence: Stewart Brand's shearing-layer model: buildings are made of layers of differing longevity, named "Site, Structure, Skin, Services, Space Plan and Stuff", each with "different longevity and impact" — cited by the TU Delft OCW course page to Brand's *How Buildings Learn: What Happens After They're Built* (1994, Penguin). The same argument in building-production form is Open Building's support/infill separation (Habraken, from 1961; RE-DWELL/Open Building Institute definitions), where the support is the collective, durable resource and infill is the "walls and partitions that can be easily adapted". (Brand's specific per-layer half-life figures could not be verified this pass — do not quote numbers for them.)
- Source: TU Delft OCW, *3.1.3 Building Layers* https://ocw.tudelft.nl/course-lectures/3-1-3-building-layers/ — T2; Stewart Brand, *How Buildings Learn*, 1994 — T3; Open Building Institute https://www.openbuilding.co/legacy — T3; RE-DWELL https://www.re-dwell.eu/concept-definition/58 — T3/T4; "Shearing Layers of Space", *Urban Planning* (Cogitatio) https://www.cogitatiopress.com/urbanplanning/article/viewFile/9332/4320 — T2.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: Tag every wall line as `carrier` (long-life: aligns across floors with the core and with the primary module intersections, and is present on every floor) or `partition` (short-life, may differ per floor). Validation: (a) `core_tiles` identical across all floors (the lift portals must stack); (b) `carrier` tile set identical or superset-consistent across floors; (c) a "fit-out revision" diff must show changes only inside `partition` tiles and fixture tags — if the diff touches a carrier or portal, require an explicit declaration. Report `change_cost = tiles that must be rebuilt to swap room type A for room type B`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Single-floor buildings and temporary/relocatable types legitimately have no cross-floor alignment obligation. Symptom: two floors whose "identical" cell wings differ by one tile, so no vertical service or portal can be aligned — which on this grid silently breaks the lift model and produces a permanent queue at TH-29.

### TH-29 Size the vertical core against peak demand, because stacks are not free
- Rule: Every inter-floor move is a lift trip plus a queue, so the vertical core is a capacity object, not a plan detail. Count the served population per floor, choose the number of portals, and check the peak-period queue.
- Evidence: Lift traffic practice quantifies service with two headline measures: five-minute handling capacity (percentage of the building population carried in five minutes) and average waiting time in seconds; published quality-of-service bands exist in CIBSE Guide E and in the CIBSE Lifts Group's guidance on traffic analysis and simulation. Verified-in-this-pass content: the measures and their definitions (AdSimulo University lift-traffic primer; Peters Research-hosted CIBSE Lifts Group report). Not verified: the numeric second/percentage bands — reported here as `UNCITED — heuristic`, Confidence Low, so the skill must not claim a specific target without consulting Guide E. Egress sizing from IBC §1005 uses `0.3 in (7.6 mm) per occupant` for stairways, `0.2 in (5.1 mm)` when sprinklered — the same occupant-load logic applies to a portal's share of the flow.
- Source: CIBSE Lifts Group, *Lift (Elevator) Traffic Analysis and Simulation* report https://download.peters-research.com/library/CIBSE_Traffic_Analysis_and_Simulation_Open_Forum_Report.pdf — T3; AdSimulo University, *Basics of Lift Traffic Analysis* https://adsimulo.com/support/adsimulo-university/basics-of-lift-traffic-analysis/ — T4; IBC §1005 via https://up.codes/s/means-of-egress-sizing — T1.
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: commercial, hospitality, healthcare, residential mid/high-rise, civic
- Confidence: Medium for the method; Low for numeric targets; the queue-versus-portal capacity relation is the constraint carrying the class, while the handling-capacity and waiting-time targets are heuristics (not retrieved this pass)
- Grid translation: For each floor compute `served_population = Σ room capacities`, and `portal_demand = inter-floor trips/day` from the OD matrix. Validation: (a) declare `portals_per_floor` and `floor_served_tiles` (the lift hall); (b) require a turning/approach space at each portal (TH-13) sized for `queue_tiles ≥ ceil(peak arrivals × 1.2)` tiles, where peak arrivals is a declared assumption; (c) assert `population per portal` is within a declared band and grows no worse across revisions; (d) if any floor's private rooms are reachable only via a portal, its privacy depth (TH-07) must be recomputed including that portal's control door.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Low-rise or horizontally-spread types need no core and the check is void — declare `single_floor = true`. Symptom: an agent that used TH-05 lever 4 ("stack it") to solve two adjacency conflicts and now routes 400 occupants through one portal: manifests as a rising average wait, a queue region overlapping the lobby, and the lobby's integration ranking jumping (TH-20) for the wrong reason.

## Method vocabulary

- **Programme (pre-design brief)**: the table of users, activities, areas, capacities, equipment and required relationships that a plan must satisfy; the artefact a design is graded against.
- **Programming matrix**: square table of spaces against space attributes plus a diagonal-read grid of pairwise relationships; the input to the adjacency diagram.
- **Adjacency diagram / bubble diagram**: nodes for spaces, links for required relationships; the first spatial form of a plan, drawn before geometry.
- **Closeness rating**: graded strength of a required relationship (must-touch to must-not-touch); on this grid, integers 4→0.
- **Zone / territory**: a set of rooms with the same publicness rank, expected to be contiguous and legibly bounded (public / semi-public / private / service).
- **Privacy gradient / threshold**: the ordered sequence of controlled boundaries between arrival and the most private space; measured in doors, not metres.
- **Parti (parti pris)**: one sentence plus one diagram naming the organising idea — organisation form, zone bands, movement skeleton, core position.
- **Organisation form**: Ching's five forms of spatial organisation — centralised, linear, radial, clustered, grid — the menu for a movement skeleton.
- **Figure-ground**: the reading of a plan as solid (blocked tiles) against void (walkable tiles), judged for coherence of edges and continuity of the void figure.
- **Desire line**: the straight conceptual line between an origin and a destination; the test a formal route grid should not violate without cause.
- **OD pair / trip table**: a demand pair (origin room, destination room) with an expected frequency; the input to movement-cost and core-capacity checks.
- **Detour index**: octile A\* step cost between two points divided by their straight-line tile distance; ~1.0 in open floor, ~2.0 in forced L-paths.
- **Connectivity**: number of immediate neighbours a space has in the room graph (its degree).
- **Integration / segregation**: how few steps a space is from all other spaces in the graph (high integration = easy to reach = public by configuration); segregation is its inverse.
- **Support / infill (carrier / partition)**: the durable, collective frame versus the easily adapted fill; on this grid, tile lines that must repeat across floors versus those that may not.
- **Shearing layers**: Brand's six layers of change — site, structure, skin, services, space plan, stuff — ordered by longevity; the argument for designing the long-life elements to be slow.
- **Net-to-gross / circulation share**: usable area over total floor area, and the share of a floor given to movement and cores; on this grid also the wall tax.
- **Daylight zone / daylit share**: the floor area within reach of a façade, expressed in tiles from a glazed façade tile; the reason room depth is bounded.
- **Kit of parts / room type**: a small set of standardised room templates (footprint, fixtures, door offset, zone) that floors are assembled from.
- **Handling capacity / average waiting time**: the two lift-traffic measures that convert "stacked floors" into a queue problem at a portal.

- **Ching's "five circulation organisations (frontal, linear, radial, circular, grid, complex)" — as given in the brief, this is wrong.** The verified list from *Architecture: Form, Space, and Order* (4th ed., 2014) is five *forms of organisation of spaces*: centralised, linear, radial, clustered, grid. "Frontal" and "circular" do not appear in that list; do not attribute them to Ching.
- **Room depth = 1.5 × floor-to-floor (single aspect) / 2.5 × (double aspect).** Repeated everywhere in UK practice, but no primary source could be retrieved: the retrievable, code-anchored alternative is the EN 17037 lux-based target set (300 lx median over ≥50 % of the plane, 100 lx over ≥95 %, ≥50 % of daylight hours ≈ 2190 h, plane at 0.85 m) and the ASHRAE/energy-code sidelight daylight zone at ~1.0 × the height to the head of the fenestration. Treat 1.5×/2.5× as `UNCITED — heuristic`, Confidence Low. The code-derived daylight zone is stricter than the planner's heuristic, which is itself a warning about which number to trust.
- **Space syntax's predictive power.** The measures (connectivity, mean depth, integration) are computable and well defined; the *claim* that integration predicts movement, commercial viability or crime is contested — critics point to method-dependence (how convex spaces and axial lines are hand-drawn changes the result), correlational rather than causal evidence, and the difference between street-network graphs and interior room graphs. Use integration as a *ranking and self-critique tool* ("is my public room actually the most reachable?"), never as a forecast of footfall. See the synopsis article in *Sustainability* 13(6):3394 (2021) for the state of the method and "Limitations, critiques and inconsistencies of the space syntax methodology" (academia.edu / T4) for the opposing case.
- **The Modulor.** Le Corbusier's module (red series from 113 cm navel height, blue from 226 cm raised-arm height, Fibonacci/golden-section derived) is a real, documented system with a huge 20th-century influence, but it is an idiosyncratic proportional scheme, not a coordination standard: it does not align with manufactured dimensions or with the ISO 100 mm basic-module series, and it has been widely criticised as mystical. For this project, cite it at most as precedent for "pick a module derived from the body", and never as an efficiency argument. Note the host grid's 500 mm tile is not an ISO coordination size (TH-22).
- **Percentile design rules (95th male clearance / 5th female reach).** Standard ergonomics teaching, but the retrievable primary sources (HFES guidelines, ISO 7250-1, Pheasant & Haslegrave) could not be text-verified in this pass. The *asymmetry principle* is safe; the *named percentiles* are `UNCITED — heuristic`. Codes give the defensible numbers (32 in door, 30×48 in clear floor space, 60 in turning circle).
- **Circulation share 12–25 %.** Only a T4 vendor glossary could be retrieved. Different references measure different denominators (net vs gross, whether the lift hall counts, whether the corridor inside a suite counts), so any single percentage is comparable only with an identical definition. Always publish the numerator/denominator tile classification with the number.
- **Alexander's patterns.** *A Pattern Language* is empirically grounded in the authors' own observation and is explicitly a design heuristics collection, not a measured standard: Pattern 159 asserts a behavioural preference and gives no dimensions. Use it to justify *ordering decisions* (prefer two aspects), never to justify a number.
- **Defensible space and privacy regulation.** Both are influential but their crime-rate findings are contested on causation, reporting effects and changing policing/design practice, and both were developed for housing estates and face-to-face interaction at street/estate scale, not for interior floor plates. Their transferable core here is the *territorial grammar* (legible boundary, controlled crossing, scale of shared territory), which is what this file uses.
- **Altman/Goffman operationalisation.** Altman's four states and Goffman's access/order-of-interaction concepts are cited from standard secondary descriptions; the primary texts could not be retrieved in this pass, so this file uses them at the level of "privacy is regulated at boundaries" (safe) and does not attribute taxonomy details or counts to them.
- **Gaps I could not close.** (i) No retrievable verified source for hotel/healthcare/education dimensional standards (guest-room areas, ward bed spacing, classroom m²/pupil) — so this file supplies none for those types, and TH-24's verified minima are England-residential-only. (ii) CIBSE/EN lift quality-of-service numeric bands unverified. (iii) ISO 1006/2848 basic-module text unverified (titles only). (iv) The full ISO/BOMA/RICS definitions of net-to-gross and leasable/sellable ratio were not retrieved, so the efficiency entry rests on a T4 circulation-share band plus grid arithmetic; a follow-up pass should pull RICS IPMS 2/3 or ANSI/BOMA Z65.1 measurement definitions.
