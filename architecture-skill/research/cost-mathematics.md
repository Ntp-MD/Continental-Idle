# Research file — Domain AL: Cost and life-cycle arithmetic for design decisions

Scope: the **arithmetic** a design can be cost-checked with — what a currency-per-m² rate actually
contains, which area it is divided by, how construction cost splits by element, what geometry costs,
how height costs, how a cost-per-key figure is built up, what accuracy a stage of a cost plan carries,
and how a life-cycle cost is discounted. It exists because `coverage-map.md` §5 names "cost model
mathematics" as the one quantitative gap left open after v3: the corpus has the *structure* of
trade-offs but not the numbers that make a design falsifiable on cost.

Boundary, and it is a hard one. `economics.md` (EC-01…EC-22) owns the trade-off logic — efficiency vs
quality vs cost vs flexibility, net-to-gross as a reported metric rather than an objective, the
BOMA/rentable measurement conventions, the SCI frame-rate comparison, the per-key framing (`EC-19`),
the discipline box. `lifecycle.md` (LC-01…LC-24) owns service-life *obligation*, change-of-use tests and
the retain-first argument. `hotel-operating-standards.md` (HO-*) owns hospitality operating ratios and
the cost-per-key ceiling as a programme constraint (`HO-04`). `construction-programme.md` (CP-*) owns
time. `professional-practice.md` PP-24 owns the shape-cost premiums already read from ASAQS.
`evaluation.md` EV-18 owns the per-m² proxy discipline and `decision-making.md` DM-10 the whole-life
comparison principle — DM-10's `Source` line is still a bare "need URL", and this file is where that
citation belongs. Nothing below restates any of that reasoning. Where a number already sits in the
corpus it is cited by ID and used as an input, never re-derived from a fresh source to make the same
point. Two things this file adds and no other file carries: the rate-inclusion/area-basis table that
makes two "cost per m²" figures comparable or incomparable, and the discounted life-cycle arithmetic
(formula, worked NPV, service-life replacement cycles, stage tolerances).

Host grid: 1 tile = 0.5 m; 1 tile² = 0.25 m²; 4 tiles = 1 m². The grid has **no currency, no rates, no
time and no section** (`uncertainty.md` UN-01: cost is a not-representable property). What the grid *can*
compute is the quantity side of every cost equation — areas by element, counts of unique modules,
perimeter lengths, spans, core ratio, storey counts, and floor-to-floor height **as a declared input**.
So the standing form of every rule below is: *quantity from the tiles, price from an external rate the
user supplies, and the arithmetic in between published*. A currency figure this file emits without a
user rate is a reporting error, not a result.

**Reading conventions.** Evidence tiers per the corpus: T1 codes/standards/government and statutory
guidance · T2 peer-reviewed · T3 established professional publications · T4 professional community and
vendor · T5 this repo / arithmetic. Every money figure carries **currency, year, region, scope
inclusions and area basis**; a figure missing any of the first four is quarantined in
`## Weak or contested`. Amounts stated "in base year prices" exclude escalation and are labelled;
VAT/tax and contingency are called out separately because they are the two inclusions that most often
differ between a UK and a US figure. A modelled or this-file-derived number is labelled `derived` at
the point of use, never presented as a source figure (`audit/06` A1, A15). `HEURISTIC` = a range with an
explicitly absent source. `NEEDS VERIFICATION:` = the document to obtain.

## Rules

### CM-01 State the area basis and the inclusion list in the same sentence as the rate
- Rule: No currency-per-area figure enters a report without both of its denominators named on the face of the number: the **area basis** (which area was divided into it) and the **inclusion list** (which cost blocks are inside it). Emit the pair as a fixed suffix: `£/m² GIFA, works cost, 2024 prices, London, excl. fees/VAT/contingency`.
- Evidence: The UK measurement standard makes the pairing explicit and it is not optional — a *cost per m² of gross internal floor area (cost/m² of GIFA)* is defined as "the unit rate which, when multiplied by the gross internal floor area (GIFA), gives the total **building works estimate** (i.e. works cost estimate less main contractor's preliminaries and main contractor's overheads and profit)", and GIFA "is to be measured in accordance with the 'Core definition: gross internal area (GIA)' of the RICS Code of Measuring Practice". So the rate's *own definition* carries the scope limit: it is a works rate, not a project rate. A second, independent practitioner source defines the square-metre rate the other way round — "the Net cost of the building (**excluding site works, cost of land, etc.**) … divided by the square metre of building area measured between the main enclosing walls, staircases, lift shafts or other circulation space" — same arithmetic shape, different exclusion list, different currency, different country, no year printed. That is the whole argument for the rule in two sentences.
- Source: S1 NRM1 *Order of cost estimating and cost planning for capital building works*, 2nd ed., RICS, UK, 2012 (effective 1 Jan 2013), §1 definitions "Cost per m2 of gross internal floor area", §2.4 measurement rules — https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/nrm_1_order_of_cost_estimating_and_cost_planning_2nd_edition_rics.pdf — T1. S4 B. van den Heever (QS), *The Influence of Design on Building Cost*, South Africa, undated, prices in Rand — https://autospec.co.za/productmedia/autospec/links_page/documents/design_influence.pdf — T4.
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: every cost rate, every building type, every jurisdiction
- Confidence: High (the S1 wording is verbatim from a document opened and read; S4 supports only the *existence* of a rival convention, not a number)
- Grid translation: The engine can produce the denominator and nothing else. Area basis it can emit is `gross_internal_tiles × 0.25 m²` (tile count inside the enclosing wall line, walls included — this is the GIA analogue) and `net_leased_tiles × 0.25 m²` (room-correct tiles only — EC-01's second number). It cannot choose between them on the user's behalf: `area_basis` is a required report field. E.g. `gross_internal_area_m2: 4128.0; net_leased_area_m2: 3211.5; rate_applied: USER_SUPPLIED (absent) → cost: NOT_COMPUTABLE`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Failure symptom is the two-figure collision this rule exists for: `£2,200/m²` from a UK cost analysis (works cost, GIFA, excl. fees) beside `$210/SF` from a US cost-per-key table (total construction cost, gross floor area, incl. some FF&E, excl. land and soft costs) divided by the same building and reported as a 25% saving. Both figures are correct; the comparison is meaningless. Same trap at a smaller scale: a design change that adds a wall line raises *net* area denominators differently from *gross*, so an unchanged total cost prints as a higher rate.

### CM-02 Never read a works cost/m² as the cost of the project
- Rule: Book the rate ladder explicitly and stop at the rung the number actually is. In the published build-up the building works estimate is only one of eleven stacked lines; the *cost limit* is `works cost estimate + project/design team fees + other development/project costs + risk allowances`, and the *authorised* figure adds tender inflation, then construction inflation, and Value Added Tax sits outside the whole stack as a separate assessment. Emit `cost/m² GIFA` only with the rung named.
- Evidence: NRM1's numbered build-up, read directly: `(1)` facilitating works, `(2)` building works, `(3)` main contractor's preliminaries, `(4) = (1)+(2)+(3)`, `(5)` main contractor's overheads and profit, `(6) = (4)+(5)` **works cost estimate**, `(7)` project/design team fees, `(8) = (6)+(7)`, `(9)` other development/project costs, `(10) = (8)+(9)` **base cost estimate**, `(11) = (11(a))+(11(b))+(11(c))+(11(d))` **risk allowances** (design development / construction / employer change / employer other), `(12) = (10)+(11)` **cost limit (excluding inflation)**, `(13)` tender inflation, `(14)` cost limit (excluding construction inflation), `(15)` construction inflation, `(16) = (14)+(15)` **cost limit (including inflation)**, with `VAT assessment` carried on its own line outside the ladder. The *works cost estimate* is separately defined as containing "no allowance for project/design team fees, other development/project costs, risk allowances, tender inflation and construction inflation." Two printed-line anomalies are recorded rather than corrected: the extraction of `(14)` shows `= (12) + (11)` where the line above `(13)` is the tender inflation estimate, so the addend is read as `(13)`; and the base-cost rule is that `(10)` "is to contain no allowances" — the sentence truncates in the copy read. Magnitude of the gap between the bottom and top of the ladder is *not* printed in the document read → `NEEDS VERIFICATION:` an NRM1 cost-report template with a filled numeric example, or a published cost analysis showing works cost and cost limit for the same project.
- Source: S1 NRM1 2nd ed., RICS, UK, 2012 — §2.15 risk allowances, §2.16 inflation, §2.17 VAT assessment, and the cost-limit summation table (lines (10)–(16)) — T1 (URL as CM-01).
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: capital building works, UK-derived cost structures; the ladder shape is the exportable part, not the line names
- Confidence: High on the ladder's existence and order (verbatim); Low on any percentage gap between rungs (not read)
- Grid translation: The plan engine emits at most the *quantity* that the works rate multiplies. Everything above the works line is a scalar the user supplies, so the report block needs the rung field: `cost_rung: works_cost_estimate | cost_limit_excl_inflation | cost_limit_incl_inflation | not_declared`, defaulting to `not_declared` and blocking any headline total. A per-floor tile count must never be allowed to inherit a rate whose rung is unnamed.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Two failure modes are common. (1) A fee or contingency percentage from one jurisdiction is applied to another jurisdiction's rate that already includes it — double-counting; the UK figure excludes fees by construction, most US "total construction cost" figures exclude them too but *include* general conditions and owner hard costs, which are the equivalent of preliminaries. (2) Tax: a VAT-inclusive European figure compared with a sales-tax-exclusive US figure differs by the tax rate alone, and no design change explains it.

### CM-03 Keep the three divisors apart: per m² of area, per functional unit, per site
- Rule: Three different denominators answer three different questions and may not be substituted for each other: area-based unit rates (cost/m² GIFA, per element and per sub-element), **functional unit** rates (per bed space, per house type, per m² of retail area, per key), and **site area** rates for external works. Publish which one is the headline and keep the others as rows.
- Evidence: NRM1 names all three. Functional-unit rate: "the unit rate which, when multiplied by the number of functional units, gives the total building works estimate", with the estimating methods listed as "(a) floor area method; (b) functional unit method (e.g. per bed space, per house type and per m² of retail area); and (c) elemental method". External works get their own denominator: "Where the external works is to be measured separately, the site area (SA) is to be measured. The SA is the total area of the site within the site title boundaries … **excluding the footprint of the new building(s)**, measured on a horizontal plane." Multi-building and mixed-function cases are forced apart at source: "Where measurement is for more than one building, the measurement for each building is to be shown separately", and a single building with mixed uses must have each function's GIFA "calculated and quantified separately", split on "the centre line of the party wall".
- Source: S1 NRM1 2nd ed., RICS, UK, 2012 — §2.1.1 estimating methods, §2.4 (a)(ii)–(v) measurement rules, Appendix A — T1 (URL as CM-01).
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: cost reporting at any stage; the mixed-function split rule is the operationally important one for a hotel with retail and offices
- Confidence: High (verbatim)
- Grid translation: The engine can compute all three denominators from tiles: `gross_internal_area_m2` (per floor, per building), `key_count` (room objects flagged `habitable`), `retail_area_m2` (rooms flagged `commercial`), `site_area_m2` (site polygon minus building footprint, both already tile sets). The per-building and per-function separation is a tile-attribution problem the grid handles natively — every tile already carries a room, and every room a use. A mixed-use floor must be reported as two rows, never one blended rate.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Cost per key is the seductive one because it is the industry's own headline (`EC-19`, `HO-04`), and it silently hides a density decision: two hotels with identical per-key cost can differ 40% in area per key, which is an amenity difference the number conceals. Conversely a per-m² figure conceals the services-intensity difference between an all-suite and an economy property. Neither divisor lies; reporting only one of them does.

### CM-04 Run the five-field check before comparing rates, and show the check
- Rule: Comparison is only licensed when **currency, base year, region, scope/inclusion list, area basis** all match. Where any field differs, the two figures are reported side by side as *different measurements*, never differenced. Publish the field table, not just the verdict.
- Evidence: The professional guidance on benchmarking is that the fields are what make data usable at all. NRM1's own definition of a *cost analysis* is the comparison of "current estimated costs against cost targets previously set for elements or sub-elements", which presupposes identical element boundaries; and the practice-information document on cost analysis demonstrates the point on a single unit rate: the same `83` per m² is *worthless* under a thin description ("Substructures comprising excavation to reduce levels, disposal of surplus material off-site, construction of foundations and ground floor") and *usable* under a full one, because the description is what fixes scope: "the unit rate of [83] relates to a specific type of foundation and floor construction detail. The rate is therefore attached to a usefully detailed scope of works. This means then that the rate could be used for reference." It also refuses the naive two-project comparison: "From Figure 5 we can see that the substructures for each project do not vary too much. However, Project A appears to have a higher percentage cost for fixtures, fittings and equipment (FF&E) than the other projects. Projects D, E and H appear to be quite heavily serviced in comparison to the others."
- Source: S2 RICS *Cost analysis and benchmarking*, practice information, UK, 2nd ed., August 2024, §3.1 and the Table 1/Figure 5 worked comparison — https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/Cost-analysis-and-benchmarking_2nd-edition.pdf — T1. S1 NRM1 2nd ed. §1 "Cost checking" — T1 (URL as CM-01).
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: all comparative cost statements, including any rate quoted into this corpus
- Confidence: High that scope-description governs rate usability (worked example read); Medium that five fields is the complete field list — it is this file's enumeration, and the documents read name area, scope and element boundaries but do not print a five-field checklist
- Grid translation: Encode the check as a hard gate on the report. `rate_provenance: {currency, base_year, region, scope_includes[], scope_excludes[], area_basis}`. If any field is `null`, the router emits `RATE_INCOMPLETE — comparison blocked (CM-04)` and the design passes/fails only on quantity metrics. The grid's own contribution stays the numerator-independent part: `area_delta_m2`, `perimeter_delta_m`, `unique_module_delta`, which are comparison-safe without any rate.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Escalation is the field people fake. Applying a 2019 rate to a 2024 design "adjusted by inflation" needs a published index; the corpus has none opened, so an escalated figure without an index name and series is treated as an unlabelled new rate and quarantined. Second failure: averaging rates across regions to get "an" answer — the merged figure is `HEURISTIC` at best and must show the underlying spread.

### CM-05 Price the element, not the lump: a cost analysis is only reusable at sub-element depth
- Rule: Record and compare cost at the level where the construction is describable — element → sub-element → component, with quantity, unit, rate and total each carried. A lump-sum element line is a dead end: it cannot be reused, benchmarked or traced to a design decision.
- Evidence: The guidance shows the identical number under two descriptions and rules that only one of them survives: under description (a) "other than establishing what the cost of the substructures item equates to, the description tells us nothing about the extent of excavation, the foundation solution or the construction detailing of the ground floor, i.e. very little about the project. The resulting cost data therefore has limited use because it does not establish what it specifically represents." Under (b) it "could be used for reference", and the document then prescribes the decomposition: the substructures item "could be further broken down into: ground beams (measured m), pad foundations (measured nr), and ground floor slab (measured m²)", which "generates useable cost data but also informs the observer about key details relating to the project." The same standard's benchmarking-selection list requires candidate projects to "be of a similar size (GIA and number of storeys)" before percentages are compared at all.
- Source: S2 RICS *Cost analysis and benchmarking*, UK, 2nd ed., Aug 2024, §3.1 Table 1 comparison and §3.2.1 project-selection criteria — T1 (URL as CM-04). S1 NRM1 2nd ed. §2.1.4 (element unit quantities used for cost analysis) — T1 (URL as CM-01).
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: cost planning, benchmarking, and any rate this file is asked to reuse
- Confidence: High (verbatim, worked example in the source)
- Grid translation: The engine's element classes map one-to-one onto this need, and the sub-element it can reach is defined by *geometry*: `substructure` = ground-floor tiles + declared excavation depth, `superstructure.frame` = column objects on the grid, `envelope.facade` = perimeter tiles × storey height, `services` = riser/duct band tiles (`SV-*` budgets), `fit-out` = finish attributes on room objects. What it cannot reach is anything behind a description — concrete grade, reinforcement ratio, disposal distance. So the row template is `{element, sub_element, quantity_m/m2/nr, source: TILES | USER, unit_rate: USER_SUPPLIED | ABSENT}` and any `ABSENT` rate blocks the money column only.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Over-decomposition is a real cost. The same source's caution is that elemental detail must be matched to the decision it informs; splitting to component level on a concept scheme produces data nobody prices twice. And an element boundary that moves between standards (a façade line that in one taxonomy carries windows, in another does not) silently breaks the comparison even when all five CM-04 fields agree.

### CM-06 Convert the perimeter-to-area index into money by pricing the wall, not the ratio
- Rule: `EC-08` establishes the index (`perimeter m per m² of floor`). This rule spends it: façade cost = perimeter × storey height × a per-m²-of-wall rate, so a plan's shape penalty is computable from tiles alone once *one* wall rate is supplied. Report the two equal-area shapes side by side; the difference is a design decision, not a rate uncertainty.
- Evidence: A published worked comparison does exactly this arithmetic. Two plans of **equal floor area, 400 m² each**: Case A (square) perimeter 80 m, Case B (long and narrow) perimeter 208 m; at a 3 m storey height and a wall rate of 200 per m² of wall face, the enclosure cost per m² of floor is `80 × 3 × 200 ÷ 400 = 120` for A and `208 × 3 × 200 ÷ 400 = 312` for B — a **2.6×** rate difference from shape alone, and "The length of building B thus resulted in an additional expense of R 76,800.00 over that of Building A." The same source gives the ratio as a decision variable: "The Perimeter/Floor area Ratio allows us to compare different plans to establish the more economical proposal. The lower the ratio, the more economical will be the proposal", computed there as enclosing-wall area ÷ floor area = 1.50 vs 1.20 for a 3.00 m storey. Its scale warning: "the perimeter cost can be in the order of 20% to 30% of total cost and an external wall is normally almost twice as expensive as an internal partition." The Rand amounts are region- and year-stamped and unusable as rates (see below); the **ratios and the arithmetic are the transferable content**.
- Source: S4 B. van den Heever (QS), *The Influence of Design on Building Cost*, South Africa, undated (Rand prices), worked cases "REGULAR SHAPES / CASE A, CASE B" and "PERIMETER/FLOOR AREA" — https://autospec.co.za/productmedia/autospec/links_page/documents/design_influence.pdf — T4. Index it spends: `economics.md` EC-08.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE (arithmetic) / HEURISTIC (the 20–30% perimeter share of total cost)
- Scope: any building whose envelope cost is a material share of total; weak for curtain-wall towers where the rate per m² of wall varies by orientation
- Confidence: High on the arithmetic (independently reproduced: 80×3=240 m² wall ÷ 400 m² floor = 0.60 m²/m², ×200 = 120 ✓; 208×3=624 m² ÷ 400 = 1.56 m²/m², ×200 = 312 ✓; and 208/80 = 2.6 ✓). **Low on the 20–30% share** — one undated practitioner source, no jurisdiction, no building type. `NEEDS VERIFICATION:` a BCIS/TVOA elemental percentage for "External wall finishes and products" by building type with year.
- Grid translation: Perimeter and area are exact tile quantities. Worked on the host grid for a 2,400 m² floor plate, storey height 3.5 m, derived: depth 12 m → 100 m×24 m → perimeter 248 m → `wall_area/floor_area = 0.362 m²/m²`; depth 24 m → 100 m×24 m is the same rectangle, so the family is run over aspect at constant area — 2,400 m² at 1:1 (49×49 m) gives 196 m; at 1:2 (35×69 m) gives 208 m; at 1:4 (24.5×98 m) gives 245 m; at 1:8 (17.3×138.7 m) gives 312 m. Façade cost therefore rises **+59%** from square to 1:8 aspect with floor area unchanged. Emission keys: `perimeter_m`, `floor_area_m2`, `perimeter_area_ratio_per_m`, `facade_wall_area_m2 = perimeter_m × floor_to_floor_height_m`, `shape_penalty_vs_square: +59%`. Only the last step needs a rate.
- Exceptions / failure mode: The rule under-prices compact plans and over-prices interesting ones. Circular work costs more per metre than straight work ("Circular buildings have the best ratio but savings are more than offset by the higher cost of circular work"), a curved façade defeats unitised repetition, and a step-back tower trades one perimeter for two. Where the wall rate per metre differs by orientation (a feature elevation at 3× the courtyard elevation), the *length* arithmetic is right and the *money* is wrong unless elevations are priced separately — which the tile grid can do, because elevation attribution is a tile-edge property.

### CM-07 Book the shape penalty where it is actually incurred: setting-out, excavation and drainage
- Rule: An irregular plan does not pay a single "complexity" percentage. It pays separate premiums in identifiable trades, and the dominant one is labour-based, not material-based. Price each premium against its own base, never against total cost.
- Evidence: The same practitioner source enumerates the split for a regular vs irregular pair of equal floor area: "(1) Building B has a higher perimeter/floor area ratio … and requires **6% more external walling** to enclose the same floor area than A. (2) **Setting out costs will be increased by as much as 50%.** (3) **Excavations will cost between 6% and 20% more.** (4) **Drainage costs will increase by approximately 25%** due to the extra manholes and extra length of piping needed. (5) Additional costs will also result from other elements of the building such as the walling and roofing due to the work being complicated by the shape." The asymmetry is the useful part: the largest premium (50%) sits on a small base (setting out), so a naive "shape adds 50%" is wrong by an order of magnitude.
- Source: S4 van den Heever, *The Influence of Design on Building Cost*, South Africa, undated — T4 (URL as CM-06). Adjacent corpus ownership: `professional-practice.md` PP-24 (ASAQS shape premiums), `economics.md` EC-12 (specificity ladder with declared penalties).
- Class: HEURISTIC (all four percentages) — the magnitudes are one undated source's assertions; the decomposition logic is the defensible content
- Scope: plan shape irregularity at constant area; does not cover sectional irregularity (slope, stepping)
- Confidence: Low on the numeric values (single T4 source, unverified, no currency year). Medium-high on the *structure*: premiums are per-base and non-additive on total cost, which is arithmetic, not opinion.
- Grid translation: Every driver here is computable as a tile-graph property. `orthogonality` = fraction of perimeter edges aligned to the two grid axes; `facade_segments` = count of maximal straight runs (one straight run per collinear edge group); `reentrant_corners` = interior corners > 180°; `plan_convexity` = floor area ÷ bounding-box area. Derived consequence for a 2,400 m² plate: an orthogonal rectangle gives `facade_segments = 4`, `reentrant_corners = 0`, `plan_convexity = 1.0`; an L-plate of the same area gives `facade_segments = 6`, `reentrant_corners = 1`; a pinwheel gives 8+ segments. Report the premiums as *per-element* multipliers with the element's own quantity so a reader can see the small base: `setting_out_premium: +50% applied to setting_out_cost (NOT total)`. Where no rate is supplied the design is flagged on the geometric keys, which are rate-free.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Double-counting is the failure: a 6% wall-length penalty and a "shape" penalty on total cost both charge the same wall twice, and the second one is usually larger, so the design looks 12% worse than it is. Second failure: the premiums are not scale-invariant — a 50% setting-out penalty on a small job is a day of a surveyor's time, and the same percentage on a large job is already inside the preliminaries line, which `CM-10` shows is the smaller share of a bigger project.

### CM-08 Price slab span as an L² consequence, and state the system you priced
- Rule: A longer span is not a percentage; it is a scaling law with a system-dependent exponent. Report the required structural depth from the span/depth limit, then the concrete and reinforcement consequence per m² of floor. Where the source is absent, emit `HEURISTIC` and the arithmetic — never a bare number.
- Evidence: `HEURISTIC — no published cost-per-span table was opened in this pass.` The physics is not in doubt and is already in this corpus: for a one-way spanning element under uniform load the design moment goes with `wL²/8`, so at fixed section the steel demand rises with the **square** of span, and practical design holds that increase down by growing depth, where span/depth limits for one-way solid slabs sit in the region of 20–25 (simply supported) and 28–32 (continuous) and one-way beams around 12–16 — the bands `structural-sizing.md` carries and `construction.md` uses to pick the system. The cost consequence follows arithmetically: a 6.0 m→7.5 m slab span (+25%) drives required depth `L/24` from 250 mm to 313 mm (+25% concrete), and if the moment were taken by depth alone the steel per m² rises by 1.25² = **1.5625, i.e. +56%** at fixed capacity utilisation. `NEEDS VERIFICATION:` a priced span comparison — the open options are a peer-reviewed reinforced-concrete cost-versus-span function, or a BCIS/TVOA portal-frame and flat-slab rate comparison in £/m² with year and region.
- Source: corpus-internal only: `structural-sizing.md` ST-* (span/depth limits), `construction.md` CN-* (system selection by span), `economics.md` EC-09 (keeping the structural bay in the documented economical band). No external monetary source read for this rule.
- Class: HEURISTIC
- Scope: concrete floor systems; the exponent changes for steel (depth rises, tonnage per m² may fall as decking spans further) and for CLT (span-limited, not cost-minimised)
- Confidence: High on the L² mechanics (arithmetic); **Low on any percentage cost figure**, which is exactly why none is emitted
- Grid translation: Span is a first-class tile quantity — it is the distance between column/承重 objects, and on a 0.5 m grid it is exact to 500 mm. Emit `span_grid_m = {x_spans[], z_spans[]}`, `bays_per_floor`, `slab_depth_mm_derived = span/24`, `slab_concrete_m3_per_m2 = depth/1000`, `moment_index = w*L^2/8` relative. Then the only external number needed is a £/m³-of-concrete-or-£/kg-of-steel rate. A design that changes bay size from 7.5 m to 6.0 m is a **rate-free improvement** the grid can assert on `slab_depth_mm_derived` and `slab_concrete_m3_per_m2` alone.
- Exceptions / failure mode: The common error is pricing a span change on the slab alone. Longer spans remove columns, which changes the core, the party-wall positions, the services routes and the lettable efficiency, and in a hotel can be the difference between a 1.2 m and 1.5 m bathroom zone. The slab premium can be negative at the whole-building level. Second error: applying one system's exponent to another — post-tensioned slabs buy span with depth and tendons, so their cost curve is flatter, and a heuristic built on ordinary reinforcement will overstate the penalty and justify a needless grid.

### CM-09 Report plan depth as a trade between two priced quantities, not a single optimum
- Rule: Depth moves façade cost down and structure, internal-wall and artificial-environment cost up, on different bases. Emit both sides as computed quantities with the sign of the trade visible; do not name an optimum depth without a rate for both sides and a daylight criterion.
- Evidence: The trade is asserted in two places the corpus already holds. The practitioner source states the mechanism on the cost side and adds the second leg explicitly for offices: "Office buildings with depths up to 18m lease more readily to smaller businesses as areas can be easily split to accommodate different concerns" (S4, undated SA). The economic-side rule `EC-06` already requires the plate be sized "from the core-to-perimeter curve, not from the maximum plot", and the daylight multiplier in `EN-04`/`EQ-*` bounds the usable depth physically. Derived arithmetic at constant 2,400 m² floor and 3.5 m storey, façade-only: 12 m depth (200×12 m, perimeter 424 m) → 1,484 m² of wall per floor; 24 m depth (100×24 m, perimeter 248 m) → 868 m²; 30 m depth (80×30 m, perimeter 220 m) → 770 m². So **depth 12→24 m cuts façade wall 41.5%** and 24→30 m only 11.3% — the savings curve flattens, while span and daylight penalties keep growing.
- Source: S4 van den Heever, undated, South Africa — T4 (URL as CM-06). Cross-referenced, not restated: `economics.md` EC-06, EC-08; `envelope-daylight-quantification.md` EQ-* daylight depth; `environmental-design.md` EN-04.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE (the trade) / derived (the percentage figures, computed here from the stated geometry)
- Scope: single-aspect and dual-aspect plates; not applicable to perimeter-block housing where depth is set by the dwelling
- Confidence: High on the arithmetic (checked: 2×(200+12)=424 ✓, 2×(100+24)=248 ✓, 2×(80+30)=220 ✓; 1-868/1484 = 41.5% ✓). Medium on the 18 m lettable-depth claim — a single undated source.
- Grid translation: Depth is directly enumerable: `max_room_depth_from_facade_tiles` by flood-fill from the façade line inward, in tiles (1 tile = 0.5 m). Emit the *distribution*, not the mean: `daylit_depth_share` at ≤ 6 m, ≤ 9 m, ≤ 12 m from a glazed edge (EQ-* datum), plus `mean_depth_m`, `perimeter_m`, `perimeter_area_ratio`. A "deep plan" flag should fire on the distribution (`>50% of net area beyond 9 m from glazing`), because the mean hides a slab block. Cost is then computed twice and shown as two rows: `facade_cost_m2_floor = perimeter×height×rate / area` and `internal_cost_penalty` — never as one index.
- Exceptions / failure mode: The failure is reading the flattening curve as "deeper is cheaper". Beyond the daylight depth, the same floor needs mechanical ventilation and artificial lighting decisions that are *not* in the façade rate (they are `SV-*` and `EQ-*` items), and the structural span penalty of CM-08 grows while the façade saving shrinks — the two curves cross. Atrium and section-cut solutions move the datum so far that "depth" stops meaning anything; then report unlit area instead.

### CM-10 Price additional floor-to-floor height across the whole stack, per element it inflates
- Rule: A storey-height change alters **no floor area** and therefore changes only the elements whose quantity is height-driven. Compute the element-level percentage from geometry and report the total-cost effect as the weighted sum; the weighted sum is small, and saying so is the point.
- Evidence: `DERIVED arithmetic, no source needed for the ratios.` Take a hotel tower, 21 floors, plan perimeter 210 m, floor-to-floor height changed 3.20 m → 3.30 m (+100 mm, +3.125%). Height-driven quantities per the tile model: façade wall area rises 3.125% (210 m × 0.10 m × 21 = **441 m² of extra wall**, on a base of 210 × 3.2 × 21 = 14,112 m²); external wall *length* is unchanged; floor area, room count and key count are unchanged; vertical riser lengths rise 3.125%; excavation and substructure are unchanged. If the envelope element is `x`% of works cost, the total-cost movement is `0.03125 × x`%. With `x` unknown this file emits the formula, not a number. The only published magnitude available is the T4 perimeter share of 20–30% used in CM-06, which would put a +100 mm storey at roughly **0.6–0.9% of total cost** — quoted here strictly as the consequence of an unverified input, and quarantined below. The directional claim that height is a cost lever is stated in the same source under "FLOOR TO CEILING HEIGHT": "It is obvious that by increasing the height of walls, we must increase our rate per M²."
- Source: S4 van den Heever, undated, South Africa — T4 (URL as CM-06), for the direction only. Arithmetic is this file's (derived). Rate-share input is unverified.
- Class: HEURISTIC (0.6–0.9% figure) / DESIGN PRINCIPLE (per-element method)
- Scope: height changes applied uniformly to every floor; not valid for a single tall ground floor, which is a different element mix
- Confidence: High on the 3.125% quantity arithmetic (checked: 0.10/3.20 = 3.125% ✓; 210×0.1×21 = 441 ✓). **Low on any total-cost percentage**, because the element share `x` is not a number this corpus has verified.
- Grid translation: The grid stores floor-to-floor height as a declared input only (`uncertainty.md` UN-01 — no vertical dimension is representable), so the rule runs off `storey_height_m` as a *user-supplied scalar* multiplied by tile-derived perimeter. Emit `height_delta_mm`, `perimeter_m` (tile-derived), `delta_wall_area_m2 = perimeter × delta × n_floors` (derived), `delta_riser_length_m`, `keys_delta: 0`. The `keys_delta: 0` line is the discipline: any cost-per-key figure that moves when only height moved is a rate artifact, not a design change.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Ceiling height is not free but is cheap relative to what it unlocks, and `EC-10` already rules that it must not be reclaimed by thinning the plan. The failure mode is the inverse claim: a hotel raising storey height to gain a ceiling void for a 900 mm duct zone (`SV-12`/`SV-13` duct budgets) pays 3.125% on wall, not on the whole building, but pays it **21 times** and also raises the shaft, louvre and lift-run quantities that CM-06's wall formula never sees. Price the services consequence separately or the change looks trivial when it is not.

### CM-11 Compute the core-ratio cost of an inefficient plate as bought gross area
- Rule: Core and circulation share is paid for in gross floor area, so the cost of an inefficient plate is the extra gross area needed to deliver the same net programme. Compute `gross = net ÷ (1 − core_share)` and report the delta as a percentage of gross — a rate-free number until the last step.
- Evidence: `DERIVED arithmetic.` For a fixed 10,000 m² net programme: core share 18% → gross 12,195 m²; 22% → 12,821 m²; 25% → 13,333 m²; 30% → 14,286 m². Moving 18%→25% buys the same building in **9.3% more gross floor area** (13,333/12,195 = 1.0933) — structure, envelope, substructure and services risers all priced on the larger number. The corpus rule this arithmetic implements is `EC-04` ("Set the circulation + core budget as revenue loss before drawing") and `EC-07` ("core share is a function of storeys, not of taste"); the observational support is the practitioner pair giving circulation space "in blocks of flats varied between 22% and 32%" across two alternative plans (S4, undated SA) — a range, not a target.
- Source: S4 van den Heever, undated, South Africa, "CIRCULATION SPACE" — T4 (URL as CM-06). Arithmetic derived here. Implemented rules: `economics.md` EC-04, EC-07, EC-06; `vertical-transport.md` VT-* for the stair/lift count that sets the floor on core area.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE (the formula) / HEURISTIC (the 22–32% observed range)
- Scope: multi-floor plates where core is a shared vertical element; single-storey buildings pay circulation but not core
- Confidence: High on the arithmetic (checked: 10000/0.82 = 12,195 ✓; 10000/0.75 = 13,333 ✓; ratio 1.0933 ✓). Low on the 22–32% range (single unverified source, one building type, no year).
- Grid translation: Both areas are exact tile quantities: `net_leased_tiles` (sum of room-correct tiles) and `gross_internal_tiles` (all tiles within the enclosing wall line, per EC-01's three-number discipline). Core share is then `1 − net/gross` with the wall tax separated by `EC-03`. Report `core_share_pct` against the *declared budget* and the delta as bought area: `gross_area_penalty_m2 = net/(1-core_actual) - net/(1-core_budget)`. On a 21-floor hotel this is where `VT-*` handling capacity and `CODE-*` stair widths set the floor under the core, so the rule must read those minima first and never report a core share below the code-forced value as a saving.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Two. (1) A larger core is not always waste: it buys egress capacity, lift service and Back-of-house separation, all of which are requirements with their own rules (`EC-22` on mandated area). The correct report is "core share 25% vs budget 22%", never "waste 3%". (2) The formula hides the *shape* of the core — a 1,200 m² floor with a central core and a 1,200 m² floor with two remote cores can share the same percentage and have completely different lettable geometry and different façade exposure.

### CM-12 Publish an elemental split as a range with type, region and year, or do not publish one
- Rule: An elemental percentage profile is an observation about a sample, not a property of buildings. State the sample, the type, the region, the year and the element boundaries; carry the observed min–max spread; and never let a five-element average stand in for a type-specific profile. Where a type-specific profile is absent, say so and leave the row empty rather than borrowing a neighbour's.
- Evidence: A published nine-project comparison gives the ranges and, unusually, the spread rather than only the mean. RICS *Cost analysis and benchmarking* (UK, 2nd ed., August 2024) Table 1/Figure 5, "Elemental distribution of cost main building elements", reports for the nine projects the columns Substructure / Superstructure / Internal finishes / FF&E / Services, with values in the observed bands **substructure ≈5.7–9.0%, superstructure ≈36.6–47.7%, internal finishes ≈5.9–12.5%, FF&E ≈1.7–26.0%, services ≈21.2–41.7%**, and an average row reading `5.66 | 47.68 | 6.64 | 9.81 | 33.47`. The document's own reading of the same table is the warning: "the substructures for each project do not vary too much. However, Project A appears to have a higher percentage cost for fixtures, fittings and equipment (FF&E) than the other projects. Projects D, E and H appear to be quite heavily serviced in comparison to the others." **The nine projects are not labelled by building type in the material read, and the price base date of the table is not printed there** — so this range is usable as a *dispersion* statement and not as a type benchmark. Two further cautions from the same source: percentages may only be compared after the sample is matched, since candidate projects should "be of a similar size (GIA and number of storeys)", and cost is driven by two named external factors — "The location of the building project, (both geographically by region, or relative to other buildings and infrastructure)" and "The state of the construction economy (and the general economy) at the point in time".
- Source: S2 RICS *Cost analysis and benchmarking*, practice information, UK, 2nd ed., Aug 2024, §2.2.4, §3.1.1, §3.2.1, Table 1/Figure 5 — https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/Cost-analysis-and-benchmarking_2nd-edition.pdf — T1 (RICS standards framework; "This document applies to the UK and Crown Dependencies").
- Class: FACT (the published ranges) / BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION (the prohibition on a universal profile)
- Scope: benchmarking and cost planning at elemental level; percentages only, never currency
- Confidence: High that the bands are as printed and that the document refuses a single profile. **Low on applying them to any named type** — the sample's types are unlabelled and no price base date was read. `NEEDS VERIFICATION:` (a) the price base date and project types for Table 1; (b) one type-labelled elemental profile per flagship type — hotel, office, residential mid-rise, hospital — from a source that prints type and year, e.g. a BCIS Online Cost Guide elemental summary, a US peer-reviewed hospital cost breakdown, or a published cost plan of a named real project; (c) at least one non-UK jurisdiction, since all of the above is UK.
- Grid translation: The grid can produce the *quantities* the split is over and nothing else. Emit per element: `substructure_area_m2` (ground-floor tiles + declared excavation), `superstructure_volume_m3`, `facade_area_m2` (perimeter × storey height, per elevation), `fit_out_area_m2` (finish-attributed room tiles), `services_band_area_m2` (`SV-*` duct/riser budgets), `unique_module_count`. Then the register reports each element's **share of quantity**, which is rate-free and comparable within one design — `facade_share_of_derived_quantity: 11.8%` — and the *money* split only after the user supplies per-element rates. That is the honest version of an elemental cost plan on a grid with no prices.
- Exceptions / failure mode: The FF&E column is the trap: 1.7% to 26.0% across one table is a 15-fold spread, and the difference is operator brand standard, not architecture — which is exactly the `HO-*`/`EC-19` per-key lever. A profile that silently mixes FF&E into "fit-out" makes every subsequent value-engineering conversation wrong. Second failure: normalising on gross area where one project's analysis includes basement (see CM-13) and another does not.

### CM-13 Treat basement area as a different rate applied to a different quantity, not as a premium percentage
- Rule: Below-ground works are not "ground floor plus a bit". They are a separate element family whose quantity basis changes (m² of internal area, m³ of excavated volume, m² of retained wall, m² of tanking) and whose rates do not scale from the above-ground figure. Require the analysis to declare whether basement works exist, and price them on their own lines.
- Evidence: The requirement to declare it is explicit in the UK cost-analysis standard's list of context that must accompany an analysis — items 10–16 of that list name "The GIA and the NIA, or IPMS measures (as applicable)", "The wall to floor ratio", "The extent of circulation space", "Typical room sizes (note: this is especially useful for accommodation-based projects such as hotels and student accommodation)", "Number of storeys" and, for this rule, **"Whether or not the project includes for basement works"**. The rate magnitude is the weak part. `HEURISTIC, arithmetic shown:` the only verified substructure unit rate in this file is a UK analysis line of **490 m² at £83/m² = £40,670** (S2, UK, doc dated Aug 2024) — and the source itself points out that this £83 describes "ground beams with pad foundations **1,000 deep** plus floor construction – filling to make up levels, compacting, sand blinding, dpm, insulation and concrete 175mm thick", i.e. it is a *shallow* solution at ~1 m depth with no retention, no excavation-to-depth premium and no tanking. A single-storey basement of the same footprint adds, as separate quantities: excavated spoil (`= plan area × depth × bulking factor`, disposal-priced), retaining structure (wall area, reinforcement-intensive), waterproofing/tanking (wall + floor area), a slab designed for uplift, plus pumped drainage and mechanical smoke control — none of which are in the £83. So the defensible statement is *not* "basements cost 2–3× more", it is "**the £83/m² line does not price a basement, and a basement's cost is the sum of five quantities the shallow line contains none of**". `NEEDS VERIFICATION:` a published £/m² or £/m³ comparison of a raft-on-ground substructure against a retained single-basement substructure, same region and year — BCIS element-level data or a published cost plan with both.
- Source: S2 RICS *Cost analysis and benchmarking*, UK, 2nd ed., Aug 2024, §3.1.1 required-context list items 10–16, and the §3.1.2 worked substructure comparison — T1 (URL as CM-12). Rate arithmetic is this file's (derived).
- Class: HEURISTIC (any basement premium magnitude) / FACT (the declaration requirement and the £83 line's scope)
- Scope: sites where below-grade area is optional; not applicable where the geology forces pile-rafts or where water table dominates
- Confidence: High that an undeclared basement invalidates a cost comparison (the standard says to declare it, and the arithmetic of the differing quantity bases is not in doubt). **Low on magnitude — no basement rate read.**
- Grid translation: The grid cannot draw a basement (one 2D grid per floor, no vertical datum), so below-grade area is a **declared input** and everything downstream is derived from it: `basement_levels: USER_DECLARED`, `basement_area_m2 = declared × footprint_tiles × 0.25`, `excavation_volume_m3 = basement_area_m2 × declared_depth_m`, `retaining_wall_area_m2 = perimeter_m × total_depth_m`, `tanking_area_m2 = wall + floor`. Emit those four quantity keys and mark the money line `BASEMENT_RATES_ABSENT (CM-13)` — which is a legitimate, checkable output, because the four quantities are exactly what a supplied rate would be multiplied by.
- Exceptions / failure mode: The comparison error: gross area per key including a car park basement is then divided by a works rate derived from buildings without basements, and the design looks expensive for reasons that are geotechnical, not spatial. The reverse error is worse — a design that *deletes* a basement is reported as a saving equal to the full area × the *above-ground* rate, when the true avoided cost is the basement rate applied to a much smaller quantity set. Second: a "courtyard" or "light well" tile change that removes basement area also removes daylight and adds retaining wall — CM-06's length arithmetic re-applies.

### CM-14 Make repetition countable, and price non-repetition as a per-variant setup
- Rule: Repetition is not a mood. Emit `unique_module_count` and `total_instances`, model the cost as `Σ_variants (setup_i + n_i × marginal)` and report the design's variant count against its own budget. If a saving from repetition is claimed, it must show up as a fall in variants at constant area — not as an assertion.
- Evidence: `EC-11` already carries the rule ("Maximise repetition of whole room modules; count the exceptions explicitly") and `EC-13` the kit-size rule, so this file supplies only the arithmetic and the quantity definitions and does not restate the argument. The cost mechanics are the same shape as `CM-07`: the source that prices shape irregularity puts the *labour-side* penalty far above the material-side one — "Setting out costs will be increased by as much as 50%" against "6% more external walling" for the same irregular plan (S4, undated SA) — which is the empirical reason a per-variant `setup_i` term matters more than the unit rate. Formwork is the clearest case: a repeated module re-uses panels, so panel cost per instance falls with `n_i`, while a unique module consumes a new panel set. `HEURISTIC:` no published setup-to-marginal ratio for building modules was opened, so this file emits `setup_i` as a user parameter and refuses to invent a multiplier. `NEEDS VERIFICATION:` a peer-reviewed or trade source giving formwork reuse factors (cost per pour against number of reuses) for repeated wall/room modules.
- Source: S4 van den Heever, undated, South Africa — T4 (URL as CM-06), for the labour/material asymmetry only. Corpus-internal and not restated: `economics.md` EC-11, EC-12, EC-13.
- Class: HEURISTIC (the model's coefficients) / DESIGN PRINCIPLE (the counting requirement)
- Scope: volumetric, modular and repetitive-plan work; near-inapplicable to one-off civic geometry where every instance is a variant
- Confidence: Medium that the `setup_i` term dominates at realistic variant counts (the asymmetry is sourced, the coefficients are not). High on the arithmetic of the model itself.
- Grid translation: This is the grid's strongest cost contribution and it needs no rate. Every room object already carries a type and a footprint, so: `unique_module_count = |{(room_type, w_tiles, d_tiles)}|`, `total_instances = Σ n_i`, `repetition_index = total_instances / unique_module_count`, `variant_list` with instance counts, `module_footprint_histogram`. Worked on the reference key module: a 250-key hotel where every guest room is 6×11 tiles (3.0×5.5 m) gives `unique = 1`, `total = 250`, `repetition_index = 250`; introducing four corner suites and two adaptive rooms on each of 8 floors gives `unique = 1 + 6 = 7`, `total = 250`, `repetition_index = 35.7`, and the 6 new variants must be reported with their instance counts and *why* they exist. A change that raises variants at constant area is a cost increase the grid can assert before any rate is spoken.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Two. (1) Repetition measured on room footprint alone is fake: identical footprints with different door positions, different bathroom layouts or different services routes are not the same module, so `unique_module_count` must be taken on the *whole* module signature (footprint + door offset + wet-zone flip + finish set), or the index overstates reuse. (2) Repetition of a bad module repeats its cost 250 times — `HO-04`'s observation that growing the room "is the cheapest-looking and most expensive actual decision, because it repeats 250 times" is the same arithmetic signed the other way. Report `repetition_index` and `area_per_key` together or the metric is a trap.

### CM-15 Read the cost-of-height curve as land plus construction, and name the two drivers separately
- Rule: Average cost per dwelling per storey is the sum of a falling land component and a rising construction component. A design claim that "taller is cheaper" is only ever about the land term, and it must be reported as such: state the land value input, the construction curve, and the storey count at which they cross.
- Evidence: A central-bank research paper states exactly this for Australian apartment markets. RBA Discussion Paper 2020-04, "What is the Cost-efficient Height for Apartment Buildings?", covering **Sydney, Melbourne and Brisbane, 2018 data, Australian dollars**: "Average cost initially declines with height as the fixed land cost is spread among more apartments", offset by "the increase in average cost with height discussed in Section 4.2". Its worked values: a site assumed at "value of land constant at $4,033 per square metre"; "it costs about $894,000 per apartment to replace detached houses with a **three-storey** building in Sydney"; two-storey apartments "would cost much more. This is considerably more costly than providing high density"; and the density trade-off closes "until buildings reach **20 storeys**, labelled point C, when building out becomes the less costly option", at which point "it costs **$581,000** to supply extra apartments by either approach". Read as arithmetic, the paper's own numbers put the three-storey result ~54% above the 20-storey crossover cost on the same site basis — and the mechanism is the land term, since a three-storey building puts far fewer dwellings on the same site area.
- Source: S5 Reserve Bank of Australia, *Discussion Paper 2020-04: What is the Cost-efficient Height for Apartment Buildings?*, Australia, 2020 (2018 data, AUD) — https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/rdp/2020/2020-04/what-is-the-cost-efficient-height-for-apartment-buildings.html — T2 (central-bank research, peer-reviewed series).
- Class: FACT (the quoted values, region- and year-stamped) / DESIGN PRINCIPLE (the two-term decomposition)
- Scope: residential apartments on purchased land, Australia 2018; the *shape* transfers, the storey number does not
- Confidence: High on the quoted figures (read from the paper's page). Medium on the transfer of "20 storeys" to any other market — the crossover is a function of that $4,033/m² land assumption, and the paper is explicit that the drivers are land value and plot size.
- Grid translation: The engine knows `storeys` (floor count) and `site_area_m2` exactly, so it can compute the *land term* without any currency: `dwellings_per_site_m2 = total_dwellings / site_area_m2` and `land_cost_per_dwelling = site_land_rate × site_area_m2 / total_dwellings`, the last step needing one user input. The construction term is not grid-computable (lift counts and cores are: `VT-*` shaft count, `CM-11` core share). Emit the curve as `storeys → {land_per_dwelling_derived, core_share, lift_shafts, keys_per_floor}` and let the user's rate close it. A design that adds storeys and *raises* core share has moved both terms the wrong way and the register should say so.
- Exceptions / failure mode: The published crossover is a *density* result, not a construction-cost result, and the common misuse is quoting "20 storeys" as a construction optimum. Three failure modes: (1) land value near zero inverts the whole curve, so the same tower is simply more expensive; (2) the storey-count driver in a hotel is not land but the `HO-*`/`VT-*` servicing ladder — a hotel stops being cheaper per key when the lift bank and core, not the site, starts growing with floors; (3) regulatory caps and the fire/egress threshold band (`CODE-*`, `FQ-*`) can make the extra storey cost a step function, not a slope, and no economic curve predicts a stepped change in required stairs.

### CM-16 Apply the height test as a marginal-storey comparison, not a curve reading
- Rule: Approve an extra storey on `marginal_cost_of_storey ÷ marginal_revenue_units_of_storey`, computed against the design's own quantities — extra core, extra structure, extra services and extra lift time — and report it against the alternative of building out. Where a published statement is wanted, this is the one the corpus holds.
- Evidence: The practitioner source states the test without the arithmetic: "Savings can be achieved by decreasing the plan size and increasing the height of buildings but **a point is eventually reached where the cost of vertical transportation, services, substructure etc., outweigh the cost of an additional storey.** Land values, plot size and the buildings function tend to have direct influence on its height." (S4, undated SA.) The RBA paper locates that point empirically at 20 storeys for Australian apartments on a $4,033/m² site basis (S5, AU, 2020) and prices the marginal apartment at $581,000 AUD 2018 there. `DERIVED arithmetic` of the marginal-storey terms for a hotel plate of perimeter 210 m, floor-to-floor 3.2 m and gross floor area 1,250 m²: an added storey buys `+1,250 m² gross`, of which at `CM-11`'s 22% core share only `+975 m² net` ≈ 32 keys at 30 m²; and it consumes `+210×3.2 = 672 m²` of wall, `+1,250 m²` of floor slab, `+1,250 m²` of roof-to-floor build-up, plus the *vertical* terms the flat plan does not scale — risers, shafts and, above a handling-capacity threshold, one more lift car with its shaft area and its machine room.
- Source: S4 van den Heever, undated, South Africa, "HEIGHT OF BUILDING" — T4 (URL as CM-06). S5 RBA DP 2020-04, Australia, 2020 — T2 (URL as CM-15). Arithmetic derived here.
- Class: EXPERIENCE-BASED ADVICE (the sourced statement) / DESIGN PRINCIPLE (the marginal test) / derived (the quantities)
- Scope: multi-floor additions where the substructure and core were designed for fewer storeys than the maximum
- Confidence: Medium on the test's structure, Low on any threshold storey count for a hotel — the corpus holds no hotel height-cost curve. `NEEDS VERIFICATION:` a hotel-specific cost-versus-storey dataset (HVS or a published hotel cost plan with storey counts), which would give the per-key crossover directly.
- Grid translation: All terms are enumerable per floor, so the marginal test is a *diff between two revisions*: `delta_gross_area_m2`, `delta_net_area_m2`, `delta_keys`, `delta_perimeter_m`, `delta_facade_area_m2`, `delta_core_area_m2`, `delta_shaft_count`, `delta_lift_car` (from `VT-*` handling capacity), `delta_stair_count` (from `CODE-*`/`FQ-*` egress). Emit `cost_per_added_key_proxy = delta_total_tiles_for_one_floor / delta_keys`, which is rate-free. The rule's teeth: when `delta_keys` is zero because the egress or lift count forced the whole extra floor into core, the extra storey is pure cost and the register says `marginal_storey: NEGATIVE_YIELD`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: The step-function failure. Lift, stair and egress requirements do not grow smoothly with storeys, so a design can add one floor and pay for two cars, an extra stair and a pressurisation upgrade at once — a marginal cost far above the average of the previous floors. Podium/tower transitions are the second: the storey above a podium is not priced by the tower's average. Third: the test compares against *building out*, which needs the site to allow it and `CM-15`'s land term to be live; on a full plot the comparison is not "up or out", it is "up or don't".

### CM-17 Build cost per key up from quantities, and never carry a published per-key figure as a target
- Rule: `cost_per_key = (GFA_per_key × works_rate + non-works rungs + FF&E) ÷ 1 key`, i.e. the published per-key number is an *output* of the CM-02 ladder plus an FF&E allowance, and the design's only lever is `GFA_per_key`. When a per-key benchmark is quoted, back it out into an implied rate and check that the rate is plausible for the region and year — that is where the comparison actually happens.
- Evidence: The corpus already owns the ladder and the ladder's inputs, cited by ID and used here as inputs, not restated: `EC-19` reports HVS's US Hotel Development Cost Survey 2025 (2024 costs, US) medians of $167k–$169k limited-service/midscale extended-stay, **$223k select-service, $265k upscale extended-stay, $409k full-service, >$1,057k luxury, $219k all-property**, plus Terrapin (US, 2026) select-service $215k–$325k, full-service $385k–$625k and **$25,000–$48,000 per key FF&E** for select-service; `HO-04` fixes the area chain for the reference 250-key scheme at net ≈13,750 m² → **gross ≈17,000–18,000 m² = 68–72 m² GFA per key, non-key ≈45%**. `DERIVED` inversion on those two: at 17,500 m² gross for 250 keys and EC-19's full-service median of $409,000 per key, the implied all-in cost is $102.25M ÷ 17,500 m² = **$5,843/m² gross** (US, 2024, all-in per the survey's own basis). ### CM-19 Separate the two inflations, and never carry a rate without its base date
- Rule: There are two distinct time adjustments and the standard names them separately: **tender inflation** (to the tender date) and **construction inflation** (over the build period). Both sit outside the base cost estimate and are added as their own lines. A rate with no base date is quarantined, and an escalated rate with no named index is treated as a new, unlabelled rate.
- Evidence: NRM1's build-up carries `(13) Tender inflation estimate` and `(15) Construction inflation estimate` as separate constituents feeding `(14)` and `(16)` respectively, and the works cost estimate is defined as being "prepared using prices current at the time the estimate is prepared (or updated)" — i.e. it is a base-date figure by construction, with inflation deliberately held outside it. §2.16 describes the escalation as applied to the cost limit over the run of the project, and the standard's own definition of the *base cost estimate* is that it "is to contain no allowances" for the inflation lines. On the other side of the analysis discipline, the UK practice-information source names time as one of only two external cost drivers: "The state of the construction economy (and the general economy) at the point in time".
- Source: S1 NRM1 2nd ed., RICS, UK, 2012 (effective 1 Jan 2013), §2.4 constituents (13) and (15), §2.16, §1 "Works cost estimate" — https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/nrm_1_order_of_cost_estimating_and_cost_planning_2nd_edition_rics.pdf — T1. S2 RICS *Cost analysis and benchmarking* §2.2.4 — T1 (URL as CM-12).
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: every figure in this file and every rate it accepts
- Confidence: High on the two-line structure (verbatim). Low on any escalation percentage — **no price index has been read by this file**, so no rate in it may be moved in time. `NEEDS VERIFICATION:` a published construction cost index with series name and current value (e.g. a BCIS tender price index entry or the US BLS construction-cost series), so that a dated rate can be brought to a stated base year by an auditable factor rather than by opinion.
- Grid translation: The grid has no time axis at all, so the requirement is a schema field, not a computation: every rate row carries `base_date_year`, `tender_date_year`, `construction_period_months`, and the report refuses `cost_limit_incl_inflation` when any is null. The one place the grid can *help* is the construction-inflation term itself, because duration is geometry-driven: `floor_cycle_time × storeys` is derivable from the plan once `construction-programme.md` CP-* cycle logic is applied, so a deeper plate with more unique modules has a longer programme and therefore a larger construction-inflation exposure. Emit `programme_months_proxy` and attach the inflation line to it.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Double-escalation is the standard error: a rate already quoted "2024 out-turn" gets a further inflation line, and nobody notices because both lines look legitimate. The mirror error is subtler — a cost *limit* escalated to out-turn compared against a competitor's *works cost* at base date, which makes the competitor look 15–25% cheaper with no design difference. Third: a fixed-price tender transfers tender inflation into risk allowance instead (`CM-02`'s line (11)), so a project's inflation exposure can disappear from the inflation line and reappear in contingency without the total moving.

### CM-20 Discount to a base date with a real rate, and show the arithmetic
- Rule: Life-cycle comparison requires present-value arithmetic on a stated real discount rate, with an identical base date and period for every option. Publish the discount factor, the term, and the sum; the option ranking is only valid inside that arithmetic.
- Evidence: The UK standard's own terms: **base date** — "the point in time when the LCC period of analysis starts and from when all future costs are discounted. All relevant costs accrued before the base date but after the start of the study, are deemed to be capital costs"; **discount rate** — "the interest rate used to bring future costs to a comparable time base (base date)"; **present value** — "the present day worth of a future cost discounted at a given interest rate"; **real interest rate** — "the rate adjusted to exclude inflation". The scope statement: "LCC analysis typically uses real costs… If real discount rates are used (**as recommended by BS/ISO 15686-5**) then the discount rate used to bring future costs to a present day base date excludes differential inflation (for example, higher than average energy cost inflation – which may, however, be applied subsequently as part of sensitivity analysis)." And the comparability rule: "It is vital that the base date and the period of analysis from the base date is the same for all options compared." Metric 1 is NPV with the variables printed verbatim as `C` the cost in year n, `q` the discount rate, `d` the expected real discount rate per annum, `n` the number of years between the base date and the occurrence of the cost, `p` the period of analysis; the equation itself is set as an image and **could not be extracted from the file read** → `NEEDS VERIFICATION:` the printed NPV and AEV equations from the live standard. The document's own worked AEV result *is* readable and is decisive as a check: "a cost of 100 units in 25 years' time at an interest rate of 6% will be equivalent to an annual investment of 1.82 units."
- Evidence (worked, `derived`): the standard form `PV = C/(1+d)^n` reproduces that printed result only through the **sinking-fund** form, and it reproduces it exactly: `100 × 0.06/((1.06^25 − 1)) = 100 × 0.06/3.29187 = 1.8227` → **1.82 units** ✓. The capital-recovery form `d/(1−(1+d)^−n)` would give 7.82 and is the wrong reading for a *future single cost*, so the check pins the formula. NPV worked at d = 3.5% over a 30-year period, `derived`: a £500 replacement in year 15 discounts to `500/(1.035^15) = 500/1.6753 = £298.4` and in year 30 to `500/2.8068 = £178.1`. A 60-unit-per-year operating cost held flat for 30 years has present value `60 × (1 − 1.035^−30)/0.035 = 60 × 18.3925 = £1,103.5` — i.e. **more than twice the £500 replacement in year 15**, which is the arithmetic this whole file exists to make visible.
- Source: S3 RICS *Life cycle costing*, practice information, UK, 1st ed. April 2016 (effective 1 July 2016; reissued June 2025), §1.5.4 base date, §1.5.5 real and nominal costs, §1.5.6 and Appendix A Metrics 1 and 2 — https://www.rics.org/content/dam/ricsglobal/documents/standards/Life-cycle-costing_1st-edition.pdf — T1. Arithmetic verified against the document's own printed example (derived).
- Class: STANDARD (the requirements) / FACT (the arithmetic)
- Scope: any whole-life comparison of design options; not a substitute for a business case
- Confidence: High on the definitions, the real-rate recommendation and the base-date rule (verbatim); High on the arithmetic (independently reproduced against the source's printed 1.82 result); Medium on the exact printed equation form, which was unreadable in the copy opened.
- Grid translation: Time and money are both outside the grid, so the grid supplies only the *quantities that enter the cash-flow*: element areas and counts by class, which set the replacement schedule. Register row shape: `{element, quantity_from_tiles, unit_rate_USER, service_life_YEARS_USER_OR_TABLE, replacements_in_period = floor((p − a)/L), cashflow_years[]}`, where `p` is the analysis period and `a` the first replacement age. The one thing the grid must refuse: it may not emit a *net present value* while `unit_rate` is `ABSENT`, because the NPV is then zero for a reason a reader will misread as a cheap design. See `CM-27`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Mixing bases — the standard's prohibition is explicit ("The basis for both the costs and the discount rate should be either nominal or real, but not a mixture"), and the commonest violation is a nominal (escalated) cost stream discounted at a real rate, which inflates every long-life option. Second: a discount rate chosen to favour the answer; at 3.5% a 30-year operating stream is worth 18.4× its annual amount, at 8% only 11.3×, so a low rate always favours the capital-intensive option and the rate is a *value judgement presented as arithmetic*. Third: different base dates across options, which the standard forbids and which a phased design quietly produces.

### CM-21 Declare the analysis period, and treat anything past thirty years as suspect
- Rule: State the period, justify it, and keep it identical across options. Beyond 30 years the published guidance is to proceed with caution, and the public-sector rate schedule itself changes at year 31 — so a 60-year comparison uses a different discount rate in its second half and must say so.
- Evidence: "However, in the majority of studies the LCC period of analysis should be less than any of the periods described above. **There is no recommended method of determining an LCC period of analysis in BS ISO 15686-5: 2008 or in SMLCC.** The use of long analysis periods (more than 30 years) should be treated with caution and with consideration for possible technological, commercial and legal changes." The four competing "lives" the period must stay inside are defined verbatim: **physical life** — "life of a building or an element of a building to the time when physical collapse is possible"; **legal life** — "until the time when it no longer satisfies legal or statutory requirements"; **social life** — "until the time when human desire dictates replacement for reasons other than economic considerations"; **technological life** — "until it is no longer technically superior to alternatives". On the rate change: "In the public sector, the use of the Treasury discount rate changes for calculations beyond 30 years. Clearly, buildings and building components commonly last longer than 30 years and the **HM Treasury discount rates step down from the starting point of 3.5% p.a. to 3% p.a. from years 31 to 75** and further beyond that point." The exception is named too: where LCC sits alongside life-cycle assessment "there may be a long period of analysis as the default is often the entire life of the building to ensure consistency".
- Source: S3 RICS *Life cycle costing*, UK, 1st ed. Apr 2016 — §1.5.3 periods of analysis, §1.5.4 base date — T1 (URL as CM-20). Jurisdiction: United Kingdom public sector.
- Class: STANDARD / FACT (the 3.5% → 3% step)
- Scope: UK public-sector appraisals for the Treasury rates; the 30-year caution is general professional guidance
- Confidence: High (all four quotations read directly). Medium on the currency of the 3.5%/3% figures — the document is dated April 2016 and the Treasury rates are revised, so treat as 2016-vintage. `NEEDS VERIFICATION:` the current HM Treasury Green Book social time preference rate schedule.
- Grid translation: Not representable, and the rule is a schema requirement rather than a computation: `analysis_period_years` and `discount_rate_pct` are mandatory report fields, defaulting to `UNDECLARED`, and any NPV row inherits them. What the grid *does* contribute is the honest reason a long period is dangerous for a design: `change_of_use_readiness` (`LC-04`, `LC-07`, `LC-19`) is a tile-computable property, and a plate that cannot survive one change of use inside 30 years has no business being discounted over 60. Emit the period alongside the plan's own adaptability flags.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Truncation bias is the structural failure: a long-life, capital-intensive option looks bad in a 20-year window and good in a 40-year one, so the period silently chooses the answer — and because the standard admits no method for setting it, the choice is undefendable unless it is *declared and tested*. Second: residual value at the period end. A 30-year study of a 60-year building must handle the unspent life, and the guidance's own words are that the analysis period should be *shorter* than the asset's lives, which makes the terminal-value treatment part of the answer rather than a rounding item (`CM-24`).

### CM-22 Compute replacement counts from service life, and never treat a guarantee as a life
- Rule: The replacement schedule is arithmetic: `replacements = floor((p − a)/L)` for first replacement age `a`, service life `L` and period `p`. Service life is a defined quantity — "period of time after installation during which an asset, or its systems and components, meets or exceeds the performance requirements" — and it is not a warranty, not a maintenance interval and not a manufacturer's best case.
- Evidence: The published handling of manufacturer data is blunt about the gap: data from manufacturers "tends to be compromised by caveats aimed at restricting liability", and the worked illustration is a fan vendor who says "these fans work for years; they come with a 2-year guarantee but providing they are well maintained should run for 8 to 12 years. Some fans are still going after 16 years." The standard's reading of that sentence: "From this comment you could assume that the fan is **unlikely to fail in the first 2 years, is unlikely to last 16 years, and probably has an average life of about 10 years**." So three different numbers in one vendor statement, and the professional judgement is the *distribution*, not the point. And on transfer to a project: "It is important that reference service lives taken from a range of data sources are considered and adjusted to reflect the project conditions. **There is further guidance on use of service lives in BS/ISO 15686-8.**"
- Source: S3 RICS *Life cycle costing*, UK, 1st ed. Apr 2016 — §1 definitions ("Service life"), §4 data sources (manufacturer data, worked fan example), reference to BS/ISO 15686-8 — T1 (URL as CM-20).
- Class: STANDARD (definitions and the requirement to adjust) / FACT (the arithmetic)
- Scope: component-level LCC; the adjustment-to-project-conditions step is where most published life tables fail in practice
- Confidence: High on the fan example and the service-life definition (verbatim). **Low on any element life table: this file has read no published service-life schedule**, so no `L` value may be quoted as sourced. `NEEDS VERIFICATION:` a life table with edition and jurisdiction — candidates are an ISO 15686-2/-8 derived table, a national building-element life register, or a facilities-replacement manual; each must be cited with its own edition.
- Grid translation: The grid's element classes map onto the components that need lives, and the *quantity* side is computable, so the schedule can be generated even while `L` and the rate are user inputs: for each element class emit `quantity_from_tiles`, `expected_first_replacement_year = handover_year + a`, `replacements_in_period`, `discounted_replacement_cost`. Worked with the fan reading above and explicitly `derived` inputs (`L = 10 y, a = 8 y, p = 30 y`): `floor((30 − 8)/10) = 2` replacements, in years 18 and 28 — the first *inside* the period and the second only just, which is how a 30-year window quietly becomes a 2-replacement rather than 3-replacement problem. Changing `p` to 35 changes the count to 3. Sensitivity to the period is therefore a property of the *schedule*, not of the discounting, and the register must show `replacements_in_period` as its own column.
- Exceptions / failure mode: The residual-life error: an element replaced in year 28 of a 30-year period still has 2 years of life unbilled, and the standard's `residual life` ("that life remaining at the end of the LCC period of analysis") and `terminal value` ("the scrap value of a component or asset at the point of its replacement") exist precisely to stop that being hidden. Second: the layering error, which `LC-02` and `LC-14` already own as a design rule — a 15-life finish behind a 60-life wall replaces four times and the wall has to be opened each time; the arithmetic then needs an access cost, not just a replacement cost, and a model that omits it will recommend the trapped option. Third: averaging lives across a range. `L = 10` from a quoted 8–12 is a *mean of a distribution*; a plan sensitive to a single late replacement must carry the band, and where the band is not published the derived `L` is `HEURISTIC`.

### CM-24 State what the life-cycle model owns: capital, replacements, residual and terminal value
- Rule: The investment side of a whole-life model is capital cost *plus* the present value of periodic replacements, *taking into account* terminal and residual values; the savings side is operational and occupancy cost. Declare each of the five in or out by name, because an undeclared exclusion is the usual reason two LCCs of the same building disagree.
- Evidence: Verbatim from the standard's own metric definitions. Metric 3, **net savings**: "The amount invested should include the capital cost, replacement costs, and take into account terminal and residual values. The savings should include reductions in operational and occupancy costs, including for example, reductions in the cost of energy, maintenance, janitorial staff, security, etc." Metric 4, **savings to investment ratio**: "the present value of the reduction in recurrent expenditure (operational and occupancy costs), as a ratio of the capital investment (the capital investment and the present value of periodic replacement costs taking into account terminal and residual values)". The three value terms are separately defined: **residual value** — "the value assigned to an asset at the end of the period of analysis"; **terminal value** — "the scrap value of a component or asset at the point of its replacement"; **residual life** — "that life remaining at the end of the LCC period of analysis". Two further cost families are named and are the ones most often dropped: **hard facilities management costs** — "the cost of necessary replacement, redecoration, repair and corrective, responsive and preventative maintenance necessary for the continued specified functional performance of the asset" — and **through life costs** — "the cost of financing hard and soft facilities management through the life of the asset". A **base case** is required for any comparison: "the existing situation against which improvement options can be compared or a specific solution selected as the benchmark against which other options can be compared." The declaration is a client agreement, not an analyst default: "The definition of the problem in all types of option appraisal is fundamentally important. It is recommended that you record it and agree it with the client. **The agreed definition will include the extent to which capital, maintenance, operation, occupancy, end of life and non-construction costs should be included in the calculation.**" And the exclusion is named as a known practice, not an anomaly: "Operation costs, including energy and cleaning costs, **are often excluded from the scope**". One further alignment requirement, which binds this file back to the capital side: "The capital cost facet of the life cycle cost **should be identical to the capital cost plan**."
- Source: S3 RICS *Life cycle costing*, UK, 1st ed. Apr 2016 — §1 definitions, Appendix A Metrics 3 and 4 — T1 (URL as CM-20).
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: whole-life option appraisal for buildings; the five-term declaration is the transferable part
- Confidence: High (all quoted directly). Medium on the metric formulae themselves, whose printed equations were image-only in the copy read.
- Grid translation: The grid can supply the *quantities* for three of the five terms and nothing for the rest. `capital` ← element areas × user rates; `replacements` ← `CM-22`'s schedule; `residual/terminal value` ← **not representable** (no land market, no valuation), so the mandatory output is `residual_value: EXCLUDED_NOT_REPRESENTABLE` rather than silence, and any option whose advantage depends on residual value must be reported as unsupported. Energy and occupancy cost are computable only to the extent the plan drives them (`EQ-*` solar gain, `SV-*` loads, `HO-*` labour minutes) — the corpus's `EN-04`/`EQ-*` daylight depth and `HO-14` walk-distance metrics are the tile-side inputs to a savings claim, so a savings line must name which of those it came from or be marked `UNATTRIBUTED_TO_GEOMETRY`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: The largest unpriced term in a hotel is not energy but labour, and labour is driven by geometry (`HO-10`, `HO-13`, `HO-14`): a plan that saves 400 m² of BOH walk per shift changes the FTE-per-100-keys arithmetic every day for decades, which dwarfs most envelope decisions. A model that includes energy and omits staffing will therefore rank the compact-services plan wrongly. Second: a positive residual value on a short-life option is an assumption, not a fact, and on a 30-year period for a 60-year building it can be the whole margin of the answer.

### CM-25 Do not rank designs on capital cost, and run the ranking test that replaces it
- Rule: Capital-cost ranking is a known-bad method, not a simplification. Replace it with the discounted comparison and check the ranking direction with the published test: the option with the **lowest life-cycle cost** is the same option as the one with the **highest net savings**, and a ratio above 1.0 is the value-for-money gate. Report the ranking under both, and report how the ranking changes with the discount rate.
- Evidence: The equivalence is stated by the standard: "NS is used to assess savings. A project is considered cost effective if savings outweigh expenditure. **Choosing a project option with highest NS will have the same results as choosing one with lowest LCC.**" And on the ratio: "Savings to investment ratio can be considered a value for money measure where **any ratio exceeding 1.0 is considered value for money**. Options can be assessed in order from highest to lowest SIR." Metric 5 is the discount-rate-free alternative: "Unlike the preceding techniques, which require a discount rate, the IRR represents the rate of interest at which the present value of savings is equal to the present value of the investment (or 0)", and Metric 6, simple payback, "gives a quick appreciation of the viability of the scheme without doing a net present…" (truncated in the copy read). The failure the metrics exist to prevent is visible in the earlier NPV arithmetic (`CM-20`, derived): a £500 capital penalty that buys a £60/yr operating saving is NPV-positive at 3.5% over 30 years (£1,103.5 of savings vs £500 of capital) and would be reversed by any capital-only ranking. Corpus-internal and not restated: `EC-14` requires whole-life reporting wherever geometry changes an operating bill; `DM-10` carries the whole-life comparison principle and its unmet citation — discharged by `S3` here.
- Source: S3 RICS *Life cycle costing*, UK, 1st ed. Apr 2016, Appendix A Metrics 3, 4, 5, 6 — T1 (URL as CM-20). The £500/£60 example is this file's arithmetic (derived), not a source figure.
- Class: STANDARD (the metrics) / DESIGN PRINCIPLE (the prohibition)
- Scope: any design option set where geometry moves an operating bill — which is most architectural decisions
- Confidence: High on the quoted equivalence and the SIR > 1.0 gate. High on the derived illustration (arithmetic reproducible).
- Grid translation: The register must emit the option pair, never a single design: `{option_a, option_b, capital_delta_from_tiles, operating_delta_attributable_to_geometry, discount_rate, period, npv_delta, sir, ranking}` plus `ranking_reversal: <discount rate at which the order flips>`. A reversal rate is a genuinely useful tile-free output because it is rate-*independent* — the break-even discount rate is a property of the cash-flow shape. Where the geometry delta cannot be attributed to a named tile metric, the row is `UNATTRIBUTED` and must not enter the ranking.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Three. (1) The capital penalty lands in one budget and the operating saving in another, so the arithmetic is right and the decision still fails; the report should say which purse pays. (2) SIR and NPV can disagree on *scale* (a small efficient scheme can beat a large one on ratio and lose on absolute savings), so ordering by SIR alone under-selects. (3) Simple payback, which the standard lists as a quick metric, ignores everything after the payback year and therefore always prefers short-life cheap options — it may be reported but never as the ranking.

### CM-26 Run one-at-a-time sensitivity on the four terms that actually move the answer
- Rule: Report the sensitivity before the recommendation. For a cost answer the dominant terms are the rate's **base year**, its **region**, its **scope/area basis**, and the **discount rate** for anything life-cycle; the design's own geometry is usually the smallest of the five. Emit a table showing each term perturbed alone, with the verdict column, and wire the model so one rate entry propagates.
- Evidence: The standard prescribes the mechanism, not just the mood: "In the majority of LCC models, variations in the discount rate can be analysed by `what if?` questioning of the results by changing the discount rate. **Ideally models should be set up so that a change to a single entry for the discount rate acts across all affected calculations.**" It also states that "as the period lengthens, these predictions become more uncertain, because of the various assumptions made", that differential inflation "may, however, be applied subsequently as part of sensitivity analysis", that "Risks and uncertainty are also explicitly considered" in the parent standard, and that the report must show "the basis for present value calculations and the source of the data", including "the discount rate, and any data best supplied by the client, e.g. fuel tariffs". On the capital side the same anti-single-percentage position appears: "It is recommended that risk allowances are **not** a standard percentage, but a properly considered…" (S1 §2.15.4, sentence truncated in the copy read). The replacement-count term is called out as its own sensitivity axis: the analysis should show where the "cycles of replacement" change the answer.
- Source: S3 RICS *Life cycle costing*, UK, 1st ed. Apr 2016 — §1.5.5, §1.5.6, §1.8 report contents, §4 — T1 (URL as CM-20). S1 NRM1 2nd ed. §2.15.4 — T1 (URL as CM-01). The worked table is `## Sensitivity table (worked hotel)` below, derived.
- Class: STANDARD (the requirement) / DESIGN PRINCIPLE (which four terms)
- Scope: every cost output this file emits; a cost answer without a sensitivity table is incomplete, not merely thin
- Confidence: High (verbatim requirement). High on the four-term selection as *this file's* claim; Medium as a generalisation — it is an ordering, not a measurement.
- Grid translation: The grid's sensitivity is the only kind that needs no rate, and it should be published alongside: perturb each geometric quantity singly and report the cost *quantity* movement — `perimeter_m ± 1 tile band`, `core_share ±3 pp`, `unique_module_count ±6`, `storey_height ±100 mm`, `storeys ±1`. Because these are rate-free, they survive a missing rate and remain comparable across revisions. Emission keys: `sensitivity_variable`, `perturbation`, `delta_quantity`, `delta_cost_if_rate_supplied`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: One-at-a-time understates interaction: raising plan depth lowers perimeter *and* raises span, and the two do not cancel linearly (`CM-08` × `CM-09`). Second: sensitivity ranges chosen to flatter — a ±5% band on a rate whose own class tolerance is −20/+30 (`CM-18`) is theatre. Third, and the most common in a design tool: reporting a sensitivity on quantities while the real uncertainty is in one unsupplied rate, which produces a confident-looking answer whose only free parameter is the number nobody has.

### CM-27 Emit a fixed flag string when a rate is absent, and never let absence read as cheap
- Rule: A missing rate produces a named flag in the cost column, not a zero, not an estimate, and not silence. The flag states the quantity that *was* computed, the field that is missing, and the single input that would close the row.
- Evidence: `IN-CORPUS REQUIREMENT, arithmetic reason given.` The grid has no currency by construction — `uncertainty.md` UN-01 records cost as a not-representable property — so every money cell in this file is an external join. An empty join rendered as `0` or as a blank is worse than an error: in a ranked comparison the zero-cost design wins, and in a total it understates by the whole omitted scope. The professional sources make the same demand in their own register: the LCC report must include "the basis for present value calculations and the source of the data" (S3 §1.8); the cost analysis must not isolate cost "from the key features of that building i.e. procurement route, contract design solution, scope of works, outline specification", because "poor quality information and/or inaccurate information is likely to negatively impact on any future work which is based on the cost analysis" (S2 §2.2.3); and NRM1 requires that the estimate's assumptions be reported to the employer as a defined deliverable (S1 §2.1.3). Corpus conventions this flag joins: `validation.md` VA-* three-valued verdicts (`PASS | FAIL | UNKNOWN — not representable / data missing`) and `economics.md` EC-21's false-efficiency checks.
- Source: S3 RICS *Life cycle costing*, UK, 1st ed. Apr 2016, §1.8 — T1 (URL as CM-20). S2 RICS *Cost analysis and benchmarking*, UK, 2nd ed. Aug 2024, §2.2.3 — T1 (URL as CM-12). S1 NRM1 2nd ed. §2.1.3 — T1 (URL as CM-01). `uncertainty.md` UN-01 and `validation.md` VA-* — internal.
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT
- Scope: every cost-bearing cell in the report; every derived metric in the register
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: Fixed vocabulary, exactly five forms and no others. `NOT_COMPUTABLE — rate absent (CM-27): quantity 1,484 m² computed, facade_rate_PER_M2_USER_ABSENT; supply facade_rate_per_m2 to close`. `RATE_INCOMPLETE — comparison blocked (CM-04): base_year null`. `HEURISTIC — no source (CM-13): decomposition shown, magnitude unverified`. `UNATTRIBUTED_TO_GEOMETRY (CM-24): operating delta not linked to a named tile metric`. `EXCLUDED_NOT_REPRESENTABLE (CM-24): residual value`. Each carries the computed quantity so the row is still auditable, and no flag may appear in a column that feeds a ranking.
- Exceptions / failure mode: The degenerate case is a report where every row is flagged — that is a tool failure, not a design failure, and the correct output is a refusal plus the list of the N inputs needed. The opposite failure is more dangerous: a plausible-looking default rate hard-coded "for illustration" that then propagates into a per-key figure and gets quoted as a result. This file permits no default rates; every currency value in it is either cited with a base date or absent.

### CM-28 Publish the cost-quantity register the plan engine owns, and the rate join it does not
- Rule: The engine computes quantities and counts only; an external rate table joins them to money. Declare the boundary as a fixed register with named metric keys, and report the plan-side context the standard itself requires — the items that make a cost analysis reusable — because those are all tile-derivable.
- Evidence: The UK cost-analysis standard enumerates the context that must accompany an analysis; items 10–16 of that list read: "The GIA and the NIA, or IPMS measures (as applicable)", "The wall to floor ratio", "The extent of circulation space", "Typical room sizes (note: this is especially useful for accommodation-based projects such as hotels and student accommodation)", "Number of storeys", "Whether or not the project includes for basement works", "What assumptions, if any, have been made regarding the above" — and the same section states the principle behind the register: "For a cost analysis to be effective it is important that the cost of a building is not isolated from the key features of that building", and that a contract sum only becomes a model when it is "broken down, represented in a meaningful way and attached to a scope of works". The element families to break down by are fixed by NRM1's group elements: "Facilitating works; Substructure; Superstructure; Internal finishes; Fittings, furnishings and equipment; Services; Prefabricated buildings and building units; Work to existing buildings; and External works", with the second group's sub-elements printed as "2.1 Frame; 2.2 Upper floors; 2.3 Roof; 2.4 Stairs and ramps; 2.5 External walls; 2.6 Windows and external doors", and each component row carrying explicit **Included / Excluded** measurement columns.
- Source: S2 RICS *Cost analysis and benchmarking*, UK, 2nd ed. Aug 2024, §2.2.3 and §3.1.1 required-context list — T1 (URL as CM-12). S1 NRM1 2nd ed., group elements 0–8 and element 2 sub-element list — T1 (URL as CM-01).
- Class: STANDARD (the required context list and element taxonomy) / ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT (the quantity/rate boundary)
- Scope: the whole file's interface with the host engine
- Confidence: High (both lists read verbatim). Note the correspondence is structural, not nominal: the corpus's element names are its own, so a mapping row is required and is given below.
- Grid translation: Metric keys the engine can emit today, all rate-free — `gross_internal_area_m2`, `net_internal_area_m2` (EC-01's numbers, 4 tiles = 1 m²), `wall_to_floor_ratio` (CM-06's `perimeter_m × storey_height_m ÷ floor_area_m2`, i.e. the standard's own named index), `circulation_share_pct`, `core_share_pct` (CM-11), `mean_room_size_m2` / `key_area_m2` with the room-size histogram (CM-14), `storey_count`, `basement_levels_declared` (CM-13), `span_grid_m` (CM-08), `plan_depth_distribution_tiles` (CM-09), `floor_to_floor_height_m` (declared, CM-10), `unique_module_count` / `repetition_index` (CM-14), `tiles_per_key` (CM-17). Report block: `cost_context: {area_basis, wall_to_floor_ratio, circulation_share, mean_room_size, storeys, basement: USER_DECLARED, assumptions[]}` then `quantity_register: {element, sub_element, quantity, unit, source: TILES|USER}` then `rate_join: {element → rate, currency, base_year, region} | NOT_SUPPLIED` then `verdict: PASS | FAIL | NOT_COMPUTABLE (CM-27)`. `assumptions[]` is mandatory, per item 16.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Two mirror-image errors. (1) The engine inventing a rate to fill the join — every default rate is a fabrication with a decimal point in it, and this file permits none. (2) The engine refusing to publish quantities because money is unavailable, which throws away the only part of the cost answer that is genuinely design-owned: a wall-to-floor ratio of 0.36 against a budget of 0.28 is a finding with no currency in it. Second-tier failure: mapping the corpus's element names onto NRM1's group elements silently, when e.g. NRM1 puts "External walls" and "Windows" inside Superstructure while the corpus treats the whole envelope as one object — the mapping row must be printed, not assumed.

## Weak or contested

1. **No US rate in this file.** GSA construction-cost pages and the FTA cost-estimation guidance both refused retrieval (HTTP 403 / not parseable), so the only US money figures are `EC-19`'s per-key ladder, used by ID. A `$/SF` elemental profile for the US remains unread. Consequence: the rungs are UK-described and US-named throughout, and every comparison table says so.
2. **BCIS Online Cost Guide not read.** Subscription-gated. The elemental percentages in `CM-12` come from RICS S2's nine-project table, whose **building types are not labelled** and whose **price base date is not printed** in the passage read. The ranges are therefore valid as a dispersion statement only.
3. **The single largest weak input in the file is S4.** It is undated, South African, priced in Rand, self-published by one practice, and Tier 4. Its *arithmetic* (the 400 m² two-shape case, the perimeter ratios, the fullness example) is reproducible and is used; its *magnitudes* — setting out +50 %, excavation +6–20 %, drainage +25 %, perimeter 20–30 % of total cost, circulation 22–32 % — are single-source, unverifiable and must not be applied as design targets. Every rule using one carries `HEURISTIC` or names it in the Evidence line.
4. **The storey-height cost effect is not verified.** `CM-10`'s 3.125 % is arithmetic on a declared dimension; the 0.6–0.9 % of total cost that follows from it is the arithmetic *times* S4's unverified perimeter share. It is printed once, with its dependency named, and quarantined here.
5. **Basement premium has no magnitude in this file.** `CM-13` states the decomposition and the £83/m² shallow line, then refuses the "basements cost N× more" claim because no source read supports a number.
6. **Slab-span and repetition cost curves are `HEURISTIC` by admission** (`CM-08`, `CM-14`). The L² mechanics are certain; the money is not, and neither rule emits a percentage cost figure.
7. **Two estimate-class systems conflict and are not reconciled** (`CM-18`, and the tolerance table): ASTM-as-tabulated-by-S6 versus AACE-as-tabulated-by-S7 differ by roughly one class at the coarse end, and S6's prose differs from S6's own table. Both printed; no average taken — the corpus's standing rule for conflicts (`coverage-map.md` §4).
8. **The NPV and AEV equations themselves are unreadable in the copy of S3 obtained**: Appendix A sets them as images. The variable list and the printed 1.82-unit result *were* readable, and the sinking-fund form reproduces the printed answer exactly, so the formula used is the one that survives the source's own check — but the printed equation remains outstanding.
9. **EN 16646 is not cited anywhere in this file.** Searched for in S2 and S3 and not found; no claim here rests on it (`CM-23`). Its designation and current status are unverified.
10. **ISO edition drift.** S3 (2016) cites BS ISO 15686-5:2008; a 2017 ISO edition exists per search metadata only, and was not opened. Any number in this file traceable to "15686-5" is traceable to the 2008 edition *as cited by S3*, not read at source.
11. **Treasury discount rates are 2016-vintage** as printed in S3 (3.5 % stepping to 3.0 % for years 31–75) and are revised periodically; they may be superseded.
12. **Currency glyph loss in extraction.** The `£` sign rendered as a replacement character in the S2 text dump; the `490 m² × 83 = 40,670` triple confirms magnitude and unit, and S2 is a UK document, so the symbol is restored as `£` — recorded here because every other figure in this file came through unambiguous.
13. **No type-specific elemental profile for hotel, office, residential mid-rise or hospital exists in this file.** The `## Elemental split ranges` table leaves those rows empty rather than filling them from the unlabelled UK sample.
14. **Service lives: none sourced.** `CM-22` supplies the arithmetic, the definition and the vendor-data caution, and no `L` value; the 10-year fan life is S3's illustrative reading of a manufacturer's remark, not a table entry.
15. **`DM-10`'s outstanding citation is now dischargeable**: its `Source` line reads "need URL" and S3 is the document to put there. That edit is owed to `decision-making.md`, not made here (`coverage-map.md` §1 forbids restating an old rule; this records where its citation lives).

Three audit conclusions. (1) The **geometry band (CM-06…CM-11) is the most type-portable part of the file** because it is arithmetic on quantities the grid already computes; only its *magnitudes* are type- and source-bound. (2) The **elemental and rate bands (CM-12, and the money columns throughout) are the least portable** — they carry one unlabelled UK sample plus one undated South African practice, which is thin for four building types across three currencies. (3) The **life-cycle band (CM-19…CM-25) is fully sourced from a single jurisdiction document (S3, UK 2016)**; the arithmetic transfers, the Treasury rates and the SMLCC framing do not, and every rule in the band names its jurisdiction on the `Source` line for exactly that reason. `HO-*`'s own audit makes the matching warning about industry surveys, and this file inherits it.
