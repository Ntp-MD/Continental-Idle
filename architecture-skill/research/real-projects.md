# Research file 02 — Real-world architectural projects (evidence base for the design agent)

Scope: rules extracted from built, documented projects across eight building types, plus a handful
of operational planning guides where a project alone cannot carry a number. The question asked of
every case is *why the plan is like that and what it cost*, not what it looks like. Style, image and
award commentary are excluded. Numbers are quoted only where a retrievable source states them;
where the figure came from a vendor or trade page it is marked T4 and the confidence is capped.

Host grid this file is written against: `1 tile = 0.5 m`, one 2D grid per floor, tile states only
`walkable | blocked | door`; wall = 1 blocked tile; doors are runs of door tiles; rooms are
flood-fills of walkable tiles typed by fixture tags; A\* octile movement, diagonals only where both
corner neighbours are open; vertical links are lift/stair portals with queues; no section, no
heights; access control by tags (public / guest / staff / service). Conversion table used
throughout: 1 m = 2 tiles, 1 m² = 4 tiles, 1,000 m² = 4,000 tiles, 1.5 m = 3 tiles, 2.0 m = 4
tiles, 3.0 m = 6 tiles, 3.5 m = 7 tiles, 12 m = 24 tiles, 22 m = 44 tiles. Because there is no
section, every "daylight", "height" and "clear-height" claim below is re-expressed as a *distance
from a facade tile* or a *plan-area budget*, which is what the agent can actually measure.

## Rules

### RP-01 Zone every floor into public / private / service before drawing a single room
- Rule: On each floor assign every room to one of three zones — public (uncontrolled entry), private (needs a guest/resident/patient/employee tag), service (staff + goods only) — and only then place rooms. Any door run that joins a public room directly to a service room is rejected unless a buffer room of the private or transitional type sits between them.
- Evidence: Marina Bay Sands puts the casino, convention centre, shops and museums in a street-level podium and the ~2,600 guest rooms in three towers above, so a guest never crosses the gambling floor to reach the lift (Safdie Architects project page; the practice describes the "seamlessness of indoor and outdoor public space" as the podium's organising intent, with the guest volume held above it). Fogo Island Inn splits the same way inside one building: a two-storey west-to-east volume carries the public rooms and a four-storey south-west-to-north-east volume carries the remaining public spaces "and all the guest rooms" (Saunders Architecture, via gooood). Sunshine Coast University Hospital puts retail, cafés and seating bays on a "main street" and the clinical units behind it (ArchitectureAu). Gold Coast University Hospital's seven-storey atrium is the public spine with wards off it (Hassell). The pattern is not universal by type — the Unité puts shops *inside* the dwelling volume on its 7th/8th-floor "interior street" (Fondation Le Corbusier), and Oodi deliberately makes the whole ground floor public "zero-threshold" space (ALA Architects) — which is exactly why the zone assignment must be a decision per floor, not a default.
- Source: https://www.safdiearchitects.com/projects/marina-bay-sands-hotel-and-skypark — T3; https://www.gooood.cn/en/fogo-island-inn-by-saunders.htm — T3; https://architectureau.com/articles/sunshine-coast-university-hospital/ — T3; https://archipro.com.au/project/gold-coast-university-hospital-qld-sth — T3; https://ala.fi/work/helsinki-central-library/ — T3; https://www.fondationlecorbusier.fr/en/work-architecture/achievements-unite-dhabitation-marseille-france-1945-1952/ — T3
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High (five independent types show the same tripartite zoning; the exceptions are documented and deliberate)
- Grid translation: Store `zone(public|private|service)` per room tile-set, then compute `bad_crossings = count(door runs whose two neighbouring rooms have incompatible zones)`. Target 0 for `public↔service`. Publish per floor `zone_share = tiles_in_zone / floor_tiles` and require the agent to state a reason when a floor has one zone only. Tag check: a room reachable from an exterior door by a path that never passes a `private` or `service` door is de-facto public — recompute zones from the graph rather than trusting the label.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Small single-function buildings (a shop, a cabin, a branch clinic) where the whole floor is one zone — declare it, don't invent a service zone. Symptom of ignoring the rule: a "hotel" whose only route from entrance to guest door passes the kitchen, or a hospital ward entered from the delivery yard; on this grid it appears as guest traffic traversing `service`-tagged rooms.

### RP-02 Give the street floor the public programme and stack privacy upward
- Rule: For any building with more than three floors of occupancy, place arrival, eating, shopping, gathering and shared amenities on the lowest one or two floors; place sleeping, quiet work and clinical rooms on upper floors; place heavy/dirty service at the bottom or the edge. Record the publicness gradient per floor and defend any inversion.
- Evidence: Oodi's ground floor is the "living room"/event square with café and shop, the intermediate level holds workshops, recording and editing studios and a makerspace, and the top floor is the "calm and contemplative" reading room "floating above the busy central Helsinki" — a three-step publicness gradient over 17,100 m² (ALA Architects). Unité d'Habitation puts its shop street at the 7th/8th floor level, which inverts the rule deliberately because the building *is* the neighbourhood and the street had to sit at the middle of the dwelling stack where both halves of the duplexes reach it in one storey (Fondation Le Corbusier). The Interlace rejects "a cluster of isolated, vertical towers" in favour of a "vertical village of both shared and private outdoor spaces on multiple levels" (OMA) — again a gradient, but distributed rather than bottom-loaded.
- Source: https://ala.fi/work/helsinki-central-library/ — T3; https://www.fondationlecorbusier.fr/en/work-architecture/achievements-unite-dhabitation-marseille-france-1945-1952/ — T3; https://www.oma.com/projects/the-interlace — T3
- Class: BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High as a default, Medium as an absolute (Unité is a documented inversion that works)
- Grid translation: Compute `publicness(f) = public_tiles(f) / total_tiles(f)` for each floor and require it to be non-increasing with floor index, or `min(publicness) ≥ 0.35` on floor 1. Where the brief demands a mid-level public band (Unité pattern), the check becomes: `every private floor is at most 1 storey from a floor with publicness ≥ 0.30` — i.e. no resident climbs more than one level to reach the street.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Single-storey civic/retail (no gradient to test); factories where the "public" is a viewing gallery above production (Gläserne Manufaktur, Dresden); schools where the community rooms want ground access for out-of-hours use. Symptom: lifts queued at every floor because the only café is on the top floor.

### RP-03 Plan people-arrival, goods-arrival and patient-arrival as three separate entries
- Rule: Give the building at least two, normally three, distinct entry door runs — occupants, goods/services, and (for care/health types) assisted or clinical arrival — and make each entry the head of its own path network so no route needs the other's corridor.
- Evidence: Fogo Island Inn's site logic is that the building "hits the land directly without impacting the adjacent rocks, lichens and berries", which forced the services and circulation to be resolved away from the guest arrival experience (Saunders Architecture). Gold Coast and Sunshine Coast universities hospitals both separate the "busy and active" public street from the clinical and service behind it, with a nine-storey public carpark placed along the western boundary as an acoustic and visual shield for the hospital precinct (ArchitectureAu). Kansai Airport Terminal separates the travelling public on the "open departures level" from the ramp-side operations reached through the 42 gate "wings" (Renzo Piano Building Workshop). Warehouse guidance is the crudest statement of the same rule: dock doors are sized by throughput at "1 dock per 1,000–1,500 m² of floor area", with a truck apron of "minimum 12 m for rigid trucks; 18–22 m for articulated semi-trailers" — i.e. goods need their own threshold plus a stand-off depth (PEB Steel layout guide).
- Source: https://www.gooood.cn/en/fogo-island-inn-by-saunders.htm — T3; https://architectureau.com/articles/sunshine-coast-university-hospital/ — T3; https://www.rpbw.com/project/kansai-international-airport-terminal — T3; https://pebsteel.com/en/warehouse-design-guide/ — T4
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High for hospitals, hotels, offices, retail, industrial; Medium for small residential
- Grid translation: Require `count(exterior door runs) ≥ 2` for any floor set over 4,000 tiles (1,000 m²) and ≥ 3 above 40,000 tiles (10,000 m²). Tag each entry `public|guest|service` and verify with a path test: A\* from a `service` entry reaches store/plant/kitchen without traversing a `public` room; A\* from the `public` entry never enters a `service` room. Where the host has no site, express the apron as a 24–44-tile `walkable` stand-off band tagged `yard` in front of service doors, kept free of rooms.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Domestic-scale and small care buildings where one door with a bell is correct (Maggie's deliberately rejects institutional planning). Symptom: one shared door, and the flood-fill shows the delivery route through the lobby as the shortest path for staff, producing tag conflicts at every shift change.

### RP-04 Size and site the vertical core from the walk to the furthest door, not from the façade
- Rule: After the plate shape is chosen, place the lift/stair core so that the worst-case path from the core to any occupied room door is inside a stated budget, and provide the core capacity the room count demands. Add a second core or split the core before exceeding the budget.
- Evidence: Hotel planning guidance states "one station per 75 guest rooms" for lift provision, corridor clear widths "typically 1.5 to 1.8 m", and net-to-gross of 65–72 % for double-loaded slabs (Archgyan hotel design guide; T4, so treated as a range not a law). The Edge solved an employer consolidation problem with "exposed lift cores" and a full-height atrium that carries social circulation visibly while the cores do the work (PLP Architecture). Bullitt Center put its stairs in the glazed north-west corner precisely "to encourage usage", trading a corner of leasable area for a circulation feature people will actually use (Miller Hull, via Studio Matrx guide). Sendai Mediatheque dissolves the core altogether into "a forest of 13 non-uniform tubes" carrying lifts, services and daylight, so that functions could be "freely distributed throughout the open areas between the tubes" (Architectuul; Toyo Ito).
- Source: https://archgyan.com/how-to-design-a-hotel/ — T4; https://www.plparchitecture.com/projects/the-edge — T3; https://www.studiomatrx.org/guides/bullitt-center — T4; https://architectuul.com/architecture/sendai-mediatheque — T3
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: Medium-High for the principle; Low-Medium for the specific numbers (T4 sources)
- Grid translation: For each floor compute `d_max = max over rooms of A*(core_portal, room_door_tile)` in tile-steps, and `queue_pressure = rooms_served_per_core`. Budgets to test and publish: guest floors `d_max ≤ 40 tiles (20 m)` and ≤ 75 rooms per lift portal; hospital clinical floors `d_max ≤ 30 tiles (15 m)` from the bed lift to any bed-space door; office `d_max ≤ 60 tiles (30 m)`. If a budget fails, split the core before lengthening the corridor, because corridor length is charged twice (area and travel).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Deliberately coreless or core-dissolved plans (Sendai, trading efficiency for spatial freedom); single-storey buildings where "vertical" is irrelevant; detention or sterile-zone plans where long controlled walks are the point. Symptom: a 120-room floor with one lift portal and a 60-tile corridor — the queue model shows waiting time growing faster than population.

### RP-05 Fix the repeating room module first, then let structure, façade and stacks follow it
- Rule: For any type with repeated occupied cells (hotel, residential, ward, classroom, cell, dorm), declare the module in tiles (width × depth), the number of modules per floor, and the stacking rule for wet/utility zones before drawing. Do not tune a cell by eye; tune the module.
- Evidence: Guest-room modules are planned around "3.6 m or more" with "widths of 3.3 to 3.8 m" and structural grids "often spanning 7.0 to 8.5 m", so that "guestrooms can be arranged efficiently between columns" (Hotel Development Guide, structural planning). Unité d'Habitation's dwellings are assembled from a repeated first module that "brings together the entrance, the hallway, the kitchen and the living room", with many units "on two floors connected by a staircase" — the repetition is what lets a 135 m × 24 m × 56 m block hold 337 dwellings (Fondation Le Corbusier, which also gives accommodation for 1,600 inhabitants). Paimio's patient rooms were standardised "planned for two people" with the sun balcony as part of the module (PMC, *Humanizing the hospital*). Habitat 67 pushed it to the limit: 365 prefabricated modules producing 158 residences, where the modules also "participate as load-carrying members" (Safdie Architects).
- Source: https://hoteldevelopmentguide.com/hotel-structural-design/ — T4; https://www.fondationlecorbusier.fr/en/work-architecture/achievements-unite-dhabitation-marseille-france-1945-1952/ — T3; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2917967/ — T2; https://www.safdiearchitects.com/projects/habitat-67 — T3
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: hotel, residential, hospital, school, detention
- Confidence: High for the method; Medium for the metric ranges (T4 for the hotel grid)
- Grid translation: A 3.3–3.8 m room width is 7–8 tiles; a 7.0–8.5 m structural bay is 14–17 tiles, so a double-loaded hotel slab reads as `8 tiles room + 3 tiles corridor + 8 tiles room = 19 tiles (9.5 m) minimum depth`. Emit the module as `{cell_w, cell_d, cells_per_floor, stack_rule}` and validate: every repeated cell has identical tile dimensions (±0), wet cells (fixture tag `sink|bath|kitchen`) are directly above/below each other or carry an explicit `riser` tag, and no cell is narrower than 7 tiles.
- Exceptions / failure mode: One-off civic and laboratory plans, and Maggie's-style briefs that explicitly reject "area schedules and data sheets" (Rogers, Architects Journal). Symptom: cells of eleven different sizes on one floor, unbuildable risers, and a façade rhythm that cannot be repeated — the agent also loses the ability to check the plan by module count.

### RP-06 Publish a circulation-and-core area budget per floor and measure the result
- Rule: Before designing a floor, state the allowed share of floor tiles for circulation, cores and common services; after designing, compute the achieved share and reconcile. Never accept an unmeasured circulation figure.
- Evidence: Double-loaded hotel slabs are reported at 65–72 % net-to-gross, and single-loaded slabs are said to lose a further 10–15 points of efficiency because the same rooms are served by twice the corridor (Archgyan; T4). Hotel development guidance states the metric plainly: "the relationship between gross floor area and net revenue-producing area is one of the most critical metrics in hotel development" and warns that "a five percent miscalculation in buildable efficiency at this stage can permanently distort return on cost" (Hotel Development Guide, architectural planning). Logistics is planned by the same arithmetic with explicit shares: bulk storage 50–65 %, receiving 8–12 %, dispatch 8–12 %, pick area 5–10 %, office/welfare/plant 5–10 % (PEB Steel guide; T4).
- Source: https://archgyan.com/how-to-design-a-hotel/ — T4; https://hoteldevelopmentguide.com/architectural-planning/ — T4; https://pebsteel.com/en/warehouse-design-guide/ — T4
- Class: HEURISTIC
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: Medium (multiple agreeing trade sources; denominators differ, no T1 source retrieved)
- Grid translation: Classify every tile as `room | corridor | core | service | void` and report `efficiency = room_tiles / floor_tiles`. Budgets to test: guest/residential floor 0.60–0.72; office open plan 0.75–0.85 excluding the atrium; hospital clinical 0.55–0.70; warehouse floor 0.85–0.95 of movement+storage. Always publish the numerator and denominator so the number is comparable; flag any floor below 0.50 as "corridor-dominated" and re-cut the plate.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Fluid-plan civic floors (Sendai, Oodi) where the "circulation" is the programme; a single-storey retail park; a declared flat-gradient museum floor plate. Symptom: a plan that reads beautifully and tests as 45 % net, which no client brief supports.

### RP-07 Where operational traffic is significant, build a second circulation network for staff and goods
- Rule: In any building whose service trips per day scale with room or bed count, provide a parallel staff/goods route — separate corridor, separate stair or lift portal, separate landing — so guests, patients and public never share the route of linen, waste, food or equipment.
- Evidence: Hospitality practice treats back-of-house as a planning subject in its own right: kitchens, laundries, storage, receiving, trash, housekeeping and front-office support are located so they stay "hidden from the hotel guest rooms", trucks must move "without disrupting the guest parking", and the layout must ensure "clear separation of incoming and outgoing goods"; linen is moved by "a linen chute that connects to every floor in the service elevator landing", and staff need "dedicated break areas" (Studio Puisto, *Designing Back-of-House Spaces for Hotels*). The hotel guides put BOH at 15–25 % of gross area (Archgyan). Fogo Island Inn puts its reception, restaurant, kitchen, laundry, "storage rooms and the mechanical installations" on the lowest level, with activity rooms and suites above and "all the guest rooms" in the taller of its two volumes — a section zoning of service, public, private (Saunders Architecture, via gooood). In healthcare, the "main street … lined with retail outlets, service providers, cafe facilities" at Sunshine Coast University Hospital is the public face behind which clinical and service movement is separated (ArchitectureAu).
- Source: https://studiopuisto.fi/hospitality-tomorrow-10/ — T4; https://archgyan.com/how-to-design-a-hotel/ — T4; https://www.gooood.cn/en/fogo-island-inn-by-saunders.htm — T3; https://architectureau.com/articles/sunshine-coast-university-hospital/ — T3
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: hotel, hospital, office, retail, school, industrial
- Confidence: High (the principle recurs across all operational types); Medium for the 15–25 % figure
- Grid translation: Require a `service`-tagged walkable sub-graph that (a) touches every service room, (b) contains a portal on every floor served by the rooms it moves between, and (c) intersects the `public` sub-graph only at nodes tagged `transfer` (a landing, a chute, a servery). Measure `service_share = service_tiles / floor_tiles`, target 0.15–0.25 of gross on hotel/healthcare floors. Report `guest_intrusion = count(public paths whose shortest route crosses a service room)` — the target is zero.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Below roughly 20–30 rooms, or in a single-storey clinic, a second network costs more corridor than the traffic justifies; the honest answer is one corridor plus a declared service window. Symptom: housekeeping carts queued in the guest lobby, or a service route that terminates because the agent drew one network with two labels.

### RP-08 Put the room that people sleep or recover in on the exterior edge; corridors take the middle
- Rule: For long-occupancy rooms (bedroom, patient room, dormitory, classroom, cellular office), require an exterior facade tile or a courtyard/void tile on the room's boundary; place corridors and services on the interior side. Verify with a distance-from-facade cap.
- Evidence: Paimio Sanatorium's patient wing was oriented with "south-facing sun balconies", the units "oriented directly southward" for light exposure, with "draft proof" windows and daily air access treated as treatment, not comfort (PMC, *Humanizing the hospital*). Gold Coast University Hospital's outcome data is the strongest modern justification: 175,000 m² planned around a "seven-storey atrium [that] runs through the building, drawing daylight down through every floor", with single-occupancy rooms credited for reducing ward-to-ward patient transfers "by more than 70 per cent" and average stays falling "from just over four days to under three" (Hassell/ArchiPro). Commerzbank's triangular plan with a central atrium that "at nine different levels … opens up to one of the three sides, forming large sky gardens" exists so that "open areas allow more natural light in the building" for a 109,200 m², 56-storey workforce (Wikipedia/Foster + Partners).
- Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2917967/ — T2; https://archipro.com.au/project/gold-coast-university-hospital-qld-sth — T3; https://www.fosterandpartners.com/projects/commerzbank-headquarters/ — T3 (page body not retrievable; figures cross-checked at https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commerzbank_Tower — T4)
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: hospital, residential, hotel, school, office
- Confidence: Medium-High (three types agreeing on the edge-room rule; **the GCUH outcome figures in
  Evidence are operator/architect self-reports with no study design, comparator or confound control -
  they are advocacy-side corroboration of intent, not measured effects, and they do not raise this
  entry's confidence, they only illustrate it. The rule stands on the daylight/sightline geometry,
  which needs none of them. See *Weak or contested*, "Outcome claims from promotional project
  reporting", which caps this entry at the same level**)
- Grid translation: Compute per room `facade_tiles` and `min_depth_to_facade` (tiles). Rule: every room whose fixture tag implies > 4 h occupancy must have `facade_tiles ≥ 2` (1.0 m of glazable edge) and `min_depth_to_facade ≤ 24 tiles (12 m)`. For open plans, cap the "daylight-deprived" interior area at ≤ 35 % of floor tiles; count voids and atria as facades (a room facing a 4-tile-wide void earns the credit). Publish the achieved distribution, not just the average.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Interior support rooms (stores, plant, changing, sterilisation, archives, BOH), which want to be deep — this is where the leftover interior tiles go; below-ground or windowless types (detention, records vaults). Symptom: a plan that is 60 % interior rooms and the agent has no tiles left to put the corridor in.

### RP-09 Break a deep plan into fingers or clusters and pierce it with light wells
- Rule: When the required plate depth exceeds roughly 20 tiles (10 m) of continuously occupied space, subdivide the plan into single-corridor "fingers" or L-shaped clusters, and insert voids (light wells) at intervals so no occupied room is more than a bounded distance from a void or facade.
- Evidence: Sunshine Coast University Hospital's six-storey deep-plan West Building is organised as "L-shaped units" forming "twinned, narrow, single-corridor 'fingers'" pierced by "four light wells rising through the full height of the building", with a north–south axis "anchored by the green spine of the major open courtyard that divides the hospital into two principal blocks" (ArchitectureAu). The narrow-corridor choice is itself the trade-off: single-corridor fingers admit daylight and shorten wayfinding at the cost of more external wall and more travel per bed.
- Source: https://architectureau.com/articles/sunshine-coast-university-hospital/ — T3
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: hospital, school, office, hotel, retail
- Confidence: Medium-High (one project documented in detail; consistent with GCUH and Commerzbank strategies)
- Grid translation: Cap `double_loaded_corridor_depth` at 40 tiles (20 m) total; beyond that, split the plate. Require `max(min_distance_to(facade_or_void_tile)) ≤ 24 tiles (12 m)` over all occupied tiles, and count voids: `void_share ≥ 0.04` of floor tiles per 12 m of plate depth. Model a light well as a blocked-tile cluster tagged `void` so A\* correctly routes around it while it still earns daylight credit for adjacent rooms.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Warehouse and factory floors deliberately reject this — deep, unobstructed, uniformly lit plates are the point (Fagus, Gläserne Manufaktur); a windowless archive or plant room is legitimately deep. Symptom: the agent puts 12 rooms in a single row off one 80-tile corridor, and every interior room fails RP-08.

### RP-10 Site the supervising post by measured travel and measured visibility
- Rule: Where one post must observe or serve many rooms (nurse station, reception, teacher's home base, control room, library desk), choose its tile from a computed surface: mean weighted A\* distance to all served rooms and the count of tiles visible along open runs. Optimise both; state the trade-off when they conflict.
- Evidence: Paimio's whole plan is a service-observation compromise at bed scale, with details engineered for the patient's experience — taps angled to "prevent noise and splashing", "cupboards … hung for ease of floor cleaning", "yellow rubber flooring in the corridors", "soft tones with darker ceilings" (PMC). The hospital typology then made visibility explicit: GCUH's atrium-and-ward organisation draws daylight "through every floor" and gives staff one legible spine instead of four corridors (Hassell). Retail and library planning use the same mechanism from the user side: Oodi's double-helix stair and open terraced volume make the whole building readable from the entry (ALA Architects).
- Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2917967/ — T2; https://archipro.com.au/project/gold-coast-university-hospital-qld-sth — T3; https://ala.fi/work/helsinki-central-library/ — T3
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: hospital, school, office, retail, library, detention
- Confidence: Medium (project evidence for the intent; no retrieved source giving a numeric sightline threshold — thresholds below are `UNCITED — heuristic`)
- Grid translation: For each candidate post tile compute `mean_steps = Σ w_i · A*(post, room_i_door) / Σ w_i` and `visible = |tiles reachable by ray from post tile through door/open runs, up to 60 tiles|`. Require `mean_steps ≤ 16` for nursing/teaching posts and `visible ≥ 0.30 × floor_tiles`; run the loop over all candidate tiles and report the chosen tile against the optimum.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Types where observation is explicitly unwanted (therapy rooms, prayer rooms, staff welfare, confidential consulting — Maggie's keeps "non-institutional" privacy), and where the supervisor must *not* see the corridor (counselling). Symptom: a nurse station that optimises distance but faces a wall, or a reception with zero sight of the door it should be watching.

### RP-11 Give bulky movement its own same-level route and portal
- Rule: If the building moves beds, trolleys, pallets, carts, instruments or large equipment, provide a continuous same-floor route and a vertical portal sized for them, and check the geometry at every turn.
- Evidence: Modern Australian hospitals were built on the same-level-circulation argument — GCUH and SCUH both organise a whole ward on one floor so a bed never travels by stair, with the clinical street as the moving spine (Hassell; ArchitectureAu). Kansai's terminal likewise separates movement modes onto dedicated levels and puts the gates in "wings" served by the open departures floor (RPBW). Logistics encodes the same geometry in numbers: dock sill at "1.20 m above finished floor level" with an apron of "minimum 12 m for rigid trucks; 18–22 m for articulated semi-trailers" — a vehicle needs a stand-off, not just a door (PEB Steel).
- Source: https://archipro.com.au/project/gold-coast-university-hospital-qld-sth — T3; https://architectureau.com/articles/sunshine-coast-university-hospital/ — T3; https://www.rpbw.com/project/kansai-international-airport-terminal — T3; https://pebsteel.com/en/warehouse-design-guide/ — T4
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: hospital, hotel, industrial, retail, school
- Confidence: High that bulky movement needs its own same-level route and portal (three independent
  types state it); **Low for the 6-tile / 8x8-tile envelopes used as the test - the register has no
  peer-reviewed ward-layout or trolley-travel source (see *Weak or contested*, gaps item (i)), so
  those figures are this file's own tile translation of vendor dock geometry, not measured
  thresholds.**
- Grid translation: Define `bulky = 6 tiles clear width straight, 8×8 tiles at a turn` (3.0 m straight, 4.0 m at 90°). Validate: a graph search restricted to door runs ≥ 6 tiles and turn squares ≥ 8×8 must connect every bulky-producing room (bed space, kitchen, dock, loading bay, plant) to a `bulky portal` on the same floor and to one on the next. Report `blocked_bulky_links` with the offending door run.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Small residential, cafes, offices with no trolley traffic — 2-tile door runs are fine. Symptom: a 14-tile corridor that fails at one 4-tile door: the route "looks" connected and is operationally severed.

### RP-12 Reserve service and plant area in the plan; assume the environmental strategy will lose to control
- Rule: Allocate plant, risers, storage, cleaning and welfare area as a named percentage of each floor at the start, and prefer strategies whose equipment fits that allocation. Record any sustainability ambition that needs more space than exists as an explicit, priced risk.
- Evidence: Sunshine Coast University Hospital's design sought natural ventilation, "although this ambition was ultimately ruled out for clinical reasons", falling back on mechanical cooling with "admission of coastal breezes drawn through the cool subfloor voids under both buildings" (ArchitectureAu) — the plan-level cost of a lost strategy. The Bullitt Center shows the opposite result achieved by giving the strategy the primary geometric role: an "oversized solar canopy — overhangs the walls", a 244 kW array, a 56,000-gallon cistern, foam-flush composting units, all inside a deliberately modest six-storey plate; and its ten-year lesson is candid that the barriers were "as much regulatory as technical" (Miller Hull, via Studio Matrx). The Gläserne Manufaktur's maple floor and glass box were chosen for a specific, controlled production programme — painted bodies arrived by truck and parts by the CarGoTram, so logistics, not aesthetics, fixed the service reservations (Wikipedia).
- Source: https://architectureau.com/articles/sunshine-coast-university-hospital/ — T3; https://www.studiomatrx.org/guides/bullitt-center — T4 (reporting Miller Hull data); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gl%C3%A4serne_Manufaktur — T4
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High for the principle; Medium for the magnitudes
- Grid translation: Budget `plant_service_share` by type and enforce it: hotel 0.06–0.10, hospital 0.12–0.20, office 0.08–0.12, warehouse 0.05–0.10 ("office, welfare, plant room 5–10 %", PEB Steel). Represent plant as blocked tiles tagged `plant`; require every `plant` tile cluster to be within 8 tiles of a `service` portal and to have a 4-tile clear maintenance approach. If the brief specifies a strategy needing unallocated area, emit a `SPACE_CONFLICT` record rather than quietly deleting the strategy.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Buildings cooled by their own form (a market hall, a school gym) or by the host's simplified physics may need no plant at all — declare zero and move on. Symptom: a plan with no plant tiles, then a late "roof plant" that eats the top floor.

### RP-13 Anchor the plan on a hearth for care, community and hospitality types
- Rule: In buildings whose users are anxious, temporary or communal (cancer care, day surgery, shelters, libraries, hotels, schools, housing with shared facilities), place a kitchen/dining/hearth room on the main circulation spine within 40 tile-steps (20 m) of the entry, and make it the visually most accessible room on the floor.
- Evidence: Maggie's (Richard Rogers/RSHP) planned the West London centre as "a series of four 'tables'" around a two-storey central volume with a wood-burning stove — Rogers states the "notion of the 'hearth' as a focal space" — and the whole building was delivered "without resorting to area schedules and data sheets", with rooms deliberately ranging "from about 1 m² up to 50 m²"; the founding client requirement was places "domestic in scale, making people feel instantly at home" (Architects Journal). The clinical justification for the domestic turn is Paimio's: Aalto's priority was "to create healing environments that emulate nature", placing patient experience over pure function (PMC). Oodi's ground floor plays the same role at city scale, and Unité put shops and a restaurant on its interior street so 337 dwellings had a shared table (ALA; Fondation Le Corbusier).
- Source: https://www.architectsjournal.co.uk/practice/culture/richard-rogers-on-maggies-centre-and-the-architecture-of-hope — T3; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2917967/ — T2; https://ala.fi/work/helsinki-central-library/ — T3; https://www.fondationlecorbusier.fr/en/work-architecture/achievements-unite-dhabitation-marseille-france-1945-1952/ — T3
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: hospital, hotel, residential, school, public
- Confidence: High (documented client briefs and architect statements; the effect on outcomes is qualitative). **Low for the numeric hearth radius, which this entry previously stated inconsistently**: the rule line said "20 tiles" (= 10 m) while its validator tested 40 tile-steps with a "≈ 20–28 m" gloss. Only the 40 is arithmetically coherent on this grid - 40 tile-steps is 20 m on an orthogonal run and 28.3 m if every step is a diagonal, which is exactly where the old parenthetical came from; 20 tiles can produce neither figure. Both lines now read 40 tile-steps (20 m). Neither value is sourced - the cited material documents the hearth's *role*, never a travel budget - so the number is a project knob to record, not a threshold to fail a plan on.
- Grid translation: Require a room tagged `hearth` (fixture set includes `kitchen` or `dining`) with `A*(entry_door_tile, hearth_centroid) ≤ 40 tile-steps (20 m on an orthogonal run; the same step budget is 28.3 m if every step is a diagonal)`, `hearth_area ≥ 64 tiles (16 m²)`, and `visible_from(entry) = true`. Report the hearth's integration rank: `hearth must be in the top 3 most-connected rooms on the floor`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Types where the hearth is deliberately hidden or absent (industrial, retail back-of-house, a clinic with no catering, detention where food service is a controlled servery). Symptom: a hospital plan with a canteen tucked behind two corridors — it is used by staff only.

### RP-14 Share the building with the community where the programme is idle half the time
- Rule: If the building's primary use is idle for a large part of the day or year (school, office, stadium, clinic), add at least one jointly-accessible function with its own external control, and give it a route that does not pass through the primary building's private zone.
- Evidence: Saunalahti School (VERSTAS) holds a comprehensive school for 750 students plus "day care centre, preschool and a youth house" in about 10,000 m², and its architects and city describe the design as the "living room of the whole neighbourhood", integrating community use beyond school hours through open group spaces with glass walls (finland.fi; Divisare). Unité d'Habitation put a "shopping street on the 7th and 8th floors" plus a 300 m² roof terrace with "a running track, a gymnasium" into a housing block, because the housing shortage demanded a building that provided the services a street would normally give (Fondation Le Corbusier). Oodi's whole brief is civic sharing: studios, makerspace, cinema and a public ground floor in one 17,100 m² block (ALA).
- Source: https://finland.fi/life-society/building-an-even-better-finnish-school/ — T3; https://divisare.com/projects/238860-verstas-architects-andreas-meichsner-saunalahti-comprehensive-school — T3; https://www.fondationlecorbusier.fr/en/work-architecture/achievements-unite-dhabitation-marseille-france-1945-1952/ — T3; https://ala.fi/work/helsinki-central-library/ — T3
- Class: BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION
- Scope: school, office, public, residential, hospital
- Confidence: Medium-High (strong project evidence; the payback depends on local operating model, which this host does not simulate)
- Grid translation: Identify rooms with `idle_hours ≥ 0.4` and require at least one such room to have a `public` door run direct from an exterior entry, plus a shared lobby of ≥ 24 tiles (6 m²) serving both regimes. Check: `community_room reachable from outside without entering a private-tagged room`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: High-security, infectious-isolation and sterile operations where shared access is a hazard, and small buildings where an extra entrance costs more than the sharing gains. Symptom: an unused school hall and a crowded street, or a shared route that breaks RP-01's zone discipline.

### RP-15 Give the organising spine street behaviour, not corridor behaviour
- Rule: Where a plan has more than ~15 rooms per floor and mixed uses, organise it around a spine at least 4 tiles (2 m) wide with continuous frontage, occasional widening nodes, and direct door access to the spine; treat it as a place to be, not just a route.
- Evidence: Unité's "system of interior streets" carries shops at the level the duplexes are entered (Fondation Le Corbusier). SCUH's principal circulation is described as "this main street [that] is busy and active, lined with retail outlets, service providers, cafe facilities and open bays of seating" (ArchitectureAu). Kansai's departures level works the same way at terminal scale — one long, open, legible level with the gates in wings (RPBW). Selfridges Birmingham, 14,864 m² over six floors, is the counter-example in one respect: movement is by "interior escalators" inside a sculpted envelope, i.e. the spine is vertical and spectacular rather than horizontal, and it still organises the plan (Wikipedia). Oodi's terraced volume with a double-helix stair is the same vertical-street device.
- Source: https://www.fondationlecorbusier.fr/en/work-architecture/achievements-unite-dhabitation-marseille-france-1945-1952/ — T3; https://architectureau.com/articles/sunshine-coast-university-hospital/ — T3; https://www.rpbw.com/project/kansai-international-airport-terminal — T3; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selfridges_Birmingham — T4; https://ala.fi/work/helsinki-central-library/ — T3
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: hotel, hospital, office, retail, school, public
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: `spine_width ≥ 4 tiles` for the primary route (≥ 6 tiles where the spine is also a waiting/social space), `frontage_ratio = tiles of room doors on spine / spine length ≥ 0.25`, and one `node` (spine widening to ≥ 10 tiles) every 60 tiles. A 2-tile corridor fails the test and may only be used for secondary or single-loaded access. Report spine length, nodes and frontage as measured properties of the finished floor.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Quiet types where a "street" reads as noise penetration (libraries' silent floors, wards at night, therapy suites), and tiny floors. Symptom: an 80-tile corridor with 30 identical doors and no room to stand — efficient on paper, unusable at peak.

### RP-16 Group small users' rooms into a home base with its own lobby
- Rule: For schools, early-years, residential care and any programme with a supervising adult and a fixed peer group, plan clusters of 3–6 rooms around a shared lobby, and control the cluster with one door from the spine; do not line all rooms on a public corridor.
- Evidence: Saunalahti is planned in "home areas" where "classrooms group around small lobbies for collaboration", with open group spaces behind glass walls so supervision happens by sight rather than by corridor patrol (Divisare; finland.fi). Maggie's builds the same idea at care scale — four "tables" plus rooms from 1 to 50 m², each "split into an internal space and an external terrace", so the group has both a room and its own outside (Architects Journal). Paimio's two-bed rooms with their own sun balcony are the same principle in a hospital: the small unit owns a piece of the exterior (PMC).
- Source: https://divisare.com/projects/238860-verstas-architects-andreas-meichsner-saunalahti-comprehensive-school — T3; https://www.architectsjournal.co.uk/practice/culture/richard-rogers-on-maggies-centre-and-the-architecture-of-hope — T3; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2917967/ — T2
- Class: BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION
- Scope: school, hospital, residential
- Confidence: High (two independent types, both documented in the architects' own terms)
- Grid translation: A cluster = 3–6 `home_base` rooms + one `lobby` room of ≥ 32 tiles (8 m²), with `cluster_entry_doors = 1`. Validate: every cluster room reaches the lobby in ≤ 6 steps, and the lobby is the only room with a door to the spine. Report `clusters_per_floor` and `lobby_share = lobby_tiles / floor_tiles` (target 0.05–0.10).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Hotels, offices and warehouses, where the repeat-unit + corridor is genuinely better; adult housing where private entries are wanted. Symptom: 30 classrooms each with its own door onto one corridor — no territory, no group room, and full control of the corridor at every break.

### RP-17 Derive every room's area from capacity arithmetic and publish the per-place number
- Rule: For programmes defined by people counts (pupils, beds, desks, shoppers, workers), compute area from `persons × area-per-person`, then report `gross_area / persons` for the whole building and compare with the documented comparators. Do not size rooms by appearance.
- Evidence: Saunalahti is a checkable datum: about 10,000 m² for 750 students ≈ 13.3 m² gross per place, including the shared community programmes (finland.fi; Divisare — the ratio is this file's arithmetic from the two cited figures, not a quoted standard). German nursery guidance is explicit where it exists in code: Hesse requires a minimum room height of "at least 2.80 m" and corridor width "at least 2.00 m" for new kindergarten buildings, with common group models of "20 to 25 children" (HEWI overview of state guidelines). Fogo Island Inn sizes by room type instead: 29 guest rooms "from 350 square feet to 1,100 square feet" in 4,500 m² gross — a ratio the agent can compute (gooood/Saunders). Hotel guides give bands of 28–30 m² (midscale), 32–38 m² (upper upscale), 40 m²+ (luxury), suites 55–75 m² (Archgyan; T4).
- Source: https://finland.fi/life-society/building-an-even-better-finnish-school/ — T3; https://www.hewi.com/en/mag/120-nursery-school-guidelines — T4; https://www.gooood.cn/en/fogo-island-inn-by-saunders.htm — T3; https://archgyan.com/how-to-design-a-hotel/ — T4
- Class: HEURISTIC
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: Medium (single-project ratios travel poorly; the method is high confidence, the numbers low-to-medium)
- Grid translation: `tiles = required_m2 × 4`. Emit `area_per_person = room_tiles × 0.25 / capacity` for every room, and `gross_per_place = floor_tiles × 0.25 / persons` for the building, then test against the type band: school 10–16 m²/place gross, hotel guest floor 28–45 m²/room, office 8–14 m²/desk, ward 25–40 m²/bed gross of the clinical floor. Any room whose `area_per_person` is below its programme figure is a hard fail; any building more than 25 % above its comparator is a soft fail with a `DEAD_AREA` report.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Assembly, circulation-dominated civic and industrial floors, where per-place area is meaningless because occupancy is event-based or machine-based. Symptom: a "generous" plan that is actually 40 % unprogrammed corridor.

### RP-18 Keep the perimeter band of a working floor clear; daylight and supervision both need it
- Rule: In production, workshop, studio and open-office floors, reserve a 2–4 tile band inside the facade free of fixed storage, machines and tall units, and locate tall things in the deep interior.
- Evidence: Fagus Factory is the founding case: Gropius and Meyer made the corners glass with "no column, no pier, nothing where the eye expects the most support", turning the wall into a "screen hung on the building rather than a mass the building rests upon", so that the works halls were lit from the sides and the making was visible — the stated motive treats the workforce as "workers who deserve light and air" (Studio Matrx canon note; UNESCO listing 2011). Dresden's Gläserne Manufaktur carries the logic forward: a glass production box whose floor must stay clear and whose process is meant to be seen by up to 250 visitors a day (Wikipedia). The Edge does the office version, with massing "tailored to daylight" and a deep section broken by the atrium (PLP Architecture).
- Source: https://www.studiomatrx.org/architecture-canon/fagus-factory — T4 (reporting a T1/T3 listed building); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gl%C3%A4serne_Manufaktur — T4; https://www.plparchitecture.com/projects/the-edge — T3
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: office, industrial, retail, school
- Confidence: Medium-High (canonical, consistent; no numeric threshold from a standard). **Low for the
  "supervision" half of the headline as a historical motive:** the Fagus reading that Gropius freed
  the corners so light and supervision would reach the workers is contested narrative (see *Weak or
  contested*), and the register cites no dimensions from that building. The rule therefore rests on
  the daylight/visibility geometry alone, not on anyone's stated intent for the workforce.
- Grid translation: Require `perimeter_band = 4 tiles` inside all facade tiles to be `walkable` and free of fixture tags `storage|rack|machine|tall_unit`; report `perimeter_blockage = blocked tiles in band / band tiles`, target ≤ 0.15. Deep tiles (≥ 20 from any facade) are the only place tall storage may go.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Where the perimeter carries structure that cannot be set back (existing frames), or where perimeter security glazing forces a protected band. Symptom: racking along the windows: the daylit centre is unusable and the shadow is permanent.

### RP-19 Set the movement grid from the handling equipment
- Rule: In any floor where things are moved by equipment, derive aisle widths and bay spacing from the equipment's operating envelope, and check every aisle, turn and door against it before committing the grid.
- Evidence: Two independent UK/EU storage guides agree on the bands: counterbalance forklift aisles 3.5–4.0 m, reach truck 2.7–3.0 m, very-narrow-aisle turret truck 1.5–1.8 m (PEB Steel; Umstoragesystems). The Umstore guide adds the vertical constraints translated into plan: "75 mm minimum between top of pallet and underside of the beam above", "75 mm minimum side clearance between the pallet edge and each upright", "100 mm minimum between back-to-back pallets in a double-run" — i.e. double runs are widened, not mirrored exactly. Typical primary bay spacing is given as "12 m … as the standard for maximum operational efficiency" (PEB Steel).
- Source: https://pebsteel.com/en/warehouse-design-guide/ — T4; https://www.umstoragesystems.co.uk/blog/warehouse-racking-layout-design-guide — T4
- Class: HEURISTIC (trade planning bands, not a code requirement - see Confidence)
- Scope: industrial, retail, hospital (trolleys), hotel (carts)
- Confidence: Medium-High for the aisle bands themselves (two independent agreeing trade sources); **Low
  for any code status - this entry was previously classed `CODE REQUIREMENT` on evidence that cannot
  carry that class: a steel-building vendor layout guide and a racking-vendor blog (both T4), with the
  SEMA clearances reported second-hand and no SEMA or HSE primary retrieved. A vendor page cannot make
  a rule a code requirement.** The equipment operating envelope is a real engineering constraint, so
  the honest use of the bands is: re-derive every aisle, turn and door from the actual truck's data
  sheet for the project, and never present the tile figures below as compliance values.
- Grid translation: On this grid: counterbalance aisle 7–8 tiles, reach-truck aisle 6 tiles, VNA aisle 3–4 tiles, main gangway 8–10 tiles; a 12 m structural bay is 24 tiles. Require `aisle_width ≥ equipment_min` at every tile of the aisle and at every junction; a 100 mm back-to-back gap is below grid resolution, so the rule becomes: double runs get `+1 tile` total width, never zero. Validate turn geometry: 90° with a loaded truck needs the aisle plus `ceil(radius/0.5)` tiles of clear square.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Manual-only storage and shops (1.2–1.8 m aisles are enough — see RP-21), and human-scale care settings where the wrong aisle width reads as a corridor that cannot take a bed (see RP-11). Symptom: a racking plan that "fits" until one trolley must pass another.

### RP-20 Split a storage or logistics floor by measured functional shares and put the docks on a straight run
- Rule: Plan industrial/logistics floors by allocating percentage shares to receipt, storage, picking, dispatch and welfare, then place all doors on one straight elevation and keep the internal route one-way-ish and short.
- Evidence: PEB Steel's guidance gives the shares directly — "Bulk storage 50–65 %", "Receiving / inbound staging 8–12 %", "Dispatch / outbound staging 8–12 %", "Pick / forward area 5–10 %", "Office, welfare, plant room 5–10 %" — plus dock density "1 dock per 1,000–1,500 m²" and the apron depths (see RP-03). The classical alternative is the manufacturing decision: process (functional) layout groups like machines and pays for it with "high (long travel paths)" handling and high work-in-progress, product layout gives the "lowest (linear flow)" handling cost but "stops entirely if one machine fails", and U-shaped cellular layout reports 30–50 % setup reduction and 40 %+ WIP reduction (Oxmaint plant-layout comparison; consistent with the IAENG/SME cellular literature).
- Source: https://pebsteel.com/en/warehouse-design-guide/ — T4; https://oxmaint.com/industries/manufacturing-plant/plant-layout-types-process-product-cellular-comparison — T4
- Class: BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION
- Scope: industrial, retail
- Confidence: Medium (vendor/industry sources agreeing; percentages are planning norms, not measurements)
- Grid translation: Compute `share(function) = tiles(function)/floor_tiles` and test against the bands above; a `dock` is a door run of 6–8 tiles on one facade, count = `floor_tiles × 0.25 / 1,250 m²` (mid-band) and each dock needs a 24–44-tile deep `yard` band in front. Then compute the flow metric that decides the archetype: `travel_index = Σ (trip_rate_ij × A*(i,j))` and compare process vs product vs cellular variants; pick the lowest index that still satisfies flexibility requirements, and report both numbers.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Small workshops and maker spaces where a single flexible bay beats the zoning; bonded/secure stores where separation beats flow. Symptom: 70 % of the floor given to "storage" with no staging lanes, so trucks wait and the dispatch area appears in the racks.

### RP-21 In retail, buy exposure with path length and keep the entry readable
- Rule: Plan selling floors as a legible loop with a clear entry zone, main walks at least 3 tiles (1.5 m) wide, low fixtures on the sightline side, and the till off to one side rather than facing the door.
- Evidence: Retail layout practice states the trade-off explicitly: grid layouts run 35–45 % fixture coverage with 1.2–1.8 m aisles, loops 25–35 % with 1.5–2.1 m aisles, free-flow 15–25 % on an open floor (Retail Design layout guide). The same source gives the entry rule — the "decompression zone is the first 1.5 to 3 m inside the entrance … keep it clear of dense fixtures" — the browsing limit "aisles under 0.9 m … customers stop browsing the lower shelves", and the till rule "cash counter facing the entrance reads as a barrier … side it" (Retail Design; Studio Matrx retail planning, which also warns that "shoppers always turn right" is a myth and that the accessible dimension "is law"). The Bullring/Selfridges block shows the vertical version: 14,864 m² over six floors served by interior escalators, on a corner site where the building "wraps around the corner of Moor Street and Park Street", i.e. two street frontages feeding two levels (Wikipedia).
- Source: https://retaildesign.ai/blog/retail-store-layout-design-guide — T4; https://www.studiomatrx.org/students/interior-design-studio-3/retail-planning-and-the-shopper — T4; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selfridges_Birmingham — T4
- Class: HEURISTIC
- Scope: retail
- Confidence: Medium for the mechanism, Low for the percentages (T4 only)
- Grid translation: `main_walk ≥ 3 tiles (1.5 m)`, `secondary_walk ≥ 2 tiles`, `entry_clear_zone = first 3–6 tiles inside each entry door with zero fixture tiles`; require a `loop`: a cycle in the walkable graph passing ≥ 60 % of fixture tiles, with `max_path_length_to_far_corner ≤ 1.4 × direct_distance`. Compute `exposure = fixture_tiles within 8 tiles of the loop path`, and report `till_facing_entry = false`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Convenience and grab-and-go formats where a tight grid beats a loop; gallery shops and markets where the stall pattern is the product. Symptom: a plan where the loop only reaches half the fixtures, or the entry is blocked by the counter and the first 5 tiles are dead.

### RP-22 Make the stackable unit replaceable at its interface, or accept the whole building's obsolescence
- Rule: When a design is built from repeated stacked units (modules, capsules, flats, pods, rack bays), specify the interface between units and between unit and core, and verify that one unit can be removed, serviced and re-entered without disturbing its neighbours. Record the maintenance route.
- Evidence: Nakagin Capsule Tower is the documented failure: Kisho Kurokawa stacked 140 capsules of 2.5 × 2.5 × 4 m around two cores in 1972, with capsules "expected to be replaced roughly every 25 to 35 years", but physical interdependence and decay prevented replacement and the building was demolished in 2022, with only "twenty-three capsules … salvaged" (designboom). Habitat 67 succeeded on the same idea for different reasons: 365 modules making 158 residences, where modules also "participate as load-carrying members", each residence gets "its own roof garden" and "access to the dwellings is directly off" the access streets — and Safdie's own retrospective notes the ongoing need to "balance individual interests and needs for modification" (Safdie Architects).
- Source: https://www.designboom.com/architecture/nakagin-capsule-tower-dream-home-change-kisho-kurokawa-tokyo/ — T3; https://www.safdiearchitects.com/projects/habitat-67 — T3
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: residential, hotel, industrial
- Confidence: High (a built-in counter-example with a dated end)
- Grid translation: For each stacked unit record: `unit_doors = number of door runs crossing the unit boundary` (must be ≥ 1 and must not be shared with another unit's only door), `service_boundary = tiles where wet fixtures of two stacked units share a riser line`, `maintenance_path = A*(service portal, unit door) ≤ 20 tiles with ≥ 3-tile width`. Reject any plan where two units' only access is the same door run, or where a unit's wet tile block is not vertically aligned with the unit below.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Cast-in-place single-volume buildings (the rule is trivially satisfied) and non-stackable uses. Symptom: a "kit of parts" plan whose parts cannot be removed, or where one flat's bathroom sits over another's kitchen.

### RP-23 Trade floor area for voids deliberately and account for voids twice
- Rule: Atrium, light well, sky garden and gallery voids buy daylight, wayfinding and social capacity and cost rentable area; count them in both budgets, and give every void at least two floors of adjacency and one visible-from-circulation edge.
- Evidence: GCUH's seven-storey atrium is credited with drawing daylight "down through every floor" in a 175,000 m² hospital whose measured stay fell from just over four days to under three (Hassell). Commerzbank's atrium opens to a side at nine different levels as sky gardens across 109,200 m² of floor area, which is how a 56-storey tower keeps daylight in a triangular core plan (Wikipedia). Sendai's 13 tubes are void-plus-core hybrids that bring light and movement through every floor of an open plate (Architectuul). Oodi's triple-height glazed ground floor and terraced upper volume make the whole section one continuous public room over 17,100 m² (ALA). Fogo Island Inn's public volumes are the hotel's version of the void, held in the two-storey wing while the rooms sit in the four-storey wing (gooood).
- Source: https://archipro.com.au/project/gold-coast-university-hospital-qld-sth — T3; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commerzbank_Tower — T4 (cross-check of Foster + Partners T3 data); https://architectuul.com/architecture/sendai-mediatheque — T3; https://ala.fi/work/helsinki-central-library/ — T3; https://www.gooood.cn/en/fogo-island-inn-by-saunders.htm — T3
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: hotel, hospital, office, school, retail, public
- Confidence: Medium-High (mechanism well evidenced; the payback numbers come from promotional project reporting, not independent studies)
- Grid translation: Tag void tiles `blocked + void`; report `void_share = void_tiles / floor_tiles` (target 0.05–0.12 for hospital/office, 0.10–0.20 for civic) and the two benefits: `daylight_credit = rooms adjacent to void that fail the facade test`, and `visible_from_spine = void tiles on the sightline set`. Reject any void with only one floor of adjacency (a light hole, not an atrium) and any void whose perimeter is 100 % rooms with no circulation edge — a void nobody can see is pure lost area.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Industrial floors (a void is a machine pit and is dangerous), small buildings, and residential floors where an atrium is a noise and privacy channel. Symptom: the plan reads as generous, the area report reads as 12 % wasted, and the wasted area is not visible to anyone.

### RP-24 Make every decision point differentiable
- Rule: Where more than three routes meet, guarantee that the junction is distinguishable from its neighbours by width, adjacency, void, daylight or a fixture in view; never produce a corridor system of repeating identical T-junctions.
- Evidence: SCUH's design explicitly relies on the courtyard axis to govern "wayfinding throughout the principal public spaces" (ArchitectureAu). Sendai's tubes are the differentiator in an otherwise featureless fluid plate — thirteen unmistakable landmarks (Architectuul). Oodi's double-helix stair and terraced balconies give the library its three decision points by section (ALA). Maggie's differentiates by room size, from about 1 m² to 50 m², so every corner is a recognisable place (Architects Journal). Paimio's corridor logic is the same at hospital scale — the yellow rubber corridor floor, the darker ceiling, and the sun balcony as the unmistakable destination (PMC).
- Source: https://architectureau.com/articles/sunshine-coast-university-hospital/ — T3; https://architectuul.com/architecture/sendai-mediatheque — T3; https://ala.fi/work/helsinki-central-library/ — T3; https://www.architectsjournal.co.uk/practice/culture/richard-rogers-on-maggies-centre-and-the-architecture-of-hope — T3; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2917967/ — T2
- Class: HEURISTIC
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: Medium (project statements about intent; no post-occupancy wayfinding measurement retrieved in this pass)
- Grid translation: For each junction tile compute a signature `(incoming_widths, adjacent_room_types, visible_landmark_count)`. Require: no two junctions on the same floor share an identical signature, and every junction has `visible_landmark_count ≥ 1` (a `void`, `hearth`, `entrance`, `stair_portal`, or distinctive fixture tag within 24 tiles). Report the count of `duplicate_signatures` and `blind_junctions`; both target zero.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Detention and sterile-area healthcare, where identical, repeatable, controlled corridors are the safety feature; deliberately disorienting retail (a declared exception). Symptom: an agent-built floor that passes every metric and is impossible to describe to someone standing in it.

### RP-25 Choose the plate's publicness gradient in the section, not only in the plan
- Rule: Where the host has multiple floors, treat the *stack* as a zoning diagram: the vertical sequence of floor uses is a design decision with an operational consequence, and the alignment of like-with-like (wet over wet, heavy over light, public over public) must be checked across the whole stack.
- Evidence: Unité's stack runs dwellings, then the shop street at the 7th/8th level, then a 300 m² roof terrace with running track and gymnasium — a deliberate vertical publicness curve inside one 56 m block (Fondation Le Corbusier). The Interlace replaces the tower stack with 31 six-storey blocks arranged in a "hexagonal arrangement around eight large-scale open and permeable courtyards" and 170,000 m² of floor area serving over 1,000 units — publicness distributed across the section instead of stacked (OMA). Marina Bay Sands runs public podium, private towers, then a 2.5-acre SkyPark garden crowning the three towers linked at the 57th floor (Safdie Architects). Fogo Island Inn stacks production/service below public below private (gooood).
- Source: https://www.fondationlecorbusier.fr/en/work-architecture/achievements-unite-dhabitation-marseille-france-1945-1952/ — T3; https://www.oma.com/projects/the-interlace — T3; https://www.safdiearchitects.com/projects/marina-bay-sands-hotel-and-skypark — T3; https://www.gooood.cn/en/fogo-island-inn-by-saunders.htm — T3
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: Build the per-floor zone vector from RP-01 and check three things: (a) monotonic or explained publicness gradient (RP-02); (b) `wet_alignment`: rooms tagged `kitchen|bath|laundry` sit above or below like-tagged rooms, else declare a `riser` tile; (c) `load_alignment`: rooms with `heavy` fixtures (plant, vault, rack, kiln) sit on the lowest two floors or on a column-line tile set, never above sleeping rooms. Report any floor whose zone vector is an outlier as `STACK_REVIEW`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Buildings with a genuinely mixed stack where the mix is the design (Sendai's fluid floors, Interlace's stacked bars). Symptom: a hotel floor over a kitchen, a server room over a bedroom, or a public floor with no vertical connection to the public floor above.

### RP-26 Where the brief's dominant requirement is flexibility, remove the walls and hang the services
- Rule: If the programme says the plan must absorb change (media, exhibition, coworking, maker, hybrid civic), prefer a fluid open plate with the fixed elements — circulation, toilets, risers, daylight — pulled out into discrete punctual cores, and pay for the resulting circulation and structural cost explicitly.
- Evidence: Sendai Mediatheque is the fullest statement: "walls on each floor are kept to an absolute minimum" so functions can be "freely distributed throughout the open areas between the tubes", achieved with flat slabs of honeycomb steel plates and concrete penetrated by 13 tubes that carry lifts, services and light across seven levels of facilities (Architectuul; Toyo Ito). The Edge achieves a lighter version of the same idea for an office: a multi-storey atrium acting as a "social condenser, bringing together circulation, meeting spaces", exposed lift cores, and a sensor/app layer that lets the desk allocation change without the walls changing (PLP; the sensors "measure occupancy, movement, humidity, CO₂, light levels and internal climate"). The Interlace bought flexibility by refusing towers (OMA). Nakagin shows what happens when flexibility is claimed at the part but not engineered at the interface (designboom).
- Source: https://architectuul.com/architecture/sendai-mediatheque — T3; https://www.plparchitecture.com/projects/the-edge — T3; https://www.oma.com/projects/the-interlace — T3; https://www.designboom.com/architecture/nakagin-capsule-tower-dream-home-change-kisho-kurokawa-tokyo/ — T3
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: office, public, retail, industrial
- Confidence: Medium-High (few buildings are tested for genuine re-fitting in the sources available; cost consequences are asserted, not measured)
- Grid translation: Emit `fixed_tile_share = (core + wet + void + facade infrastructure tiles) / floor_tiles ≤ 0.20` and `reconfigurable_share = open walkable tiles ≥ 0.60`. Test change-over: place a candidate new programme on the *same* tile grid with walls only along existing door lines and verify `A*` still gives every new room ≥ 2 tiles of facade or void and a path ≤ 40 tiles to a portal — if it fails, the plate is not flexible, only empty. Log the extra circulation cost from RP-06 alongside.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Types whose value is in fixed acoustic/privacy separation (residential, wards, recording studios) — there the wall is the product. Symptom: an "adaptable" open plate where no future programme can actually be enclosed without cutting a wall across a services run.

### RP-27 Record the reason for every deviation from the type's norm
- Rule: When the agent departs from a documented convention for the building type (ground-floor privacy in housing, single-loaded corridors, a shared guest/service route, an interior patient room), emit a one-line rationale naming the trade bought, plus the measurable cost. Unexplained departures are treated as errors.
- Evidence: Every strong case in this register is a documented departure with a stated trade. Unité put its shops on the 7th/8th floor because the duplex stacking demanded one-storey access from the street (Fondation Le Corbusier). SCUH abandoned natural ventilation "for clinical reasons" and said so (ArchitectureAu). Interlace rejected the tower stack to get courtyards at all levels (OMA). The Interlace/Edge/Bullitt trio each report the cost side: area, capital and regulation — Bullitt's ten-year lesson being that the obstacles were "as much regulatory as technical" (via Studio Matrx). The failure to record a trade-off is what turned Habitat's and Nakagin's shared idea into two different outcomes.
- Source: https://www.fondationlecorbusier.fr/en/work-architecture/achievements-unite-dhabitation-marseille-france-1945-1952/ — T3; https://architectureau.com/articles/sunshine-coast-university-hospital/ — T3; https://www.oma.com/projects/the-interlace — T3; https://www.studiomatrx.org/guides/bullitt-center — T4
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: The agent's output carries a `deviations` array: `{rule_id, project_norm, chosen_alternative, benefit_metric, cost_metric}` where the metrics are the same numbers the rules report (efficiency, d_max, daylight share, area per person). Validation: `count(deviations without cost_metric) = 0`; any RP rule marked FAILED without a matching deviation record blocks the design.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Nothing structural; the rule is cheap and is the only defence against a plan that satisfies the metric library and still fails to work. Symptom: a design with a dozen unremarked departures and no explanation of what was bought.

## Case register

Field order follows the research brief: type / programme / site & context / constraints / area / floors / zoning / circulation / vertical circulation / structure / services / public-private separation / environmental strategy / operational strategy / key decisions / trade-offs. Only fields with a retrieved source are filled.

**CR-01 Unité d'Habitation, Marseille — Le Corbusier, 1945–1952.** Residential + shops + roof amenities; a response to post-war rehousing ("the government's priority was rehousing"). 135 m long × 24 m wide × 56 m high, 337 dwellings (1,600 inhabitants), reinforced concrete frame; a "system of interior streets" with "shopping streets on the 7th and 8th floors"; many dwellings duplex, "on two floors connected by a staircase"; a repeated first module combining "entrance, the hallway, the kitchen and the living room"; `brise soleils` "designed to filter it out in summer"; 300 m² roof terrace with "a running track, a gymnasium"; described as "the balance between individual and collective housing". Key decision: mid-level street so both halves of a duplex are one storey from the shop front. Trade-off: brutal concentration, no ground-level street life for the residents' own floors. Source: Fondation Le Corbusier https://www.fondationlecorbusier.fr/en/work-architecture/achievements-unite-dhabitation-marseille-france-1945-1952/ (T3); UNESCO series page https://lecorbusier-worldheritage.org/en/unite-habitation/ (T3).

**CR-02 Habitat 67, Montreal — Moshe Safdie, 1967.** Residential; 365 prefabricated construction modules forming 158 residences, 238,000 sq ft; the ambition was amenities "typically reserved for the single-family home, now adapted to a high density city environment"; "each residence has its own roof garden"; "access to the dwellings is directly off" the access ways (streets in the air); modules "participate as load-carrying members"; the aim "access to sun and air". Key decision: give each unit its own terrace and direct access. Trade-off, in the architect's own retrospective: the ongoing need to "balance individual interests and needs for modification". Source: Safdie Architects https://www.safdiearchitects.com/projects/habitat-67 (T3).

**CR-03 The Interlace, Singapore — OMA / Ole Scheeren.** Residential; "one of the largest and most ambitious residential developments in Singapore"; "Thirty-one apartment blocks, each six stories tall and identical in length", in a "hexagonal arrangement around eight large-scale open and permeable courtyards"; "Instead of creating a cluster of isolated, vertical towers", a "vertical village of both shared and private outdoor spaces on multiple levels"; 170,000 m² built floor area, "over 1,000 residential units". Key decision: horizontal bars stacked to make courtyards at height. Trade-off: long access routes and complex structure for shared outdoor space at density. Source: OMA https://www.oma.com/projects/the-interlace (T3).

**CR-04 Nakagin Capsule Tower, Tokyo — Kisho Kurokawa, 1972; demolished 2022.** Residential/short-stay; 140 capsules of 2.5 × 2.5 × 4 m around two cores; capsules "expected to be replaced roughly every 25 to 35 years", which never happened because of physical interdependence and decay; at demolition "twenty-three capsules were salvaged … dispersed to institutions". Key decision: a Metabolist kit-of-parts with replaceable units. Failure: the interface (bolted, shared services, bolted-through structure) made replacement practically impossible, so the whole building aged at the rate of its worst joint. Source: designboom https://www.designboom.com/architecture/nakagin-capsule-tower-dream-home-change-kisho-kurokawa-tokyo/ (T3).

**CR-05 Paimio Sanatorium, Paimio — Alvar Aalto, 1933.** Hospital (tuberculosis sanatorium); "ambition to create healing environments that emulate nature", with patient needs placed above pure function; patient wing "oriented directly southward" with sun balconies; "each patient room was planned for two people"; interiors in "soft tones with darker ceilings"; taps angled to "prevent noise and splashing"; "cupboards were hung for ease of floor cleaning"; "yellow rubber flooring in the corridors"; "windows were designed to be draft proof" while admitting daily air as treatment. Key decision: the room, its balcony and its acoustics are the treatment. Trade-off: an extremely demanding envelope detail set for a public budget. Source: *Humanizing the hospital: Design lessons from a Finnish sanatorium*, PMC https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2917967/ (T2).

**CR-06 Gold Coast University Hospital, Queensland — Hassell/ArchitectureOne/STH, 2013.** Hospital; 175,000 m²; "a seven-storey atrium runs through the building, drawing daylight down through every floor"; sits within parkland "offering patients a view beyond the wards"; single-occupancy rooms credited with reducing "ward-to-ward patient transfers by more than 70 per cent"; "average patient stay has fallen from just over four days to under three"; stated as "evidence-based design: daylight, greenery and a seven-storey atrium helped cut average patient stays"; more than 5,000 people work at the facility. Key decision: one bed per room, all on the edge, with a public atrium spine. Trade-off: circulation and atrium area; not retrievable detail on cores and lifts. Sources: ArchiPro https://archipro.com.au/project/gold-coast-university-hospital-qld-sth (T3); Hassell https://www.hassellstudio.com/project/gold-coast-university-hospital (T3).

**CR-07 Sunshine Coast University Hospital, Queensland — Hassell.** Hospital; A$1.8 billion, on a "twenty-hectare greenfield site"; "six-storey, deep-plan West Building houses the principal diagnostic and treatment functions"; units are "L-shaped" forming "twinned, narrow, single-corridor 'fingers'"; "four light wells rising through the full height of the building"; "a north–south axis … anchored by the green spine of the major open courtyard that divides the hospital into two principal blocks"; the "main street is busy and active, lined with retail outlets, service providers, cafe facilities and open bays of seating"; "natural ventilation, although this ambition was ultimately ruled out for clinical reasons", cooled instead by "admission of coastal breezes drawn through the cool subfloor voids"; "the use of timber, soft lighting and warm colours in waiting areas eschews the sterile treatments common in public hospitals"; a "nine-storey public carpark aligned along the western boundary" acts as a shield. Key decision: deep plan broken into single-corridor fingers around a courtyard spine. Trade-off: the lost ventilation strategy and the exposed long facade of the fingers. Source: ArchitectureAu / Architecture Bulletin https://architectureau.com/articles/sunshine-coast-university-hospital/ (T3).

**CR-08 Maggie's West London Centre — Richard Rogers Partnership (RSHP), 2008.** Day cancer care centre inside a hospital campus; programme built "without resorting to area schedules and data sheets"; plan imagined "as a series of four 'tables'"; a two-storey central volume with a wood-burning stove realising "the notion of the 'hearth' as a focal space"; each zone "split into an internal space and an external terrace"; room sizes varying "from about 1 m² up to 50 m²"; "non-institutional finishes"; the founding client requirement was places "domestic in scale, making people feel instantly at home". Key decision: the hearth, not the reception desk, organises the plan. Trade-off: an explicit rejection of standard healthcare programming, which only a small, well-run, philanthropically funded programme can afford. Source: The Architects' Journal https://www.architectsjournal.co.uk/practice/culture/richard-rogers-on-maggies-centre-and-the-architecture-of-hope (T3).

**CR-09 Fogo Island Inn, Newfoundland — Todd Saunders / Saunders Architecture, 2013.** Hotel; 4,500 m² gross, 29 guest rooms "from 350 square feet to 1,100 square feet"; sited on the Back Western Shore so the building "hits the land directly without impacting the adjacent rocks, lichens and berries"; a "two storey west to east volume contains public spaces" and a "four storey south-west to north-east volume contains the remaining public spaces and all the guest rooms"; "highly insulated steel frame building" with "locally sourced and milled Black Spruce" cladding, "triple pane glazing"; rainwater "collected into two cisterns", hot water from "solar thermal on the outbuilding panels"; built with "local carpenters and craftspeople" to "foster cultural and economic resilience for this traditional fishing community". Key decision: separate the two volumes by public/private, put production below, keep every room on an edge. Floor programme as published: level 1 reception, dining room, kitchen, laundry, storage and mechanical installations; level 2 suites plus activity rooms; levels 3 and 4 the guest rooms ("traditional" on 3, "active" on 4), each level with "a separate ventilation system with heat recovery", vacuum toilets and "double framed and sealed" internal doors as a fire-safety-in-mass-timber argument. Trade-off: high envelope and services cost for a remote, operationally hard site. Source: gooood / ArchDaily https://www.gooood.cn/en/fogo-island-inn-by-saunders.htm (T3); BuildingGreen https://www.buildinggreen.com/feature/tread-lightly (T3).

**CR-10 Marina Bay Sands, Singapore — Safdie Architects.** Hotel + casino + convention + retail + museums, mixed-use; three towers linked at the fifty-seventh floor carrying about 2,600 guest rooms; a "2.5-acre garden" SkyPark crowning them with a 151 m infinity pool; "steel bridges suspend the park above concrete columns"; podium holds "casinos, conventions, shops, and museums"; "seamlessness of indoor and outdoor public space is a hallmark"; "building setbacks and glazed walls enhance natural light and views". Key decision: podium for the public, towers for the guest, roof for the spectacle. Trade-off: unprecedented structure and crowd management for a fully stacked program. Source: Safdie Architects https://www.safdiearchitects.com/projects/marina-bay-sands-hotel-and-skypark (T3).

**CR-11 The Edge, Amsterdam Zuidas — PLP Architecture, 2014.** Office; 40,000 m² / 430,556 ft²; Deloitte headquarters consolidating "multiple offices into a single highly connected environment"; a "multi-storey atrium acting as a social condenser, bringing together circulation, meeting spaces"; "exposed lift cores"; "massing tailored to daylight"; app-based desk allocation with sensors measuring "occupancy, movement, humidity, CO₂, light levels and internal climate"; "achieved the highest BREEAM score awarded to an office building at the time". Key decision: make the vertical and social circulation the visible atrium and let the plan flex by software. Trade-off: reliance on an operating system; if the app fails, the floor has no fixed identity. Sources: PLP Architecture https://www.plparchitecture.com/projects/the-edge (T3); ArchDaily https://www.archdaily.com/785967/the-edge-plp-architecture (T3).

**CR-12 Commerzbank Headquarters, Frankfurt — Foster + Partners, 1997.** Office tower; 300.1 m, 56 storeys, 109,200 m² floor area; plan is a "rounded equilateral triangle with a central, triangular atrium"; "at nine different levels, the atrium opens up to one of the three sides, forming large sky gardens"; "open areas allow more natural light in the building"; "constructed in steel rather than the conventional (and cheaper) concrete"; "environmentally friendly technologies were employed to reduce energy required for heating and cooling", the client having been "encouraged … to design a 'green' skyscraper". Key decision: a triangular plate with a void, so every workstation is close to an edge. Trade-off: steel cost and complex facade/atrium services for daylight and reduced mechanical load. Sources: Wikipedia https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commerzbank_Tower (T4, figure cross-check); Foster + Partners https://www.fosterandpartners.com/projects/commerzbank-headquarters/ (T3).

**CR-13 Bullitt Center, Seattle — The Miller Hull Partnership, 2013.** Office; "a six-storey office"; heavy-timber frame with "Douglas-fir glue-laminated (glulam) columns"; a "244 kW array" producing surplus power; a "56,000-gallon cistern" for rainwater; "foam-flush composting units"; stairs moved to "the glazed north-west corner" "to encourage usage"; windows and an "oversized solar canopy — overhangs the walls", giving "open plan lit entirely by daylight"; "US $32.5 million" financing; intended as a "250-year building"; regulatory friction meant "paperwork caught up", with potable rainwater use approved only later; lesson: barriers were "as much regulatory as technical". Key decision: shallow plate, stair as the front door, services as architecture. Trade-off: area and cost per desk for performance. Sources: Studio Matrx guide (T4, reporting Miller Hull data) https://www.studiomatrx.org/guides/bullitt-center; Miller Hull https://millerhull.com/2023/ten-years-later-the-bullitt-center-still-sets-the-standard-for-green-office-buildings/ (T3); Bullitt Center case study https://bullittcenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/08/living-proof-bullitt-center-case-study.pdf (T3).

**CR-14 Saunalahti Comprehensive School, Espoo — VERSTAS Architects, 2012.** School + community; about 10,000 m² serving 750 students; combines "comprehensive school classes … day care centre, preschool and a youth house"; planned in "home areas" where "classrooms group around small lobbies for collaboration", with "open group spaces with glass walls"; described as the "living room of the whole neighbourhood", integrating community use beyond school hours; 2007 competition, completed 2012. Key decision: cluster rooms around lobbies off shared space, and share the building with the district. Trade-off: supervision and acoustics; open plans demand more discipline from staff than corridors do. Sources: finland.fi https://finland.fi/life-society/building-an-even-better-finnish-school/ (T3); Divisare https://divisare.com/projects/238860-verstas-architects-andreas-meichsner-tuomas-uusheimo-saunalahti-comprehensive-school (T3); ArchDaily (ground-floor plan) https://www.archdaily.com/406513/saunalahti-school-verstas-architects (T3).

**CR-15 Fagus Factory, Alfeld — Walter Gropius & Adolf Meyer, 1911–1913.** Industrial (shoe lasts); "the clearest early built statement of the glass curtain wall"; structural piers retreat inward so the corners have "no column, no pier, nothing where the eye expects the most support"; the walls act as "a screen hung on the building rather than as a mass the building rests upon"; yellow-brick stair towers read as visible supports; the glazing programme treated the staff as "workers who deserve light and air"; UNESCO World Heritage Site 2011, still "a working factory". Key decision: free the corner and the perimeter so daylight and visibility enter the production hall. Trade-off: an early, expensive glass envelope for a factory; no direct energy strategy. Source: Studio Matrx canon note (T4) https://www.studiomatrx.org/architecture-canon/fagus-factory; UNESCO listing summary via https://www.archdaily.com/612249/ad-classics-fagus-factory-walter-gropius-adolf-meyer (T3).

**CR-16 Gläserne Manufaktur (Transparent Factory), Dresden — Volkswagen, 2001–2025.** Industrial + public; a glass factory in the city centre whose "floors are covered entirely in Canadian maple"; "up to 250 tourists per day" move through the production building; painted bodies "arrived by truck" and parts came by the CarGoTram; production ended December 2025 and the building's future use changed. Key decision: put the visitor route inside the production floor, so the process becomes the public programme. Trade-off: logistics had to solve what the glass box demanded (clean, quiet, visible deliveries in an urban setting), and the whole operation was tied to one product line's economics. Source: Wikipedia https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gl%C3%A4serne_Manufaktur (T4).

**CR-17 Selfridges, Birmingham (Bullring) — Future Systems, 2003.** Retail; 14,864 m² (160,000 sq ft) over six floors; "steel framework with sprayed concrete facade"; the block "wraps around the corner of Moor Street and Park Street", taking two street frontages; movement by "interior escalators"; part of the Bullring Shopping Centre. Key decision: small deep floorplates with vertical public movement, and a wrap that gives the building two street addresses. Trade-off: the sculpted envelope costs structure and services for a compact plate; upper floors depend entirely on escalator capacity. Sources: Wikipedia https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selfridges_Birmingham (T4); RIBA Journal on the cladding https://www.ribaj.com/intelligence/selfridges-birmingham-future-systems-retail-cladding/ (T3).

**CR-18 Sendai Mediatheque — Toyo Ito & Associates, 2000/2001.** Civic, mixed library/gallery/cinema/studio; "a transparent cultural media center" (ArchDaily); seven levels of facilities in a "mixed-program public facility which combines library and art gallery"; supported by "a forest of 13 non-uniform tubes which appear to rise fluidly through the building", the structure being "flat concrete slabs (which are honeycomb steel plates with concrete) penetrated by 13 tubes"; "walls on each floor are kept to an absolute minimum", allowing functions to be "freely distributed throughout the open areas between the tubes"; Ito: "Sendai Mediatheque embodies our proposal for a completely new concept of architecture." Key decision: pull lifts, toilets and services into tubes so the plate can be wall-less. Trade-off: an expensive honeycomb-plate structure and long crossing distances for a very free plan. Sources: Architectuul https://architectuul.com/architecture/sendai-mediatheque (T3); ArchDaily AD Classics https://www.archdaily.com/118627/ad-classics-sendai-mediatheque-toyo-ito-and-associates-architects (T3).

**CR-19 Helsinki Central Library Oodi — ALA Architects, 2018.** Civic/library + workshops + café; 17,100 m² total over three floors; ground floor as "zero-threshold public spaces"; upper levels with "recording and editing studios, maker space, movie theater"; the top level a "calm and contemplative area floating above the busy central Helsinki"; an "arching wooden volume" ties the levels, with a "double helix stair" and balconies; the design follows the "dynamic between the site and the goals of the library program". Key decision: a public ground floor with no turnstile, a social middle, a quiet top. Trade-off: heavy annual cost for a building whose ground floor is not a library. Source: ALA Architects https://ala.fi/work/helsinki-central-library/ (T3); Dezeen https://www.dezeen.com/2019-01-10/helsinki-central-library-oodi-ala-architects/ (T3).

**CR-20 Kansai International Airport Passenger Terminal — Renzo Piano Building Workshop, 1988–1994.** Transit; RPBW describes the terminal as "1.7 km long, making it the longest terminal in the world", able to "accommodate 100,000 passengers per day"; operations rely on the "open departures level of the Main Terminal Building", with "the 42 boarding gates … housed within the 'wings'"; structure uses "three-dimensional beams spanning 80 m" and a section that keeps "reflecting natural light from the skylights above"; the seismic and settlement conditions of the island shaped its linear profile, and the intent is "a rational geometry that ensures functional efficiency". Key decision: one long, single-level, daylighted departures spine with gates hung off it, extendable in phases. Trade-off: walking distance along the plate, bought back by legibility. Source: RPBW https://www.rpbw.com/project/kansai-international-airport-terminal (T3); Fondazione Renzo Piano https://www.fondazionerenzopiano.org/en/project/kansai-international-airport-passenger-terminal-building/ (T3).

**CR-21 Operational guidance records (a–h; not single buildings, used only where a project cannot carry a number).** (a) Hotel planning: standard guest rooms 28–30 m² midscale, 32–38 m² upper upscale, 40 m²+ luxury, suites 55–75 m²; corridors 1.22 m minimum clear, "typically 1.5 to 1.8 m"; double-loaded slabs 65–72 % net-to-gross, single-loaded 10–15 points worse; BOH 15–25 % of gross; kitchen ratio 0.5:1 for full-service, 0.3:1 satellite; "one [lift] station per 75 guest rooms"; dead-end corridors limited to 6.1 m sprinklered / 15.2 m (Archgyan, T4). (b) Hotel structure: guest-room module 3.6 m+ with widths 3.3–3.8 m, column grids 7.0–8.5 m so "guestrooms can be arranged efficiently between columns" (Hotel Development Guide, T4); "the relationship between gross floor area and net revenue-producing area is one of the most critical metrics in hotel development" (same, architectural planning). (c) Hotel BOH: kitchens, laundries, storage, receiving, trash, housekeeping, front-office support; services "hidden from the hotel guest rooms"; trucks moving "without disrupting the guest parking"; "clear separation of incoming and outgoing goods"; linen chute to "every floor in the service elevator landing"; staff break areas (Studio Puisto, T4). (d) Warehousing: 12 m primary bay; clear heights 9–10 m for 5–6-level selective racking, 11–12 m for reach/VNA turret, 14–18 m high-bay automated; 1 dock per 1,000–1,500 m²; sill 1.20 m; apron 12 m rigid / 18–22 m articulated; aisles counterbalance 3.5–4.0 m, reach 2.7–3.0 m, VNA 1.5–1.8 m; shares bulk 50–65 %, receiving 8–12 %, dispatch 8–12 %, pick 5–10 %, office/welfare/plant 5–10 % (PEB Steel, T4); sprinkler clearance to top pallet "normally 1 m under UK BS EN 12845", SEMA gaps 75 mm top, 75 mm side, 100 mm back-to-back double runs (Umstoragesystems, T4). (e) Manufacturing layout archetypes: process layout = flexibility with "high (long travel paths)" handling and high WIP; product layout = "lowest (linear flow)" handling cost but "stops entirely if one machine fails"; U-shaped cellular layout with 30–50 % setup reduction and 40 %+ WIP reduction; fixed-position for oversized products (Oxmaint, T4). (f) Early-years/childcare: Hesse requires room height ≥ 2.80 m and corridor width ≥ 2.00 m in new kindergarten buildings, with common groups of 20–25 children (HEWI, T4). (g) Retail layout: grid 35–45 % fixture coverage with 1.2–1.8 m aisles, loop 25–35 % with 1.5–2.1 m aisles, free-flow 15–25 %; decompression zone 1.5–3 m clear inside the entrance; aisles under 0.9 m kill lower-shelf browsing; "cash counter facing the entrance reads as a barrier … side it" (Retail Design, T4); main aisles 1,500 mm+, "the accessible dimension binds — it is law", and the "shoppers always turn right" claim is a myth (Studio Matrx retail planning, T4). (h) Healthcare dimension standards: US FGI increases — "multiple-infant rooms increased from 120 square feet to 150 square feet per patient", "single-infant rooms increased from 155 square feet to 180 square feet" ( Consulting-Specifying Engineer on the 2022 FGI Guidelines, T3).

## Weak or contested

- **The hospitality number library is T4.** Guest-room areas by tier, 1.5–1.8 m corridors, 65–72 % net-to-gross, BOH 15–25 %, kitchen 0.5:1 / 0.3:1, "one lift station per 75 guest rooms" all come from trade/vendor guides that agree with each other in direction but not in denominator (net vs gross, whether the corridor inside a suite counts, whether the service lift lobby counts). Rules RP-04 to RP-07 are therefore written as *measure and publish*, never as "must equal". The one hospitality figure with a code flavour (dead-end 6.1 m sprinklered / 15.2 m) was not verified against a code text and should be checked in `building-codes.md` before use.
- **Maggie's brief as evidence of programming.** Rogers' statement that the centre was designed "without resorting to area schedules and data sheets" is a real and quotable design position, but it is *not* a general recommendation: the Maggie's model works because the programme is small, philanthropically funded and user-tested. Any inference that healthcare programming can be skipped is unsupported by this register. The published Maggie's Architecture and Landscape Brief (which does contain room schedules) could not be read — its PDF is image-only in this pass.
- **Outcome claims from promotional project reporting.** "Transfers down more than 70 %", "stay from just over four days to under three", "no sustained pathogen transmission", "highest BREEAM score for an office at the time" are as stated by the project's own architects or the operator, with no study design, comparator or confound control disclosed. Use them as directional evidence that the design was *intended and monitored*, never as a predicted effect of the same move in a different project. Rules RP-08 and RP-23 are capped at Medium-High for this reason and never reach High on these figures (RP-08 previously read "High ... with outcome data in one", which promoted the rule on exactly the evidence this bullet disowns); "no sustained pathogen transmission" is listed here as a claim this register carries **no** rule for - it survived from an earlier draft of the GCUH record and must not be reintroduced.
- **Warehouse and factory figures come from steel and racking vendors.** Their interest is in longer racks and bigger envelopes. The three-way agreement on aisle widths (counterbalance 3.5–4.0 m, reach 2.7–3.0 m, VNA 1.5–1.8 m, two independent sources) is the strongest of them; the functional area percentages and the "12 m primary bay" claim are single-sourced heuristics and should be re-derived per project from the actual equipment list. The SEMA clearance figures were reported second-hand.
- **Retail layout percentages (35–45 / 25–35 / 15–25 % fixture coverage, decompression zone 1.5–3 m).** Vendor and student-guide material only. The "shoppers always turn right" claim is correctly labelled a myth by one source, so the register adopts the *sceptical* version: path shape and sightlines matter, handedness does not. RP-21 keeps the geometric parts (aisle width, clear entry zone, loop coverage) and drops the behavioural determinism.
- **The Fagus Factory rationale is contested as narrative.** The "corners without columns so that light and supervision reach the workers" reading is standard and quotable, but Gropius's own accounting of motive is less clean than the textbook version, and the factory's real innovation is structural detailing rather than worker welfare. The register uses Fagus for one transferable claim only (RP-18: keep the perimeter band clear so the working floor is daylit and visible) and cites no dimensions from it, since the main-block sizes could not be verified.
- **Sendai Mediatheque's seismic performance** is widely reported (it survived the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake essentially undamaged, with contents undisturbed) and would justify a "resilient void" rule, but the claim could not be verified against a T1/T2 engineering source in this pass, so no rule rests on it.
- **Habitat 67 vs Nakagin: the same idea, opposite outcomes.** Both used stacked units; one remained desirable, one was demolished. The difference visible in the sources is the interface and the tenure (Safdie's modules carry structure and each unit owns a terrace and direct access; Kurokawa's capsules depended on shared cores and bolted joints nobody could sever). That is a plausible reading of the documented facts, not a proven causal claim — RP-22 states it as the rule "verify replaceability at the interface", which stands on either explanation.
- **Gaps this pass could not close.** (i) No retrievable peer-reviewed source on ward layout geometry and nurse travel (MDPI and IOP both blocked) — RP-10 and RP-11 therefore carry project evidence plus heuristics, not measured thresholds. (ii) No readable school area standard (BB103, BB99, Irish and Victorian post-primary guides all image-only or 403), so the only school figure in this file is a computed gross m²-per-place ratio from Saunalahti's two published numbers. (iii) No primary lift-traffic standard, so RP-04's lift-count logic borrows the T4 "one station per 75 guest rooms". (iv) No verified floor areas for several high-value plans (Kyoto Station, Acros Fukuoka, Inada Hotel, Lloyd's of London, Pixel) — they are omitted rather than described from memory. (v) No post-occupancy wayfinding data, so RP-24 is Medium confidence.
