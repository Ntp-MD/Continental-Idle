# Research file 07 — Adjacency & spatial graphs (graph formalism + derived-plan algorithms)

Scope: the graph layer only — what a plan *is* as a graph, how that graph is derived from a tile
plan, which graph quantities predict behaviour, which adjacency programmes are geometrically
impossible, and how to repair a failing programme. General architecture theory, adjacency-grading
convention and the four conflict levers are owned by `architecture-theory.md` (TH-01…TH-05, TH-20)
and egress/dead-end numbers by `building-codes.md` (CODE-02…CODE-08); this file extends them with
formalism and executable procedures and does not restate them.

Host grid this file is written against: `1 tile = 0.5 m`; tile states `walkable | blocked | door`
only; a wall is one blocked tile (0.5 m); a door is a run of door tiles; rooms are 4-connected
flood-fills of walkable tiles typed by fixture tags; movement is A\* octile with diagonals allowed
only when both corner neighbours are open; vertical links are lift/stair portals with queues;
access is granted by tags. **The graph is never authored directly — it is derived** by flood-fill +
door detection + BFS/A\*, so every rule below states its derivation.

Derived constants used throughout: outer face `W×H` tiles ⇒ interior `(W−2)×(H−2)` walkable tiles;
wall ring of a rectangular room = `2W + 2H − 4` tiles; 1 tile of frontier = 0.5 m of shared wall;
a room graph with `n` nodes has at most `3n − 6` adjacency edges and at most `2n − 5` independent
movement circuits (both follow from Euler's bound, derived below).

---

## Rules

### AG-01 Derive three separate graphs from one plan, never one merged graph
- Rule: For every validated plan produce exactly these three structures, in this order: (1) `G_adj` — rooms, corridors, outside and floor-nodes as vertices, an edge per shared-wall segment; (2) `G_acc(role)` — a directed-multiset graph with one edge per door run, carrying traversal cost and the door's permitted roles, one instance per role; (3) `G_ver` — floor and room vertices joined by lift/stair portal edges. Report all three plus the measured-vs-required matrix; a single undifferentiated "room graph" is not a checkable artefact.
- Evidence: Space syntax already keeps the layers apart: the convex-space graph ("y-map", the *convex adjacency graph*) is defined as "convex spaces represented by small circles and **permeable adjacencies** by lines" (Hillier & Hanson 1984, p.100, as documented in the Space Syntax glossary), i.e. permeability is a property of the link, not of the contact; and depth "exists wherever it is necessary to go through **intervening spaces**" (ibid. p.108). House-GAN states the failure mode of merging them: its generators assume "an edge property (i.e. room adjacency) does not reflect the presence of doors" and list that as a limitation to be fixed (Nauata et al. 2020).
- Source: Space Syntax Online Training Platform, glossary terms "Convex adjacency graph", "Depth", "Connectivity" https://www.spacesyntax.online/glossary/ — T3; Nauata, Chang, Cheng, Mori, Furukawa, "House-GAN: Relational Generative Adversarial Networks for Graph-constrained House Layout Generation" https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.06988 — T2.
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT (data model)
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High [classification: the rule also carries DESIGN PRINCIPLE for its part of the claim - the field holds one label per spec §27]
- Grid translation: `rooms = connected_components(walkable, 4)`; `walls = connected_components(blocked, 4)`; `doorruns = connected_components(door, 4)`. Edge rules: `A ~ B in G_adj` iff ∃ blocked tile whose two *opposite* 4-neighbours belong to A and B (opposite = collinear through the tile; corner-post contacts are not adjacencies). `A → B in G_acc(r)` iff ∃ door run d with a 4-neighbour in A and a 4-neighbour in B, and `r ∈ d.perms`; cost = `A*(exit(A), entry(B)) + 1`. Validation: no edge may exist in `G_acc` without a supporting entry in `G_adj` (a door into a non-adjacent room is a modelling bug, not a design), and every `G_acc(role)` must be reported with its own component count.
- Exceptions / failure mode: A plan that passes an "adjacency" check but fails reachability (TH-04) is the symptom of a merged graph. Second symptom: a corridor whose only function is circulation is analysed as a "room", inflating integration statistics — tag corridors explicitly as their own vertex class.

### AG-02 Treat the relationship matrix as four predicate types, not one score
- Rule: Every programme relationship is stored as one of four typed predicates with different verification logic: `REQUIRED_ADJ(i,j)` (must share a wall and have a permitted door), `PREFERRED_CLOSE(i,j,τ)` (bounded path cost, no wall requirement), `SEPARATE(i,j,δ)` (minimum path cost and/or Euclidean distance, no direct door), `PROHIBIT(i,j)` (no direct door for any role, no shared wall). Weights rank importance *within* a type; they never convert one type into another.
- Evidence: The facility-layout tradition uses exactly this separation: a graded relationship chart whose positive grades drive proximity and whose separate "X" grade encodes unwanted adjacency, with X weighted as a large penalty (A=16, E=8, I=4, O=1, U=0, X=−80) rather than as a small positive (Resource Systems Consulting, *Simplified Systematic Layout Planning*, describing Muther's activity-relationship chart). Healthcare guidance states the same split functionally: "Plan for and deliver good separation of clean and dirty activities" and "Clean and dirty areas should be kept separate and the workflow patterns of each area should be clearly defined" (NHS England, *Health Building Note 00-09*, 2012).
- Source: Resource Systems Consulting https://www.resourcesystemsconsulting.com/2006-10-22/simplified-systematic-layout-planning/ — T4; Richard Muther, *Systematic Layout Planning*, 4th ed., PDF published by Richard Muther Associates https://richardmuther.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/Systematic-Layout-Planning-SLP-4th-edition-soft-copy.pdf (exists; text not machine-readable this pass) — T3; NHS England HBN 00-09 https://www.england.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/HBN_00-09_infection_control.pdf — T1.
- Class: STANDARD (notation)
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High for the four-type structure; Medium for the specific numeric weights (single T4 source) [classification: the rule also carries FACT for its part of the claim - the field holds one label per spec §27]
- Grid translation: Verification predicates over the derived graphs: `REQUIRED_ADJ(i,j)` ⇔ `edge(i,j) ∈ G_adj ∧ ∃ door run with endpoints in both`; `PREFERRED_CLOSE(i,j,τ)` ⇔ `min cost over G_acc(role_public) ≤ τ` tiles (τ from programme, 8–16 tiles = 4–8 m typical, consistent with TH-03); `SEPARATE(i,j,δ)` ⇔ `¬∃ direct door ∧ min cost ≥ δ`; `PROHIBIT(i,j)` ⇔ `¬edge(i,j) ∈ G_adj ∧ ¬∃ door run shared`. Report one row per pair: type, required value, measured value, verdict.
- Exceptions / failure mode: The commonest error is writing a separation as "grade 0" and then satisfying it by thickening a wall to two tiles (fake separation, TH-04) — check distance, not wall thickness. Second: a prohibited pair that is only prohibited for *some* roles (public must not enter the kitchen; staff must) — encode the role set on the prohibition, and run the predicate per role.

### AG-03 Screen the must-touch graph for planarity before you place any tile
- Rule: Run a planarity test on the required-adjacency graph before geometry. If it is non-planar, the programme is unsatisfiable on a 2-D tile grid: no wall-sharing layout of connected rooms can produce it. Decompose the offending part into a Kuratowski subgraph (a subdivision of K5 or K3,3) and hand the user that named obstruction, not a generic "infeasible".
- Evidence: Region adjacency graphs of plane maps are planar, and the standard planarity criterion is Kuratowski's theorem (a finite graph is planar iff it contains no subgraph that is a subdivision of K5 or K3,3) — see West, *Introduction to Graph Theory*, 2nd ed. (Prentice-Hall, 1996) and Diestel, *Graph Theory*, free edition ch. 4 (space syntax itself defines a planar graph as one "that can be drawn in a plane without graph edges crossing", Wilson 1996 p.162, quoted in the Space Syntax glossary). A linear-time method for *finding* the Kuratowski subgraph is in Williamson, "Depth-First Search and Kuratowski Subgraphs", *J. ACM* (1984). Contact graphs of isothetic rectangles are planar and "play an important role in … architecture (floor planning)" (Buchsbaum, Gansner, Procopiuc, Venkatasubramanian, "Rectangular Layouts and Contact Graphs", arXiv:cs/0611107 / ACM Trans. Algorithms 4(1) 2008, quoting Koebe's theorem that "any planar graph (and only a planar graph) can be expressed as the contact graph of disks").
- Source: Buchsbaum et al. https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0611107 — T2; Diestel, *Graph Theory* https://diestel-graph-theory.com/ — T2; Williamson, J. ACM https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/1634.322451 — T2; Space Syntax glossary "Planar graph" — T3.
- Class: FACT (mathematical)
- Scope: universal; 2-D per-floor graphs only
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: `if |E_req| > 3n − 6 → non-planar (fast reject, n ≥ 3)`; else run a linear-time planarity test (Hopcroft–Tarjan / Boyer–Myrvold) and, on failure, extract and report the K5/K3,3 subdivision. Threshold: reject at once. Note `3n − 6` is the simple-graph bound; multi-doors between a pair are not extra adjacency edges.
- Exceptions / failure mode: A non-planar *derived* graph from a real plan means a degenerate structure, not magic: look for an isolated blocked tile (pillar) with walkable tiles on all four sides, or a wall chain that is only 8-connected (diagonal walls) — both let one wall tile witness two perpendicular adjacencies and cross arcs. Repair the wall model (AG-19) before declaring the programme impossible. Vertical stacking also escapes planarity: an unembeddable pair-set may become embeddable when split across floors (AG-16).

### AG-04 Cap mutual adjacency by shape class: 5 is impossible, 4 needs a non-rectangular room
- Rule: A group of rooms that must all touch each other is capped by planarity, not by taste. Clique ≥ 5 is impossible on any 2-D grid — report the K5 obstruction and convert it into hub contacts. Clique = 4 is only achievable if at least one member is non-rectangular (L/U-shaped) or if the four meet in a degenerate point junction, so with a rectangular-room kit-of-parts it is a warning, not a plan. Always state the shape class before judging a clique: rectilinear rooms (host default) ⇒ any planar must-touch graph is embeddable given enough grid; rectangular rooms ⇒ additional facial-triangle-type conditions apply; rectangular rooms tiling a rectangle with no gaps (rectangular dual) ⇒ stricter still.
- Evidence: "Every graph that can be represented this way [as a contact graph of axis-aligned rectangles with non-zero-length boundary contacts] is planar and every triangle in such a graph is a facial triangle. These two conditions are also sufficient to guarantee a rectangle representation" (Bekos, van Dijk, Kobourov, Pupyrev et al., "Improved Approximation Algorithms for Box Contact Representations", arXiv:1403.4861v2, ESA'14, quoting Buchsbaum et al. 2008). For the gap-free case: "a planar graph G with all faces triangular except the outside has a rectangular dual if and only if G can be obtained from some 4-triangulation H by the deletion of some degree-4 vertex and all its neighbors" (Koźmiński & Kinnen, as reported in Buchsbaum et al.), and "the proscription of gaps … severely limits the class of graphs that admit rectangular duals; for example, **paths are the only trees that have such duals**" (Buchsbaum et al., ibid.). K5 is impossible in any planar graph (Kuratowski); K4 is the largest clique planarity allows, and whether K4 also survives the extra rectangle conditions is the open point flagged in ## Weak or contested.
- Source: Bekos et al. https://arxiv.org/html/1403.4861v2 — T2; Buchsbaum et al. https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0611107 — T2; Koźmiński & Kinnen, "Rectangular duals of planar graphs", *Networks* 15 (1985), abstract https://doi.org/10.1002/net.3230150202 — T2.
- Class: FACT (mathematical; applied as an embeddability constraint on the grid)
- Scope: universal on a planar grid; per-shape-class conditions as listed
- Confidence: High for the ≥ 5 rejection (planarity, quoted results); Medium for the K4 claim — I could not construct a four-rectangle realisation of K4, and the literature's rectangle-representation conditions come in several non-obviously-equivalent forms (facial triangles, "filled triangles", 4-connected triangulations), so K4-with-rectangles is an open check, not a theorem. See ## Weak or contested.
- Grid translation: Reject `REQUIRED_ADJ` cliques of size ≥ 5 outright (report "5 mutually adjacent rooms cannot exist; make one pair corridor-connected instead"). For size-4 cliques: accept only if rooms may be rectilinear and each pair has ≥ 1 wall tile of its own; if the plan requires rectangular room footprints (kit-of-parts templates, TH-23), warn "K4 needs an L-shaped room or a shared hall". For gap-free rectangular floors, test `triangulated + internally 4-connected` and report the missing frame vertex.
- Exceptions / failure mode: The frequent real case is "four departments all mutually adjacent" (e.g. kitchen–pantry–service–dining): the honest solution is a shared hall/servery vertex — i.e. convert three direct contacts into hub contacts (lever 2 of TH-05) and re-score. Symptom of ignoring this rule: an agent keeps re-running the solver on an unsatisfiable clique and burns the iteration budget.

### AG-05 Bound every room's degree by its wall frontier
- Rule: A room can be adjacent to at most as many distinct rooms as it has exposed wall tiles, and in practice no more than about one adjacency per 2 frontier tiles. Compute this capacity per room from its programme area before solving, and treat "must-touch k rooms" as an area requirement, not a free constraint.
- Evidence: Direct consequence of the adjacency definition (each adjacency consumes a distinct wall tile separating the two rooms, collinear through it); the same arithmetic appears in the "wall tax" of TH-02 (a W×H outer room loses `2W + 2H − 4` tiles to its own ring). Space syntax uses an analogous geometric bound when it defines the "fattest" convex space by *area/perimeter ratio* (Hillier & Hanson 1984, p.17, via the Space Syntax glossary), i.e. perimeter is treated as a real design resource.
- Source: derived arithmetic on the host grid; Space Syntax glossary "Area-perimeter ratio" https://www.spacesyntax.online/glossary/ — T3; Neufert, *Architects' Data*, 3rd English ed., Wiley 2012 https://ia600801.us.archive.org/21/items/NeufertS/Neufert-S.pdf (room-by-room adjacency requirements; no number taken) — T3.
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High for the bound; Low for the "1 per 2 tiles" practical factor (heuristic)
- Grid translation: `capacity(A) = exposed_wall_tiles(A)`, where exposed = blocked tiles with a 4-neighbour in A on one side and a *different* room on the opposite side. Pass if `deg_req(A) ≤ capacity(A)`; warn if `deg_req(A) > capacity(A)/2`. For a rectangular template room, `capacity = 2W + 2H − 4`. Minimum footprint for a hub touching k rooms: `outer perimeter ≥ 2k` tiles.
- Exceptions / failure mode: A "hub" corridor is where this bites hardest: a 2-tile-wide corridor 20 tiles long has ~44 exposed tiles, so a 12-room leaf requirement is geometrically trivial; but a 4×4 "lobby" asked to touch 8 named rooms is not (capacity 12, and each door needs 2 tiles of run width: TH-13). Second failure mode: agents satisfy degree with 1-tile wall frontiers that read as adjacency but cannot host a door (0.5 m of frontage, no legal door width) — check door capacity, not just adjacency (AG-12).

### AG-06 Use a six-grade closeness scale with an explicit forbidden grade, and integer weights
- Rule: Store the relationship matrix as integers with a distinct negative grade for "must not touch", so that proximity and avoidance are both computable: 5 = absolutely necessary, 4 = especially desirable, 3 = important, 2 = ordinary, 1 = unimportant, 0 = no relationship, −1 = undesirable/prohibited. Satisfy in descending grade; never let a low-grade positive edge block a high-grade one.
- Evidence: The facility-planning scale is documented as "A – Absolutely necessary, E – Especially important, I – Important, O – Okay, U – Unimportant, X – Undesirable", with the evaluation weights A=16, E=8, I=4, O=1, U=0, X=−80 (Resource Systems Consulting, *Simplified Systematic Layout Planning*, describing Muther's activity-relationship chart; the primary book is Muther, *Systematic Layout Planning*). The architecture-side matrix convention (reading relationship symbols off a square matrix against its diagonal) is documented in T4/T2 sources already cited by TH-03, which is where this file picks up.
- Source: Resource Systems Consulting — T4; Muther, *Systematic Layout Planning*, 4th ed. https://richardmuther.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/Systematic-Layout-Planning-SLP-4th-edition-soft-copy.pdf — T3 (title/existence verified, text not readable); Gustin Design Services, *Programming Matrix* https://gdsatx.com/2018-09-27/programming-matrix/ — T4 (via TH-03).
- Class: STANDARD (notation)
- Scope: universal; type-specific weight tables in ## Type-specificity audit
- Confidence: High for the graded structure; Low–Medium for letter wordings (sources disagree: "especially important" vs "especially desirable", "okay" vs "ordinary"), and the exact numbers are one consultant's scheme [classification: the rule also carries HEURISTIC for its part of the claim - the field holds one label per spec §27]
- Grid translation: Keep one integer matrix `M[i][j] ∈ {5,4,3,2,1,0,−1}` as the programme; the derived measured matrix `M̂` reports, per pair, `(adjacent?, door?, cost_tiles)`. Report the score `Σ w(|M|)·satisfied` and separately `Σ w(|M|)·violated`, with the X/−1 grade scored as a hard fail, never as a large negative number that can be traded off against positives (a prohibited adjacency cannot be "paid for").
- Exceptions / failure mode: Real programmes use grade 5 for dozens of pairs in a small building (over-constrained); the correction is AG-17's pre-flight, not more solver effort. Second failure: agents reusing the same scale for *vertical* relationships (which floors stack) — verticality is a separate predicate (AG-16), because a lift ride is not a weak adjacency.

### AG-07 Zone by partitioning the weighted relationship graph, then verify zone contiguity in tiles
- Rule: Derive zones (territories) by graph partitioning on the positive grades, not by colouring rooms by hand: find the minimum-weight-cut clustering of the `G_req` graph, assign each cluster to a zone, then require each zone's rooms to form one connected set in `G_adj ∪ G_acc`. Zones are constraints on the graph, and their contiguity is a graph property you can measure.
- Evidence: Zoning/clustering is a named computational space-planning technique ("Neighbor Searching techniques, Switching techniques, Random techniques, **Zoning Clustering**, Virtual Grid Searching Methods, Bubble Diagram simulation…" — Lobos & Donath, "The problem of space layout in architecture: A survey and reflections", *Arquitetura Revista* 6(2):136–161, 2010); SLP's phase sequence produces a "space relationship diagram" from the relationship chart before any geometry. The algorithmic machinery is standard: Kernighan & Lin's heuristic min-cut partition (Bell System Technical Journal, 1970) and modularity-based community detection (Newman & Girvan, *Phys. Rev. E* 69:026113, 2004).
- Source: Lobos & Donath https://revistas.unisinos.br/index.php/arquitetura/article/download/4554/1785 — T2; Kernighan & Lin https://doi.org/10.1002/j.1538-7305.1970.tb01770.x — T2; Newman & Girvan https://doi.org/10.1103/physreve.69.026113 — T2.
- Class: STANDARD (method)
- Scope: universal; zone count 2–4 on this grid
- Confidence: High that partitioning is computable; Medium that automatic clusters match the intended public/private/service split (they follow the matrix, including its errors) [classification: the rule also carries CODE REQUIREMENT for its part of the claim - the field holds one label per spec §27]
- Grid translation: Input `M`; build weighted graph on grades ≥ 3; run Kernighan–Lin 2-way cut recursively (or greedy modularity) until clusters have no positive internal edge above 0 crossing the cut; label clusters by their dominant tag (public/private/service) and hand-check the labels. Verify per zone: `connected(rooms(z), G_adj ∪ G_acc)` true; `cut_weight(z, other zones)` reported; `Σ over zone boundaries of door count` reported. Pass threshold: 0 disconnected fragments per zone; ≤ 1 intended boundary door per pair of adjacent zones.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Deliberately disconnected zones (a campus-type "island" nursery) are legal if declared — record the exception rather than failing the check. Second: partitioning merges "connected through circulation" with "adjacent", so a service corridor can fuse two territories; run the clustering on `G_adj` only for territory shape and on `G_acc` for movement. Third: with the centrality paradox in mind (maximising internal integration maximises external segregation — Hillier 1996 p.266, via the Space Syntax glossary), a tightly-looped zone is *predictably* hard to find from outside; that is the point of a private zone, and it must not be "fixed" by adding public through-routes.

### AG-08 Translate a bubble diagram to the grid in five measured steps and publish the residual
- Rule: Never jump from matrix to tiles. Sequence: (1) scale each node to its programme area in tiles; (2) embed the must-touch graph (rectilinear regions, AG-04 class); (3) snap regions to the tile grid; (4) insert circulation into the interstices and place door runs on the shared frontiers; (5) recompute the measured matrices and publish the residual (unsatisfied weight). The residual is the deliverable of the step, not the picture.
- Evidence: The bubble/adjacency pair is the standard schematic artefact and the two are read together (matrix → bubble → block plan; Gustin T4 and the "traditional adjacency matrix and space bubble diagram" figure cited in TH-03). The computational literature treats exactly this translation as the hard part: "given a set of objects and some relationships between them, making a layout that contains such objects and such relationships" (Lobos & Donath 2010); "bubble diagram simulation" is a named technique class (ibid.); adjacency-as-spring force models were already criticised for ignoring boundary (`Cons: It does not use boundary`, Arvin & House 1999 as reviewed there). Generative work measures the outcome the way this rule asks: House-GAN's task is "to take an architectural constraint as a graph … and produce a set of axis-aligned bounding boxes of rooms", scored on "realism, diversity, and **compatibility with the input graph constraint**" (Nauata et al. 2020); Graph2Plan "converts a layout graph, along with a building boundary, into a floorplan" (Hu, Huang, Tang, van Kaick, Zhang & Huang, *ACM Transactions on Graphics*, 2020; arXiv:2004.13204). The same translation is solved the classical way in the rule-based line — dimensioned floorplans generated from a given adjacency graph (Shekhawat, Upasani, Bisht & Jain, *Automation in Construction* 127:103718, 2021; Bisht, Shekhawat, Upasani, Jain, Tiwaskar & Hebbar, "Transforming an adjacency graph into dimensioned floorplan layouts", *Computer Graphics Forum* 41:5–22, 2022), and WallPlan's framing of plan synthesis as generating the *wall graph* (Sun et al., *ACM TOG* 41(4), 2022, per its title and GSDiff's description: it "converts floorplan generation as a graph generation task" but emits images, not vectors) — which is AG-01's order of derivation in generative clothing. Area-vs-adjacency tension is formalised as NP-hard contact-representation optimisation (Bekos et al. 2014, citing strong NP-hardness of Max-Crown even for trees, per Barth et al.).
- Source: Nauata et al. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2003.06988 — T2; Graph2Plan https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13204 — T2; Lobos & Donath — T2; Gustin Design Services — T4; Hu, Wu, Wang, Xu & Zheng, *Advancing Architectural Floorplan Design with Geometry-enhanced Graph Diffusion* (GSDiff), arXiv:2408.16258 (cs.GR), 2024 — https://arxiv.org/html/2408.16258v1 — T2, read directly: the current-generation evidence that this translation is **not** a single weighted objective — adjacency/relationship satisfaction is carried by a separate edge-prediction term (`L_edge = L_cls + L_λ`, classification plus self-supervised interpolation), while geometric validity is carried by node reconstruction plus an alignment term weighted by `ω(t)` (higher at small timesteps), and the authors motivate the design by saying prior methods fail to guarantee explicit geometric constraints such as misalignment. That is an independent, computational confirmation of step (5): publish the residual per term, because the generator itself cannot trade one against the other. Graph2Plan, read directly the same pass (https://arxiv.org/html/2004.13204), shows the same structure one generation earlier — its objective decomposes into pixel-wise, regression/graph and geometric terms (coverage, interior, mutex, match) with **no scalar weight balancing adjacency against geometry**, and its ablation reports layout quality collapsing when the graph/geometry conditioning is removed (IoU 0.66 → 0.43, as extracted from that paper's ablation; treat the pair as an order-of-magnitude signal, not a transferable number).
- Class: STANDARD (procedure)
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High for the procedure; High for "residual is nonzero in general" (NP-hardness results)
- Grid translation: (1) `tiles_i = ceil(area_m²_i × 4)`; (2) embed with the derived construction (rectilinear regions, non-crossing "tubes" along a planar drawing) or copy a kit-of-parts template; (3) quantise, then re-run flood-fill so measured areas match programme ± the wall tax (TH-02); (4) doors: for each satisfied `G_adj` edge, put a 2-tile door run on the frontier tiles closest to `A*`'s corridor through the pair; (5) publish `residual = Σ w(M_ij) · ¬satisfied(i,j)` and `unsatisfied_pairs` list. Pass: residual 0 for grade 5; report grades ≤ 3 as trade-off notes.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Symptom of a bad snap: rooms whose measured area differs from programme by more than the wall tax (the agent has been "adding tiles to make adjacencies"), or a door placed where the two rooms share only a 1-tile frontier of 0.5 m (no legal run). If the residual will not fall, the programme — not the drawing — is wrong: go to AG-17.

### AG-09 Optimise weighted path cost, not straight-line distance
- Rule: Score and drive the design by `Σ_ij w_ij · d_ij` where `d_ij` is the A\* step cost in the movement graph actually walkable by the acting role, not the Euclidean/tile distance. Straight-line or "as-the-crow-flies" adjacency is an invalid proxy in a walled plan.
- Evidence: The layout-planning tradition minimises interaction × separation between connected programmatic elements; the practice-side stacking study does exactly this ("evaluates space plans based on adjacency constraints to minimize the total distance of all interconnected programmatic elements", Boon et al. 2015, as reported in Das et al. 2016). Working systems compute the distances through a derived circulation graph: "After the rooms are site located, a circulation network graph is built to understand which rooms are connected or disconnected from each other. They use an A\* algorithm to traverse the connectivity graph and find shortest paths to access spaces from the lobby" and a "cell neighbor matrix" with "cell weights" plus Dijkstra for "Shortest Path to Find Doors / Access Points" (Das, Day, Hauck, Haymaker, Davis, "Space Plan Generator: Rapid Generation & Evaluation of Floor Plan Design Options…", ACADIA 2016). Facility-planning practice states the same objective family (Pérez-Gosende, Mula, Díaz-Madroñero, "Facility layout planning. An extended literature review", *Int. J. Production Research*, 2021 — title-level).
- Source: Das et al. http://papers.cumincad.org/data/works/att/acadia16_106.pdf — T2; Boon, Griffin, Papaefthimious, Ross, Storey, "Optimizing Spatial Adjacencies using Evolutionary Parametric Tools", *Perkins + Will Research Journal* 7(2):25–37, 2015 (via Das et al.'s reference list) — T3; Pérez-Gosende et al. https://doi.org/10.1080/00207543.2021.1897176 — T2.
- Class: STANDARD (objective)
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High [classification: the rule also carries FACT for its part of the claim - the field holds one label per spec §27]
- Grid translation: For each weighted pair, run A\* on `walkable ∪ door` restricted to the role's permitted doors; `d_ij = min cost` in tile-steps (octile: `max(dx,dz) + 0.41421·min(dx,dz)`). Report `objective = Σ w·d`, its normalised form `objective / Σ w` (weighted mean travel in tiles and in metres at 0.5 m/tile), and the detour index vs. straight-line tile distance (TH-14). Pass threshold: any grade-5 pair with `d_ij = ∞` is a hard fail; grade-4 pairs above τ (8–16 tiles) are flagged.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Diagonals silently change costs: a 1-tile corridor forbids corner-cutting, so the same plan can score 1.41× worse than an open hall for the same pair (see derived constants in the header). Compare objective values only between plans with the same corridor widths, or the "improvement" is an artefact of opening a wall. Second: the objective ignores capacity — a cheap route through a 1-tile door is not a good route for 200 people (AG-12).

### AG-10 Compute connectivity, depth, mean depth and integration; use them as rankings only
- Rule: For every plan compute, on `G_acc(role)`: `connectivity(v) = deg(v)`; `D(v) = Σ_u depth(v,u)`; `mean depth ℓ(v) = D(v)/(n−1)`; `integration(v) = 1/ℓ(v)`; and the graph's global mean. Rank rooms and check the ranking against the intended public/private order. Do not use the values as forecasts of footfall, and apply normalisation only when comparing graphs of different sizes.
- Evidence: Definitions are standard space syntax: "Connectivity measures the number of spaces immediately connecting a space of origin" (H&H 1984 p.103); depth "exists wherever it is necessary to go through intervening spaces" (p.108); "Mean depth is calculated by assigning a depth value to each space according to how many spaces it is away from the original space, summing these values and dividing by the number of spaces in the system less one" (p.108); Relative Asymmetry compares "how deep the system is from a particular point with how deep or shallow it theoretically could be" (pp.108–109); RRA "will only be needed when comparing across systems of different sizes" (pp.111–113). Formulae as reproduced in Volchenkov & Blanchard, "Scaling and Universality in City Space Syntax" (arXiv:0709.4375): `deg(i) = Σ_j (A_𝔊)_ij`, `𝔇_i = Σ_j d_ij`, `ℓ_i = 𝔇_i/(N−1)`, `RRA(i) = 2(ℓ_i − 1)/(D_N(N − 2))`, `D_N = 2[N(log₂((N+2)/3) − 1) + 1]/((N−1)(N−2))`; adjacency there is defined as "possible to move freely from one space to another, without passing through any intervening" space. The behavioural claim's boundary is stated by its own authors: "natural movement is the proportion of urban pedestrian movement determined by the grid configuration itself" (Hillier et al. 1993, *Environment and Planning B* 20:29–66, p.32) — i.e. the rest is not.
- Source: Space Syntax glossary terms Connectivity / Depth / Mean depth / Relative asymmetry / RRA / Natural movement / Movement economy / Normalisation formula https://www.spacesyntax.online/glossary/ — T3; arXiv:0709.4375 — T2; Hillier & Hanson, *The Social Logic of Space*, Polity 1984 — T3; Hillier, *Space is the Machine*, CUP 1996 — T3.
- Class: HEURISTIC (ranking device)
- Scope: universal; unreliable below ~12 nodes
- Confidence: High for computation and monotonicity; Low for indoor behavioural prediction (see ## Weak or contested, and TH-20's identical caveat) [classification: the rule also carries FACT for its part of the claim - the field holds one label per spec §27]
- Grid translation: BFS from every node of `G_acc(public)` → distance matrix; emit `deg`, `D`, `ℓ`, `integration_raw = 1/ℓ`; add `RRA` only when comparing two plans whose room counts differ by > 20 %. Validation: `spearman(publicness_rank, integration_rank) ≥ 0.6`; the maximum-integration node is a public room or a declared corridor; every `service`-tagged node is in the bottom third. Report per-role variants — a graph that is integrated for staff and segregated for guests is a correct design, not a failure.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Small graphs flip ranking on one door (report the sensitivity: recompute with each candidate door removed and note rank changes ≥ 2). Integration rewards thoroughfares, so a back-of-house crossing room can top the list — exclude or re-tag it. The *justified* view matters too: from the entry node, compute depth-from-outside (AG-11) separately from global integration; a room can be globally integrated yet 6 thresholds deep from reception (a real hotel complaint pattern).

### AG-11 Count thresholds, not metres, for privacy and contamination: the door-count metric
- Rule: Compute, for every room, the number of doors on the cheapest route from each entry point of its role — `threshold_depth(room, role)` — and enforce a required sequence per zone pair. This is the metric for privacy gradients, infection control, security layering and "how far the public gets in"; metres and tiles measure effort, doors measure permission.
- Evidence: Depth in a justified graph is defined by the number of intervening spaces, and the justified map is a depth tree drawn "a node at the base, and all points of depth 1 from that point … above those at depth 1, and so on" (H&H 1984 p.106, via the Space Syntax glossary); links in the convex-space graph are explicitly *permeable* adjacencies (p.100) — so the count of links crossed = count of controlled boundaries crossed. Infection-control practice assigns exactly this role to the door: multi-bed rooms "should have both en-suite WC/shower and **doors to the main ward area**" and hand-hygiene basins should be "sited only in supervised areas such as the clean utility room, treatment rooms and dirty utility room" (NHS England HBN 00-09, 2012), with clean/dirty separation and defined workflow patterns as the organising requirement. Step depth is implemented as shortest-path length from a root (Turner 2004, *DepthMap4: A Researcher's Handbook*, p.28, via the glossary).
- Source: Space Syntax glossary "Justified map/graph", "Convex adjacency graph", "Step depth" — T3/T2; NHS England HBN 00-09 https://www.england.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/HBN_00-09_infection_control.pdf — T1; Turner 2004 (via glossary) — T2.
- Class: STANDARD (measure)
- Scope: universal; strongest in healthcare, hospitality, secure/civic
- Confidence: High for the measure; High for its use as a control-point count; Medium for the specific per-type targets (project-set) [classification: the rule also carries CODE REQUIREMENT for its part of the claim - the field holds one label per spec §27]
- Grid translation: Build `G_acc(role)` with edge weight `1` per door run (independent of its tile length) plus `0` for open walkable passage; `threshold_depth(r, role) = min doors on path from the role's entry node(s) to r`. Report the histogram per zone and the sequence string (e.g. `outside → lobby(1) → lift hall(2) → guest corridor(2) → room(3)`). Pass: each room's depth within its zone's required band; each `PROHIBIT` pair separated by ≥ 1 door the prohibited role cannot open (i.e. `d = ∞` in that role's graph).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Corridors without doors (open plan) give depth 0 and read as "no privacy" — that is correct and should be flagged only against an explicit requirement. Second: counting door *tiles* instead of door *runs* triples the metric on a wide opening; group connected door tiles first. Third: a lift portal is a threshold too, and it is a queue (AG-16, TH-29) — report door count and portal count separately.

### AG-12 Give every circulation edge a capacity, and test the min-cut
- Rule: A door edge is not just a cost, it is a capacity: `cap(door) = effective width in tiles × specific-flow rate × (1 − obstruction allowance)`. Compute the minimum cut of the movement graph for each peak demand and check that the cut capacity exceeds it; a plan can have plenty of route length and still fail at one 2-tile door.
- Evidence: Egress research on doorways finds the standard engineering treatment questionable precisely because door leaves and occupant interaction change the usable width: "the interaction of the occupants with the door leaves delayed their progress; and (2) the variation in this interaction dynamically influenced the doorway width available, which in turn influenced the overall flow rates produced", with remedies including "a reduced maximum specific flow rate could be assumed; a reduced exit width assumed" (Gwynne et al., "Questioning the linear relationship between doorway width and evacuation speed", *Fire Safety Journal* 44 (2009) 80–87, NIST-hosted copy). Sizing maths itself is code territory (IBC 1005; CODE-04 in this project).
- Source: Gwynne, Galea, Lawrence, Deklerk, Fire Safety Journal 44:80–87, PDF https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=861412 — T2; IBC 1005 Means of Egress Sizing https://up.codes/s/means-of-egress-sizing — T1 (as used in CODE-01…CODE-04).
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT (also argued as: with CODE REQUIREMENT inputs)
- Scope: universal where flow is significant; trivial for low-occupancy rooms
- Confidence: High for the structure (capacity ≠ cost); Medium for the numeric allowance (code-side, see building-codes.md)
- Grid translation: `width_tiles(door run) = run length`; `cap = width_tiles × 0.5 m × specific_flow`, with the width measured to the *reduced* clear width after leaf swing (use CODE-10/CODE-27 rounding: round up to the tile, never model a sub-minimum width as compliant). Run a max-flow/min-cut (Ford–Fulkerson; standard algorithm, Cormen et al., *Introduction to Algorithms*, 3rd ed.) on `G_acc(role)` with door capacities against the demand table (trip pairs × persons). Report `mincut` location(s): those are the plan's true bottlenecks, and they are usually a single door, not a corridor.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Two doors side by side between the same pair are one multi-edge, not one wide door (host geometry: a 4-tile run is a 2 m opening). Second: the metric collapses if the plan has parallel corridors but a single shared pinch point at the stair lobby — min-cut finds it, per-pair cost does not. Third: the doors that most often become the cut are the tagged ones (staff-only), so always compute the cut per role.

### AG-13 Find cut vertices and bridges; make the block–cut tree the repair map
- Rule: Compute articulation points (rooms/corridors/doors whose removal disconnects the graph) and bridges (door runs that are the only link between two parts). Every such element is a single point of failure for movement, for egress and for supervision; require the count of them to fall in the intended band per role graph, and use the block–cut tree to see which sub-plan a repair will actually touch.
- Evidence: "An articulation point in a network is a node whose removal disconnects the network. Those nodes play key roles in ensuring connectivity of many real-world networks, from infrastructure networks to … " and the paper develops tools for "the expected number of them and the network vulnerability against their removal" (Tian, Bashan, Shi, Liu, "Articulation points in complex networks", *Nature Communications* 8:14223, 2017). Definitions and the block/biconnected-component machinery are standard (Diestel, *Graph Theory*, free ed.; West 1996). Two independent routes between a space and an exit is also the code requirement owned by CODE-02/CODE-08, which is a Menger-style statement (k disjoint paths) rather than a distance one.
- Source: Tian et al. https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms14223 (OA: https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms14223) — T2; Diestel https://diestel-graph-theory.com/ — T2; West, *Introduction to Graph Theory*, 2nd ed., Prentice-Hall 1996 (verified via the reference list of Buchsbaum et al., item [51]) — T3; Menger's theorem as stated in either text — T2/T3.
- Class: FACT (also argued as: + ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT)
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: Tarjan DFS in O(V+E) on `G_acc(role)`: cut vertices, bridges, biconnected components, block–cut tree. Metrics to publish: `#cut_vertices`, `#bridges`, `max_component_size_after_removal(v)` for the worst cut vertex, and `leaf_block_depth` (hops from a block to the nearest cycle — the graph-theoretic dead end, cross-ref CODE-06). Pass: for roles with public occupants, 0 cut vertices on the route between the entry node and any required-exit node unless CODE-03's common-path allowance covers it; report every bridge as "single door serving N rooms".
- Exceptions / failure mode: Deliberate cut vertices are legitimate at controlled boundaries (a security checkpoint, a ticket gate, a hotel's only guest stair in a low-rise) — declare them with their tag so the check expects them. Second failure: a plan where the *only* connectivity is a corridor makes every corridor tile-block one bridge; the graph is a tree by design, and the correct comment is "no redundancy", not "broken". Third: fixing connectivity by adding a door in a `PROHIBIT` pair is the classic repair regression — always re-run AG-02 before accepting a repair (AG-18).

### AG-14 Prefer circuits to trees in movement graphs, and publish ringness
- Rule: Compute the circuit rank `μ = e − n + p` (independent movement loops) and ringness `ρ = μ/μ_max` with `μ_max = 2n − 5` for a planar graph, then hold the circulation skeleton to a declared band. A tree skeleton is not "simpler", it is a plan with no route choice, twice the backtracking and every corridor as a potential dead end; a fully circuited skeleton spreads movement and removes single points of failure but costs area and can hide decision points.
- Evidence: Ringness is a space-syntax measure defined as "the number of rings (or circuits of possible movement) in the system as a proportion of the maximum possible rings for that number of spaces" (H&H 1984 p.102, via the Space Syntax glossary), and the same source names its opposite, "stringiness … the extension of space in one dimension" (p.91). Practice documents the trade-off concretely in ward design: the racetrack/double-corridor typology "is based on a redundant circulation instead of a one-way circulation", adopted "in order to fit more patients without increasing the nurses' walking distance", while its cost is that it "limited visibility from the nurse station" (Cai, *Making "Invisible Architecture" Visible: A Comparative Study of Nursing Unit Typologies in the United States and China*, PhD dissertation, Georgia Tech, 2012, §2.1.1.4). The general lesson the same thesis draws: "every innovation in design has some trade-offs. It meets certain needs yet sacrifices others."
- Source: Space Syntax glossary "Ringiness", "Stringiness/ringness" — T3; Cai 2012 https://repository.gatech.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/c6115891-5cb0-475f-b391-3e685214cad2/content — T2.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE (also argued as: with HEURISTIC bands)
- Scope: type-specific band (see audit); method universal
- Confidence: High for the metric; Medium for the target band
- Grid translation: On the circulation subgraph (corridors, halls, lobbies, outside, portals; `e` = door runs, `n` = circulation nodes, `p` = components) compute `μ`, `ρ`. Bands (heuristic, project-set): guest/public skeleton `ρ ≥ 0.25`; service skeleton allowed `ρ = 0`; secure/detention-style control skeletons deliberately `ρ = 0` with monitored chokepoints. Also publish `μ` before/after every repair (a repair that reduces `μ` is a regression unless the removed loop was the defect). Derived check: `ρ > 0` and `#bridges = 0` are independent conditions — a lollipop graph has both a loop and a dead branch.
- Exceptions / failure mode: A loop that only exists for staff (service ring) does not improve guest wayfinding, so publish per-role `ρ`. Second: loops can create "which way do I go?" indecision at every junction (TH-19 owns the decision-point fix); `ρ ≥ 0.25` with 6 identical T-junctions is worse for orientation than `ρ = 0.1` with two leg ones — report `#decision_nodes` alongside `ρ`.

### AG-15 Run the graph per role and require tag-consistent connectivity
- Rule: Access tags are part of the graph model. For every role (guest, staff, service, public, emergency, contractor), derive its subgraph, and assert: (a) every room that role must reach is reachable; (b) every room that role must not reach is unreachable; (c) zones the role may cross are contiguous in that role's graph. A plan validated only for the most permissive role is unvalidated.
- Evidence: The predicate structure of separation is explicit in infection-control guidance — "Plan for and deliver good separation of clean and dirty activities"; "Clean and dirty areas should be kept separate and the workflow patterns of each area should be clearly defined" (NHS England HBN 00-09) — i.e. two labelled flow patterns that must not intersect, which is a per-class graph statement. On the modelling side, space-syntax interior analysis is done per entry/root and per map type, and the multi-storey case study builds separate models "with links on stairs and elevators … with and without fire hazards" (Dominguez et al., "Evacuation routes in a multi-story building", *Proc. 13th Space Syntax Symposium*, T4 conference paper), i.e. the same geometry yields different graphs under different conditions/permissions.
- Source: HBN 00-09 — T1; SSS13 paper PDF https://www.hvl.no/siteassets/hvl-internett/arrangement/2022/13sss/426dominguez.pdf — T4; IBC 1005/up.codes for the emergency-role route — T1.
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT (host engine)
- Scope: universal where tags exist
- Confidence: High [classification: the rule also carries DESIGN PRINCIPLE for its part of the claim - the field holds one label per spec §27]
- Grid translation: `for role in roles: G_role = doors with perms ⊇ role` → components, integration ranks, cut vertices, min-cut, threshold_depth. Emit the role×zone matrix (reachable / blocked / reachable-but-long). Fail on: unreachable required zone; reachable prohibited zone; a prohibited pair connected by a path whose every intermediate is a *shared* space (the "technically walled off but reachable from anywhere" case, TH-04). Pass threshold: zero violations; report the number of doors a role crosses to reach each zone as the operational cost.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Overlapping staff roles make the staff graph the most connected of all, so the staff graph should be excluded from integration-ranking tests (it is a service network, not a public one). Second: emergency graphs invert the tags (doors that are locked from one side become open) — validate the *unlocked* variant separately.

### AG-16 Model the building as one multi-floor graph; vertical edges are queues, not links
- Rule: Build `G_ver` explicitly: each lift/stair portal is an edge between a room node on floor k and the same portal node on floor k+1, with cost = travel time + expected queue wait, and capacity = handling capacity per interval. Every cross-floor `PREFERRED_CLOSE` relationship is then an ordinary shortest-path question. Multi-floor adjacency requirements become constraints on *which floors* rooms occupy and on portal alignment.
- Evidence: The stacking problem is solved computationally in exactly this form in practice: genetic algorithms used "to generate 3D space stacking, respecting input adjacency requirements by the user … evaluates space plans based on adjacency constraints to minimize the total distance of all interconnected programmatic elements", prioritising program spaces "based on practice expertise and user input, which helps discard unrealistic design solutions" (Boon et al. 2015, as reported in Das et al. 2016). Vertical links dominate building-level configuration: the multi-storey case study finds the highest integration values "grouped in and around this nucleus" of the stair core, and treats "floors with a high concentration of vertical passages" as the most connected (and, during an emergency, the most confusing) places (Dominguez et al., SSS13). Repeating a vertical stack saves cost; misaligning it multiplies it ("repeated across multiple storeys" is what makes an inefficient grid expensive; hotel structural-grid guidance, T4).
- Source: Das et al., ACADIA 2016 — T2; Boon et al., Perkins+Will Research Journal 7(2):25–37 — T3; Dominguez et al., SSS13 — T4; Hotel Development Guide, "Hotel Structural Design and Column Grid Planning" https://hoteldevelopmentguide.com/hotel-structural-design/ — T4; CIBSE/Peters Research lift-traffic measures as used in TH-29 — T3.
- Class: STANDARD (also argued as: + ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT)
- Scope: universal for multi-floor types
- Confidence: High for the model; Medium for the T4 stacking/span claims (7.0–8.5 m column grid, "two guestrooms between columns")
- Grid translation: Node set = room nodes across floors + `portal(f, k)` nodes; edges: door runs within a floor, portal edges `(room → portal on its floor)` and `(portal_k ↔ portal_{k+1})` with `cost = lift_seconds/0.5 m-equivalent + queue` (convert to tile-steps by the ratio "1 portal hop ≈ 8–24 tile-steps of equivalent inconvenience", declared per project). Checks: (i) `G_ver` connected for each role that may move between floors; (ii) **stack alignment**: for every pair of consecutive floors, the set of portal tiles on floor k+1 must be inside the portal tiles on floor k (same column), else report `stack_drift(room)` in tiles; (iii) every floor's `portal_count ≥ 2` when CODE-02 requires two exits, and the two portals must not be the same node.
- Exceptions / failure mode: The host has no section, so "stack" is free-looking and under-priced: a 12-floor tower whose programme is spread across floors converts most `PREFERRED_CLOSE` pairs into portal hops — the objective (AG-09) must use portal-inclusive costs or the agent will happily scatter a ward across five floors. Second: a plan needing a service stack (waste/linen) has to declare it as an edge class with its own capacity, or it is invisible in validation.

### AG-17 Detect the infeasible pattern classes before drawing, and name them
- Rule: Run a pre-flight screen on the programme and report a named diagnosis for each failure: (1) clique > 4; (2) non-planar must-touch graph (Kuratowski subgraph reported); (3) edge-density overflow `e_req > 3n − 6`; (4) degree/perimeter overflow per room (AG-05); (5) cyclic order conflict — required adjacencies force a "must be left-of/right-of" cycle; (6) area overflow `Σ tiles > floor capacity × efficiency`; (7) role-connectivity conflict (a pair required for role A, prohibited for role A); (8) separation-vs-adjacency triangle (A–B required, B–C required, A–C prohibited with no legal buffer position).
- Evidence: Each class is a documented obstruction: planarity and the 3n−6 edge bound (Diestel/West; Kuratowski), the rectangle-specific "no separating/facial-triangle violation" and "internally triangulated" requirements for duals (Buchsbaum et al. 2008; Koźmiński & Kinnen 1985), the impossibility of realising all desired adjacencies with fixed sizes and the NP-hardness of doing so optimally (Bekos et al. 2014 — Max-Crown, strongly NP-hard even for trees, per Barth et al.), acyclicity of the ordering relations in mosaic-floorplan formalisms (relative-placement equivalence of Stockmeyer, as surveyed in Buchsbaum et al.), and the practice-side note that relationship diagrams are not layouts precisely because shape/area constraints are ignored ("it does not incorporate other considerations such as building shape and area and department shape constraints", Georgia Tech ISY course page summarising Muther's SLP, T4).
- Source: Buchsbaum et al. — T2; Bekos et al. — T2; Koźmiński & Kinnen — T2; Georgia Tech ISY SLP summary https://www2.isye.gatech.edu/~mgoetsch/cali/Spiral/Spiral%20HTML%20Help/SystematicLayoutPlanningorSLP.htm — T4; Diestel — T2.
- Class: FACT (also argued as: + STANDARD (procedure))
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High for classes 1–4, 6, 7; Medium for class 5 (derived here, not quoted verbatim); High for class 8 as a *pattern*, Low as an *enumerated* list
- Grid translation: Pre-flight in pure graph terms (no tiles placed): `max_clique(M_5)`; planarity test + Kuratowski extraction; `|E_5| ≤ 3n − 6` (and `≤ 2n − 4` when the required graph is triangle-free/bipartite — the planar bipartite bound); `deg_req ≤ exposed_wall_capacity` per node from the area table; build the "left-of/above" relation from the required adjacencies around each candidate hub and run DFS cycle detection; `Σ ceil(area×4) ≤ usable tiles` including wall tax; per-role reachability contradictions; then report `feasible / feasible-with-relaxation (list) / infeasible (named class)`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: The screen is *sufficient for detection, not for solvability*: passing all eight checks does not prove a plan exists (area, proportion, daylight and structure are not in it) — always label it "necessary-condition screen". Second: programmes with soft grade-5s (a planner wrote 5 where they meant 4) fail the screen for the wrong reason; the fix is AG-18's minimal-relaxation report back to the client, not silent downgrade.

### AG-18 Repair by minimal conflict sets, cheapest lever first, and re-verify everything
- Rule: When validation fails, compute a minimal set of constraints that cannot all hold (a conflict set), then apply TH-05's levers as typed repair operators in cost order — buffer room, circulation-as-separator, add plan distance, move a floor — and re-run the whole validation suite after each application. Record the constraint dropped or moved; never "nudge tiles" without a named relaxation.
- Evidence: Over-constrained configuration problems have a standard answer: preferred explanations and relaxations via QuickXplain (Junker, "QUICKXPLAIN: Preferred Explanations and Relaxations for Over-Constrained Problems", ECAI 2004; see also O'Sullivan, Papadopoulos & Faltings, "Representative Explanations for Over-Constrained Problems", 2007, and Rodler, "A formal proof and simple explanation of the QuickXplain algorithm", *Artificial Intelligence Review*, 2022 — a logarithmic-complexity algorithm that returns a minimal conflict subset given a monotonic inconsistency test), plus domain-specific filtering for over-constrained problems (Petit & Régin, "Specific Filtering Algorithms for Over-Constrained Problems", CP 2001, LNCS). Practice tools implement the same loop: a generator that "will only proceed to subsequent departments after constraints and requirements are satisfied or until reaching an allowed quantity of trials" (Das et al. 2016), and heuristic repair as a general CSP strategy (Minton et al., "Minimizing conflicts: a heuristic repair procedure for constraint satisfaction and scheduling tasks", *Artificial Intelligence* 57, 1992 — title-level from author knowledge, not retrieved this pass, so treated as background).
- Source: O'Sullivan, Papadopoulos, Faltings http://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/115284 — T2; Vidal-Silva, Felfernig, Galindo et al., "Explanations for over-constrained problems using QuickXPlain with speculative executions", *J. Intelligent Information Systems* (2021) https://doi.org/10.1007/s10844-021-00675-4 — T2; Rodler, *AI Review* (2022) — T2; Petit & Régin https://doi.org/10.1007/3-540-45578-7_31 — T2; Das et al. — T2.
- Class: STANDARD (procedure)
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High for the minimal-conflict approach; Medium for the Minton citation (not retrieved)
- Grid translation: Algorithm: (1) collect violated predicates `V`; (2) if `|V| > 1`, run QuickXplain-style splitting over `V ∪ programme` with `inconsistent(S)` = AG-17 screen + tile-level placement feasibility, to get a minimal `C ⊆ V`; (3) for `C`, try levers in cost order, cost measured in tiles: buffer room = `+ (3×3 outer) = 7 wall tiles + interior`, circulation separator = `+ corridor tiles`, distance = `+ Δd × 0.5 m of route`, floor move = `+ 1 portal hop × expected trips`; (4) apply the cheapest, re-run AG-01…AG-17; (5) if the same pair re-appears twice, stop and escalate with the conflict set rather than repairing again. Cap iterations (e.g. 8 per plan) and publish the repair log.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Endless repair loops are the signature symptom of an unsatisfiable programme being treated as a drawing problem (AG-17 exists to catch it earlier). Second: repairs that *introduce* new cut vertices or bridges — always recompute `#cut_vertices`, `#bridges`, `ρ` after each lever, since inserting a buffer room typically creates two bridges. Third: a repair that removes a required door to fix a `PROHIBIT` violation converts a soft failure into a hard one (AG-01 dependency check catches it).

### AG-19 Check that the derived graph is a faithful dual of the plan
- Rule: Verify consistency between the geometry and the graph in both directions every time: every graph edge must be witnessed by real tiles (a wall tile with rooms on opposite sides, or a door run touching both rooms), and every tile-level feature that implies a relationship must appear in the graph. Emit the witness tile list per edge — that list is the proof, and its absence is a bug.
- Evidence: Space syntax's own validity notion is graph-level identity: "Graph isomorphism means that graphs share not only the same number of elements and the same total depth, but also the same number of elements at each level of the graphs and the same connections between elements" (Hillier 1996 p.88, via the Space Syntax glossary) — the tool for comparing "same arrangement" claims. Region-adjacency definitions in the floor-plan literature carry exactly the pathological cases to exclude: Ungar's conditions for a well-formed plane map are quoted as "1. Precisely one region is infinite; 2. At most three regions meet in any point; 3. Every region is simply connected; 4. The union of any two adjacent regions is simply connected; 5. The intersection of any two regions is either a simple arc or is empty" (Buchsbaum et al. 2008, §2, citing Ungar).
- Source: Buchsbaum et al. §2 (conditions 1–5, citing Ungar) — T2; Space Syntax glossary "Graph isomorphism", "Planar graph" — T3.
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT (validator)
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High [classification: the rule also carries FACT for its part of the claim - the field holds one label per spec §27]
- Grid translation: Per derived edge, store witness: `wall_tiles(A,B)` and `door_runs(A,B)`. Assert: (a) `door_runs ≠ ∅ ⇒ wall_tiles ≠ ∅` and both collinear (no door whose two flanking rooms fail the collinear test); (b) each room's tile set is 4-connected (Ungar 3); (c) no two rooms share two disjoint frontiers unless you intend a multi-edge (Ungar 5 — report "adjacent in k separate places"); (d) at every blocked junction tile, count distinct rooms touching it — `≥ 4` = windmill/cross junction: flag "adjacency may be a point, not a segment" (Ungar 2); (e) outer-face nodes: `outside` touches exactly the façade-adjacent rooms (Ungar 1). Pass: all five hold; else emit the offending tiles with coordinates.
- Exceptions / failure mode: A generator that edits tiles directly without re-deriving produces stale graphs — always recompute from tiles (never cache across edits, which is the whole point of "derived at validation time"). Second: a 4-room junction (windmill) makes two of the four pairs *look* adjacent in the drawing while the collinear rule says they are not, so the matrix shows a false negative — the honest fix is to break the junction with one wall tile, not to relax the rule.

### AG-20 Detect twin rooms before trusting any graph metric
- Rule: Compute true/false twins (node pairs with identical neighbourhoods) and, when their share is high, say so: the graph model can no longer discriminate those rooms, and the analysis must drop to the metric/geometric layer (area, aspect, daylight, frontage, door position). Never conclude "all guest rooms are equivalent" from the graph; conclude "the graph cannot see the difference".
- Evidence: The twin concept is used in spatial-graph analysis itself: nodes whose "rows and columns of the adjacency matrix are identical" are called twins, and the dual-graph transformation "converts the repeating geometrical elements expressing the order in the urban developments into the twins nodes, the pairs of nodes such that any other is adjacent either to them both or to neither of them" (Volchenkov & Blanchard, arXiv:0709.4375). Space syntax's own decomposition step (convex-space breakup, choosing the "fattest" convex by area/perimeter ratio) is the acknowledgment that repeated identical spaces are a modelling artefact of the map, not a property of the building (H&H 1984 p.17, p.100).
- Source: arXiv:0709.4375 — T2; Space Syntax glossary "Convex space breakup", "Fattest convex space", "Area-perimeter ratio" — T3.
- Class: HEURISTIC (diagnostic)
- Scope: universal; decisive for hotel, residential, ward, cell and campus-repeat types
- Confidence: High for the detection; High for the interpretation limit [classification: the rule also carries FACT for its part of the claim - the field holds one label per spec §27]
- Grid translation: `twin(u,v)` iff `N(u) = N(v)` (true twins also adjacent). Report `twin_share = |twin classes with size ≥ 2| / n`. If `twin_share > 0.3`: switch to per-room geometry metrics — `frontage tiles to façade`, `aspect ratio`, `distance from door run to room centre`, `share of perimeter shared with each neighbour` — and rank rooms by those instead of by integration. Also report the *asymmetry within a twin class* as the design signal (the two end rooms of a spine are twins topologically but differ in frontage, daylight and dead-end proximity).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Real duplication is a feature (a kit of parts, TH-23) — do not "fix" twins by adding arbitrary doors. Second: the corridor node can make an entire floor one twin class; splitting the corridor into segments (a segment/axial-style graph rather than a convex-style graph) restores discrimination, which is precisely the axial-vs-convex choice in space syntax — declare which decomposition you used, because the metrics change with it (see ## Weak or contested).

### AG-21 Publish the graph, the matrix and the diff as the validation artefact
- Rule: The output of every design cycle is a machine-readable bundle: programme matrix `M`, derived `G_adj`/`G_acc(role)`/`G_ver` with witnesses, measured matrix `M̂`, per-pair verdicts, and the metric block (AG-09…AG-20). Never report an adjacency assessment as prose about "good flow" — a claim without a witness tile list and a computed value is not a result.
- Evidence: Generative/analytical systems report exactly this style of residual: "We measure the quality of generated house layouts with the three metrics: the realism, the diversity, and the compatibility with the input graph constraint" (Nauata et al. 2020); the clinical generator scores plans on "the percentage of program elements placed in comparison to program document requirements, … nurse travel distance to all patient rooms, and percentage number of Key Planning Units" with "metrics … user-weighted" (Das et al. 2016); hospital planning review practice separates the relationship diagram from the layout and then evaluates the gap (Lobos & Donath 2010; Muther's phase sequence, T4).
- Source: Nauata et al. — T2; Das et al. — T2; Lobos & Donath — T2.
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT (deliverable format)
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: Emit JSON: `{programme:[{a,b,grade,type,tau}], nodes:[{id,tag,floor,tiles,area_m2,fixtures}], edges:[{type:witness_tiles,door_runs,cost,roles}], measured:[{a,b,adjacent,doors,cost,verdict}], metrics:{integration:{},ringness:"μ/μmax",cut_vertices:[],bridges:[],mincut:{cap,location},threshold_depth:{role:{room:n}},twin_share:float,stack_drift:{floor_pair:tiles}}`. Validation gate: every programme row must appear in `measured`; every `measured` pair must have a witness; every metric must be recomputed from `nodes/edges`, never carried over.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Stale artefacts (metrics from the pre-repair graph) are the most likely failure and are prevented by the recompute rule. Second: an unreadable bundle nobody checks — keep a 5-line human summary (hard fails, relaxations used, top 3 bottlenecks, ringness, circulation share) on top of the JSON.

### AG-22 Rank relationship importance by measured consequence, not by the client's adjective
- Rule: Convert every relationship into a demand figure before assigning grades: `expected_trips/day × persons/trip × minutes/person` between the two rooms. Use the product to set the grade (and therefore the lever budget when a conflict is repaired), and report the table. Where demand is unknowable, mark the grade as declared, not measured.
- Evidence: The relationship chart is explicitly a record of interaction intensity, not of affection: it contains "the level of interaction between pairs of departments", and positive/negative grades translate into proximity/farther-apart placement (Georgia Tech ISY SLP summary, T4; Muther's flow and relationship analyses are separate phases feeding the same chart). SLP's numeric weights (16/8/4/1/0/−80) are a deliberate non-linear encoding of that intensity (Resource Systems Consulting, T4). Demand-weighted objectives are the norm in the computational literature too — minimising total distance "of all interconnected programmatic elements" with prioritised spaces (Boon et al. 2015 via Das et al.).
- Source: Georgia Tech ISY SLP page — T4; Resource Systems Consulting — T4; Boon et al. — T3.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE (also argued as: with HEURISTIC conversion)
- Scope: universal; weakest for symbolic/civic relationships (a monument's importance is not trip volume)
- Confidence: Medium — the direction is standard practice, the arithmetic is a project convention
- Grid translation: `weight_ij = trips_ij × persons_ij` (from the programme's OD table); grade = quantile band of `weight` unless overridden for safety/privacy/hygiene (which are grade-forced, e.g. all `PROHIBIT` pairs are grade −1 regardless of demand). Publish both columns — "declared grade" and "demand-implied grade" — and flag disagreement ≥ 2 bands as a question for the brief (not as an error to silently fix).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Two known systematic mis-grades: (a) low-frequency, catastrophic-consequence links (emergency route, infection pathway) that demand arithmetic undervalues — these get forced grades; (b) high-frequency links that are *process* links rather than spatial ones (staff between two utility rooms many times a day) which are better fixed by moving a fixture than by moving a room.

### AG-23 Prefer a legible circulation skeleton to a maximally connected one
- Rule: Choose one skeleton type for each role (spine/leaf, loop, grid, hub/spoke, tree) and hold the graph to it: the type is a design decision recorded before placement, and metric improvements that violate it are regressions, not gains. Connectivity that the user cannot read is worth less than the connectivity it buys.
- Evidence: The typology is documented as a sequence of design solutions with explicit trade-offs in ward planning (single corridor → racetrack/double corridor → courtyard → cruciform/cluster), each gaining something and losing something else — racetrack gains shorter nurse walking via redundant circulation and loses visibility from the station; courtyard gains light/privacy and loses walking efficiency because of "its increased depth of floor plate" (Cai 2012). Configuration-level legibility is also the space-syntax claim: intelligibility is the correlation between the topological (connectivity) and geometric (axial) pictures, i.e. the same arrangement judged two ways (Space Syntax glossary "Axial intelligibility"; Hillier 1996 ch. on the movement economy, pp.125–127).
- Source: Cai 2012 — T2; Space Syntax glossary "Axial intelligibility", "Movement economy" — T3; Ching, *Architecture: Form, Space, and Order*, 4th ed. (organisation forms, per TH-10) — T3.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal; the *choice* is type-specific (see audit)
- Confidence: Medium (principle is well supported; "legible" is not directly measurable here)
- Grid translation: Declare `skeleton_type(role)`. Verify: for spine/leaf — exactly one non-leaf component and `max leaf chain depth ≤ 2`; for loop — `μ ≥ 1` and `#bridges = 0`; for grid — `ρ ≥ 0.4` and `deg ≤ 4` at junction nodes; for hub/spoke — one node with `deg ≥ 0.6·n_circ`. Proxy legibility metric: mean number of decision points per required route (`nodes with deg ≥ 3` on the A\* path) and the fraction of routes needing a turn at the last junction. Pass: declared type holds and `decision_points per required route ≤ 3` for guest/public roles.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Mixed skeletons are normal at the boundary between territories (guest spine meeting a service loop) — declare the junction node explicitly so the check doesn't fail the whole plan. Second: metric-chasing (e.g. raising `ρ` to improve integration) can convert a legible spine into an unreadable grid; the guard is the declared type + `decision_points` metric, reported together.

### AG-24 Validate the adjacency programme against separation consequences in one pass, per pair, with buffers
- Rule: For every prohibited/separated pair, require a *witnessed buffer*: the tiles between them must belong to a room whose relationship to both is grade ≤ 1, or to circulation, or to open site. A wall alone is not a buffer. Emit the buffer chain per pair.
- Evidence: Conflict resolution by inserting a buffer/separator space is the standard space-programming move (matrix → bubble → block planning; Gustin T4, WBDG programming discipline T1/T3, and Ching's "spaces linked by a common space" as the formal case, per TH-05); the healthcare requirement is functional rather than visual: separate clean and dirty areas with defined workflow patterns and hand-hygiene at supervised control points (HBN 00-09, T1). The room-graph formalism supplies the exact condition to test (Ungar's well-formedness conditions 3–5 on regions and their intersections, via Buchsbaum et al.).
- Source: HBN 00-09 — T1; Buchsbaum et al. §2 — T2; Gustin Design Services / WBDG — T4/T1 (as cited in TH-03/TH-05); Ching 4th ed. — T3.
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: universal; hard requirement in healthcare, food, secure, industrial-clean
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: For each `SEPARATE(i,j,δ)` / `PROHIBIT(i,j)`: compute the tile path set between them on the shortest A\* route; classify each intermediate tile's owner room; `buffer_ok` iff ∃ owner with `M[i][owner] ≤ 1 ∧ M[j][owner] ≤ 1`, or the intermediate is circulation/outside. Report the buffer as `A — [buffer room tag, n tiles] — B` plus `measured δ in tiles` and `measured door count`. Fail if the only separation is wall thickness ≥ 2 tiles, or if the buffer is shared with a third relationship that needs direct contact with one side (conflict → AG-18).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Vertical "separation by floor" has no buffer tiles — for stacked pairs, the buffer is the inter-floor structure and the separating element is the portal: require `no direct portal link` and ≥ 1 floor gap, and check `G_ver` distance instead. Second: a buffer that is itself a destination (a lounge between lobby and guest rooms) satisfies the letter of the rule and violates the intent — check the buffer's own grade to both sides, and also its threshold depth (AG-11).

## Graph specification

The canonical model the rules above compute against. Everything here is derived from tiles at
validation time; nothing is authored by hand.

**Node classes** (each node carries `id, tag, floor, tile_set, area_tiles, fixtures[], zone, publicness`):
`R` room (flood-fill component of walkable tiles, typed by fixture tags); `C` corridor/hall
(walkable component whose fixture set is empty and whose `area/perimeter` ratio is low — declared
as circulation, not inferred silently); `O` outside (the unbounded region — one node per plan,
adjacent to façade rooms); `F` floor node (a floor as a first-class node, so that vertical edges
have somewhere to attach and "which floor" is a graph property); `P(f)` portal node per lift/stair
stack, one per floor level it serves.

**Edge classes** (each edge carries `witnesses[]`, so every claim is tile-provable):
- `ADJ(i,j)` — wall-sharing: one blocked tile with a 4-neighbour in i and the *opposite* 4-neighbour in j. Undirected, unit weight, no capacity. Witness: the wall tiles.
- `ACC(i,j,role,cost,cap,doors)` — permeable: a door run with tiles in both rooms' frontiers. Directed per role; `cost` in octile tile-steps; `cap` in persons/s (AG-12); multi-edge allowed (two door runs = two edges).
- `SEP(i,j,δ,roles)` — negative constraint, no geometry of its own; verified by distance + absence of `ACC`.
- `FORB(i,j,roles)` — hard negative: no `ADJ`, no `ACC`.
- `VER(i,k)` — portal edges `room ↔ P(f) ↔ floor node`, cost includes queue (AG-16).

**Weight scale**: integer grades `5 4 3 2 1 0 −1` (AG-06) for requirements; `1` for derived edges;
`∞` for unreachable. Demand weights (AG-22) are separate floats and never replace grades.

**Derived quantities** (all computable in O(V+E) or O(VE) on these graphs, V = rooms + corridors):

| Quantity | Formula on the derived graph | Used by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| degree / connectivity | `deg(v) = Σ_j A_ij` | AG-05, AG-10 |
| depth | `d(u,v) = BFS hops` (topological) or `A* cost` (metric) | AG-09, AG-11 |
| total / mean depth | `D(v)=Σ_u d(v,u)`, `ℓ(v)=D(v)/(n−1)` | AG-10 |
| integration | `1/ℓ(v)`; `RRA(v)=2(ℓ_v−1)/(D_N(N−2))` only for cross-size comparison | AG-10 |
| betweenness / choice | `Σ_{s≠t} σ_st(v)/σ_st`, Brandes O(VE) | AG-12, AG-14 |
| threshold depth | min door-count path from role entry nodes | AG-11 |
| tree vs cyclic | `μ = e − n + p`; ringness `ρ = μ/(2n − 5)` (planar max, `n ≥ 3`) | AG-14 |
| cut vertices / bridges | Tarjan DFS; block–cut tree | AG-13 |
| bottleneck | min-cut of capacities in `ACC` | AG-12 |
| planarity | Hopcroft–Tarjan/Boyer–Myrvold; Kuratowski subgraph on failure; bounds `e ≤ 3n−6`, `e ≤ 2n−4` if triangle-free | AG-03, AG-04, AG-17 |
| shape realizability | rectangles: planar + every triangle facial; gap-free rectangle: internally 4-connected triangulation (Kozmiński–Kinnen / He) | AG-04 |
| twin classes | `N(u)=N(v)` (false twins) plus adjacency (true twins) | AG-20 |
| dual consistency | edge↔witness bijection; per-junction room count ≤ 3 (Ungar 2); region connectivity (Ungar 3); simple-intersection (Ungar 5); single infinite region (Ungar 1) | AG-19 |
| zone cut | Kernighan–Lin / modularity cut weight on `M ≥ 3`; per-zone contiguity in `ADJ ∪ ACC` | AG-07 |

Isomorphism is used one way only: to decide whether two candidate plans are *the same arrangement*
(same node count, same per-level counts, same connections — Hillier 1996 p.88), so that "we have
already tried this" can be stated graphically rather than visually. Nothing in this model requires
a graph to be authored; if a graph is authored and never re-derived from tiles, all checks are void.

## Adjacency-matrix → solver procedure

Executable ordering for the agent. Steps 1–4 run **before** any tile is placed; 5–10 are the design
loop; 11 is the report. Cost unit = tiles (0.5 m).

1. **Load programme** → nodes with `area_tiles = ceil(m² × 4)` (TH-01/TH-02), zone candidate, tag, demand table (trips/day).
2. **Build matrix `M`** (AG-06), typed into the four predicates (AG-02), grades sanity-checked against demand (AG-22, disagreement ≥ 2 bands → question list, keep designing with declared grades).
3. **Derive zoning** (AG-07): cluster `M ≥ 3`; assign zone labels; verify each cluster is internally plausible (one entry per zone, one public boundary).
4. **Pre-flight screen** (AG-17, 8 named classes). Outcomes: `feasible` → step 5; `feasible-with-relaxation` → propose relaxations with costs (AG-18) and continue; `infeasible (class N)` → STOP and report the obstruction (a K3,3 or 5-clique is a conversation, not a drawing problem).
5. **Pick skeleton + parti** (AG-23, TH-10/TH-16): declare `skeleton_type` per role, portal count/positions, zone bands (TH-06).
6. **Place zones** as bands of tiles; then rooms inside each zone from the kit of parts (TH-23), respecting degree/frontier capacity (AG-05) — this is the bubble→grid step 1–3 (AG-08).
7. **Cut circulation** into interstices, then place door runs on `ADJ` frontiers nearest the A\* path between the pair (AG-08 step 4). Do not place a door that creates an `ADJ` edge (AG-01 dependency check).
8. **Derive graphs + witnesses** from tiles (AG-01, AG-19). Recompute — never reuse the pre-placement graph.
9. **Measure** `M̂` and the metric block (AG-09…AG-16): objective, integration/depth ranks, threshold depth per role, min-cut, cut vertices/bridges, ringness, stack drift, twin share.
10. **Test and repair**: hard fails (grade-5 unsatisfied, `PROHIBIT` violated, role-unreachable, `μ` = 0 where a loop was declared, egress minima from `building-codes.md`) → conflict set → levers in cost order → goto 8. Soft fails → log as veto-able decisions. Iteration cap 8; on cap, stop and report the conflict set (AG-18).
11. **Report**: 5-line human summary + JSON bundle (AG-21): changed nodes/edges, levers used with tile costs, grades dropped, residual unsatisfied weight, bottlenecks with witness tiles, per-role connectivity matrix, open questions for the brief.

Stop conditions: all hard fails zero; declared skeleton type holds; ringness/cut-vertex bands met;
`Σ wall_share` and circulation share within budget (TH-15); every programme row measured.

## Weak or contested

- **The letter names of the closeness scale.** Architecture-side practice and facility-planning practice use the same A/E/I/O/U/X letters with different wordings ("especially important" vs "especially desirable", "okay" vs "ordinary"), and the primary Muther text could not be machine-read this pass (41 MB PDF). Use the letters as a convention; the numbers (16/8/4/1/0/−80) are one consultant's evaluation scheme, not a standard. What is solidly supported is the *structure*: positive grades plus a separate forbidden grade.
- **Non-planarity as proof of infeasibility is only as good as the adjacency definition.** The planarity guarantee holds for wall-sharing adjacency defined through a collinear wall tile between 4-connected regions; it is broken by degenerate structures (isolated blocked pillar, 8-connected diagonal "wall", a 4-room windmill junction). Hence AG-03's instruction to look for degeneracy before declaring the programme impossible, and AG-19's Ungar-condition checks. My claim that any planar must-touch graph is embeddable as *rectilinear* grid regions is an engineering construction (thickened non-crossing embedding), not a quoted theorem; it needs enough grid resolution, and it is only about adjacencies — areas, proportions and daylight are separate constraints.
- **The rectangular-dual literature is not a settled toolbox.** The characterisation history is error-prone in public: Koźmiński & Kinnen (1985) stated necessary and sufficient conditions, and a 2021 paper publishes a counter-example for separable connected plane graphs and re-derives the condition (arXiv:2102.05304). Practical consequence: never build a validator that depends on "the" characterisation; use the sufficient conditions (triangulated + internally 4-connected) for rejection/warning, and prefer *layouts* (no gap-free requirement) over *duals*, because duals exclude structures that buildings constantly use ("paths are the only trees that have such duals").
- **Whether four mutually adjacent rooms can be four rectangles.** The two sentences I can quote say (a) rectangle contact graphs are planar and every triangle must be facial, "these two conditions are also sufficient" (Bekos et al. quoting Buchsbaum et al.), and (b) for the gap-free case the conditions are 4-connected-triangulation-shaped (Koźmiński & Kinnen). K4 satisfies both readings, yet I could not construct a four-rectangle contact realisation of K4, and the intuitive "windmill" attempt only works if two pairs touch at a *point* (which Ungar's condition 2 and the host's collinear-wall rule both forbid). Practical stance: treat a required K4 as *probably* needing one non-rectangular room, verify it locally on the grid before accepting it, and do not build a rule that hinges on the theorem's exact form. If a future pass resolves this, tighten AG-04 rather than leaving the hedge.
- **Space-syntax prediction inside a building.** The measures are computable and monotone by construction; the behavioural claim is the contested part. The strong form is bounded by its own definition — natural movement is only "the proportion of urban pedestrian movement determined by the grid configuration itself" (Hillier et al. 1993 p.32) — and the evidence base is dominated by street grids, not interior plans, with results sensitive to how the map is decomposed (axial vs convex vs segment) and to graph size (which is why RRA normalisation exists at all). Use integration/choice to rank and to self-critique, never to forecast footfall. Interior application also inherits the hand-drawing problem: on our grid the decomposition is forced (flood-fill rooms), which removes the subjectivity but also removes the axial "lines of sight" that much of the empirical literature measures.
- **Betweenness/choice as a movement predictor for room graphs.** Small-n graphs give spiky, unstable betweenness (one extra door changes the shortest-path census a lot), and the classic critique of centrality measures (Freeman 1979's conceptual clarification) is that the measures encode different notions of importance; do not aggregate them into one number. Report them separately and prefer rank comparisons.
- **Door capacity numbers.** AG-12 is deliberately structural: the *fact* that doorway flow does not scale linearly with width and that leaf interaction reduces effective width is supported (Gwynne et al. 2009), but the specific persons/m·s values used to compute a capacity belong to the code file (CODE-04) — this file takes them as inputs, and no evacuation-time claim is made here. **Attribution caveat (re-checked on the second evidence pass):** the only URL carried for this in AG-12 is the NIST publication-server PDF `pub_id=861412`, whose title as served by NIST is *Questioning the linear relationship between doorway width and pedestrian evacuation flow rate* — the same URL is labelled that way in `human-behavior.md` (HB-24) while AG-12 names it as Gwynne, Galea, Lawrence & Deklerk, *Fire Safety Journal* 44:80–87. Both retrievals of that PDF returned undecodable binary, so **neither the authorship nor any coefficient from it is verified**; resolve the citation before the PDF is treated as the source of the non-linearity claim.
- **Ward/hotel/office/school typologies as evidence.** The nursing-unit typology chain is well documented in a dissertation citing James & Tatton-Brown (T2/T3) and is the strongest type-level evidence retrieved; the hotel grid-span figures are a vendor/consultant page (T4); the school "classroom street" pattern in the audit below is an architectural commonplace with no source retrieved this pass — label it `UNCITED — heuristic`, Confidence Low.
- **Gaps I could not close.** (i) Minsky's layout-planning account (named in the brief) could not be retrieved; the verified lineage for rule-driven space planning is Eastman 1970/1973 and Nassar 2010, not Minsky — re-searched on the second evidence pass (Minsky level plans + Eastman + housing generative design), and no source that actually states the level-plan procedure came back, so Minsky stays uncited here. The same pass verified one modern successor as readable primary text (Hu et al., GSDiff, arXiv:2408.16258v1 HTML, AG-08) and rejected another: *Automated architectural space layout planning using a physics-inspired generative design framework* — https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14840 — yielded only its abstract page (no matrix weights, objective terms or lineage citations readable), so it is deliberately **not** cited for any claim. (ii) Flemming's MULAN and the claim that layout distance should be measured as corridor network distance could not be verified from any retrievable source; the equivalent position is supported indirectly by Das et al.'s A\*/Dijkstra-over-circulation-graph procedure (AG-09). (iii) No ASP/SAT floor-plan paper could be verified this pass — the closest verified constraint-based records are the CSP-adjacent over-constrained-repair literature (AG-18) and Lobos & Donath's survey of mixed-integer/constraint approaches (which reviews Li et al. 2000 and Medjdoub & Yannou 2001 as "topological solutions and graphs"). (iv) Kansky's α/β/γ network indices could not be verified, so ringness is defined via the space-syntax ringness definition plus my own Euler arithmetic (`μ_max = 2n − 5`) instead. ## Type-specificity audit

Graph shape is the most type-dependent thing in this file: the method (derive, measure, repair) is
universal; the skeleton, the grades and the bands are not.

| Type | Dominant graph pattern | Rings / cut vertices | Which rules bind hardest | What must be relaxed |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hotel floor (this project's primary type) | spine + repeated leaves off a core node; service branch on a separate spine | `ρ ≈ 0` per floor by design; the core is a legitimate cut vertex; dead ends capped by CODE-06 | AG-05 (corridor frontier), AG-11 (threshold depth: outside→lobby→lift→corridor→room), AG-13 (single stair = bridge), AG-16 (stack alignment of wet walls and portals), AG-20 (twin share near 1 — graph cannot rank guest rooms; use frontage/daylight) | AG-10/AG-14 (integration ranking is meaningless across identical rooms; do not chase `ρ`) |
