# Research file — Domain AG: Hospitality operating standards and area/yield indices

Scope: this file supplies the **numeric operating layer** that a hotel programme is built from — keys-to-staff ratios,
minutes per cleaned room, covers per hour, seats per key, linen kilograms per occupied room, conference space per key,
outlet area allowances and the revenue/cost indices used in feasibility work. `operations-maintenance.md` (OM-01…OM-28)
already owns *where* servicing things go — trolley bays (OM-01), cleaning catchments (OM-02), waste stores (OM-06/07),
loading (OM-10/12), service lifts (OM-15), staff welfare arithmetic (OM-22), the operational tax as four ratios (OM-24),
the BOH function register. Those placement rules are **downstream** of the numbers here, and are cross-referenced by ID
and never restated. Programme *method* (space lists, net→gross ladders, ratio discipline) belongs to
`space-programming.md` (SP-01…SP-24); adjacency and flow separation to `adjacency-graphs.md` (AG-01…AG-24); area/yield
economics to `economics.md` (EC-01…EC-22, and specifically the per-key cost ladder in EC-19); clearance geometry to
`human-scale.md` (HS-07…HS-19); the desk/queue transaction rule to `professional-practice.md` PP-08. This file adds
numbers, not new placement logic.

**Reading conventions used below.** Evidence tiers follow the corpus definition (`architecture-theory.md` Sources header): T1 codes/standards/government · T2 peer-reviewed and university · T3 professional publications and surveys · T4 professional community and vendor · T5 anecdote. Every figure carries its **source, year, segment and geography**; a `decay date` ("as of <year>") is printed wherever the figure is market-sensitive. Where a band was formed by merging incompatible sources it is labelled `HEURISTIC` with both constituents shown. `POR` = occupied rooms per day = `keys × occupancy`. `HPOR` = paid labour hours per occupied room. `FTE` = full-time equivalent (≈2,080 paid hours/yr). Host grid: 1 tile = 0.5 m, 1 m² = 4 tiles; no levels, no time, no cost model — every minute below is a *driver* for a tile count, never a drawable object.

## Rules

### HO-01 Segment, not the site, fixes how deep the programme must be

- Rule: The market segment is the first input because it determines *which rows exist* in the programme, not just their size. A limited-service property is a key stack plus a breakfast room; an upscale full-service property is a key stack plus a kitchen, a ballroom, a laundry decision, an employee dining room and a second circulation system. Size follows segment, and segment is fixed before the plate is tested (SP-21).
- Evidence: Two independent ladders agree on the direction. The cost ladder in `economics.md` EC-19 (HVS U.S. Hotel Development Cost Survey 2025, 2024 costs, US) runs ~$223k per key select-service → ~$409k full-service → >$1,057k luxury, with an all-property median of ~$219k; the labour ladder in HO-12 (HotelData/Actabl Q3 2025, US, 2025) runs paid hours per occupied room by segment — extended-stay 1.30, limited-service 1.44, traditional full-service 2.57. Department structure per segment is the aggregate of the T4 practice sources gathered across HO-09…HO-23 (US/UK practice statements, undated bands). Together they give the programme shape: a limited-service property is a key stack plus a breakfast room; an upscale full-service property is a key stack plus a kitchen, a ballroom, a laundry decision, an employee dining room and a second circulation system. The instantiated row set is this file's `## Segment × space matrix`, whose rows are Keys per floor segment · Key area basis · Clean minutes/key · Rooms per attendant (HO-10) · HPOR (HO-12, US 2025) · F&B rows · Seats per key (HO-17) · Meetings · Wellness · Laundry (HO-15/16) · BOH depth (HO-25) · Parking ratio (HO-23) · What the segment is paying for, across Economy/limited-service, Select-service, Upscale full-service, Luxury, Resort, Extended-stay and All-suite.
- Source: Segment ladder reconstructed from the cost ladder in EC-19 (HVS 2025 survey, 2024 costs, US; ladder and its URLs held there, not restated per row here) and the labour ladder in HO-12 — HotelData.COM / Actabl Q3 2025 via *Hotel Operations* https://hoteloperations.com/2025-hotel-labor-productivity-report/ — T3; department structure per segment from the T4 practice sources gathered across HO-09…HO-23. T3 (cost and labour surveys) + T4 (programme composition) — see *Weak or contested* item 3 for the segment-name instability.
- Class: BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION
- Scope: All hotel work, all geographies; the row set, not the ordering, changes by country.
- Confidence: Medium for the direction (cost/key and HPOR rise monotonically with segment across both independent surveys); Low for the exact row set, which is brand-manual territory and was not retrievable.
- Grid translation: Select the segment first, then instantiate the matching row set from `## Segment × space matrix` as programme rows with drivers attached (SP-01, SP-09) — every row then becomes a tile count through the `## Standard → spatial requirement translation table`, never a free area. Emit `segment`, `program_rows`, `rows_omitted_and_why`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Chain brand standards (the real source of these bands) are contractual and unpublished; nothing here is a brand standard. Segment labels differ between STR/CoStar, HVS and Europe's AA/AAAA star schemes — never mix them in one comparison. A deeper programme buys rate and group business but consumes non-key area that the cost-per-key ceiling (HO-04) has to pay for. Symptom: a row dropped silently (a defect) rather than on the record (a decision) — the missing servicing row then surfaces after handover as improvised corridor space.

### HO-02 Stamp every operating ratio before it sizes anything

- Rule: No hospitality ratio in this file is a constant. Each is a market measurement with a vintage, a geography and a segment. Before a number enters a programme row it must carry all four plus its decay date; if a stamped figure is unavailable, the row is a declared heuristic (SP-22, SP-24) and may not be asserted.
- Evidence: The corpus already refuses unstamped numbers: `economics.md`'s DISCIPLINE BOX permits no prices without a stamped ratio, and SP-03 requires the basis of every number to be stated. Applied to hospitality, the stamp is the seven-term record this file prints throughout — `value | unit | driver | source | year | geography | tier` — with a decay date wherever the figure is market-sensitive: HO-05's ADA scoping is stamped "as of 2010, still the operative scoping as read this session", HO-12's HPOR ladder "As of 2025, US only", HO-23's stall ratios "as of 2021, US Florida", HO-27's RevPAR "As of 2025, US national average, top-market aggregate". Where no stamped figure exists the row is declared instead: HO-13's productive-hours divisor, HO-24's 0.45 peak-shift split and HO-21's intersection/union density bands print as `HEURISTIC`, HO-19's kitchen indices as `UNVERIFIED_INDEX`, HO-17's capture and turns as declared heuristics.
- Source: Corpus discipline carried from `economics.md` DISCIPLINE BOX (no prices without a stamped ratio) and SP-03 (state the basis of every number); applied here to hospitality figures specifically — T3 (this skill's own method, inherited from the RICS-style benchmarking caution quoted in EC).
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE (procedure)
- Scope: Universal across this file.
- Confidence: High as method.
- Grid translation: Every programme cell must read `value | unit | driver | source | year | geography | tier`. Refuse to carry a hospitality ratio with fewer than five of those filled; report `unstamped_ratios_used`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Stamping does not make a T4 figure true; it makes its weakness auditable. Cost is one extra field per row; the avoided failure is an unverifiable 1990s rule of thumb presented as an industry standard — that presentation is the symptom: a bare ratio in a programme row with no year, no geography and no tier attached, or a merged figure reading as a standard.

### HO-03 The programme is sized on occupied rooms per day, not on keys

- Rule: Every operating count downstream of the key count is a function of **POR**, so the occupancy assumption is a load-bearing design input. US full-year 2025 occupancy was **62.3 %** (−1.2 % year on year, CoStar/STR top-market aggregate, as of 2025). A 250-key design assumption therefore runs at ~156 POR, not 250; a 25 % error here mis-sizes the kitchen, the laundry, the car park and the housekeeping crew at once.
- Evidence: Hotel Dive, "Key full-year US hotel metrics fall for first time since 2020" (22 Jan 2026), reporting CoStar's full-year 2025 analysis for the US top markets (calendar 2025): occupancy **62.3 %** (−1.2 % year on year — the first full-year fall since 2020), ADR $160.54, RevPAR $100.02. The design arithmetic: `POR = keys × occupancy`, so a 250-key assumption runs at 250 × 0.623 ≈ **156 POR**, not 250; every operating count downstream of the key count (housekeeping rooms/day, linen kg/day, kitchen covers/day, FTE, car-park stalls) takes POR as its denominator, which is why a 25 % occupancy error mis-sizes four systems at once.
- Source: Hotel Dive reporting CoStar's full-year 2025 analysis — https://www.hoteldive.com/news/hotel-occupancy-revpar-decline-2025/810212/ — T3 (trade press reporting an industry data provider; US, top markets, calendar 2025).
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE (the 62.3 % value it carries is FACT — US 2025 top-market aggregate)
- Scope: All hospitality; the value is market-specific and must be replaced by the subject market's figure.
- Confidence: High for the existence of the series and its 2025 values; Medium for transferability — national averages hide market spreads far wider than the design tolerance.
- Grid translation: Compute `POR = keys × occupancy_assumed`, publish the assumption, and derive every operating row from POR rather than keys: housekeeping rooms/day, linen kg/day, kitchen covers/day, FTE, car-park stalls. In the `## Standard → spatial requirement translation table` this row is "HO-03 POR = keys × 0.623 → every service row's denominator". Emit `occupancy_assumed`, `por`, `sized_on_peak_rows`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Annual average occupancy is wrong for anything sized on a peak (check-in queue HO-22, breakfast covers HO-17, ballroom load-in). Use annual occupancy for linen, laundry and labour; use achievable peak for queues. Designing on peak occupancy buys robustness with area the investor may never see used. Symptom: a 25 % occupancy error propagating at once into the kitchen, the laundry, the car park and the housekeeping crew — the worked example's 250 keys run at POR 156, not 250.

### HO-04 The non-key area budget is set by cost per key, and the room is the residual

- Rule: In a hotel the revenue unit is the key, so capital cost is quoted per key (EC-19: US 2024 medians of ~$223k select-service → ~$409k full-service → >$1,057k luxury; all-property median ~$219k). Total GFA per key is therefore bounded: `GFA_per_key ≤ (budget_per_key ÷ cost_per_m²)`, and the key's own area is what is left after the programme rows of HO-01 are funded. Growing the room is the cheapest-looking and most expensive actual decision, because it repeats 250 times.
- Evidence: The cost-per-key ladder is EC-19's: HVS *U.S. Hotel Development Cost Survey 2025* (2024 costs, US) medians of ~$223k select-service → ~$409k full-service → >$1,057k luxury, all-property median ~$219k, cross-read against Terrapin's per-key cost/FF&E ranges (US, 2026). The bound this file draws from it is arithmetic: `GFA_per_key ≤ (budget_per_key ÷ cost_per_m²)`, and the key's own area is what is left after the HO-01 programme rows are funded. Worked at 250 upscale keys: net programme 7,500 m² (keys at 30 m² outer), key corridors and pantries ≈1,900 m², public ≈2,300 m², staff and BOH ≈1,150 m², plant/vertical/loading ≈900 m² → net ≈13,750 m² → gross at the SP-05 ladder ≈17,000–18,000 m² = **68–72 m² (272–288 tiles) of GFA per key**, non-key ≈45 %.
- Source: EC-19 in `economics.md` (HVS survey + Terrapin, US, 2024/2026; URLs held there — not restated per row here) for the ladder; the residual logic is this file's arithmetic on those figures plus the gross-area ladder of SP-05 — T3 for the ladder, derived for the method.
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: Feasibility-stage hospitality; resorts and conference hotels invert it (they buy area deliberately — EC-21).
- Confidence: Medium. The *ratios* hold; the absolute bound needs a local cost per m², which the corpus does not hold (EC *Weak or contested* item 11).
- Grid translation: Publish `tiles_per_key` (EC-19's proxy) against a declared ceiling and show the residual: keys, key area, non-key area, total. On this grid the arithmetic is tiles: 4 tiles = 1 m², so the worked example's 68–72 m² of GFA per key is 272–288 tiles per key. When the scheme fails the budget, name which of the three you cut rather than shrinking corridors (EC-19's failure mode).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Cost per key mixes land, shell, FF&E and soft costs differently across sources (EC item 13); the grid has no cost, so this rule can only be applied as a *tiles per key* proxy. Key area versus programme depth versus count of keys — the three-way lever every hotel scheme loses one of. Symptom: growing the room reads as the cheapest decision and is the most expensive, because it repeats 250 times; the corridor is the one thing that gets squeezed instead, and that is the named failure.

### HO-05 Accessible keys come out of a scoping table, and the table splits by bathroom type

- Rule: The required number of guest rooms with mobility features is a **count from a table**, and within it a defined fraction must have roll-in showers rather than transfer showers — which are different rooms with different footprints, so the scoping result propagates into bathroom geometry, door width and the accessible route (HS-07, HS-08, HS-22).
- Evidence: 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design §224.2, Table 224.2 (US federal; as of 2010, still the operative scoping as read this session), read via the UpCodes reproduction — total rooms → without roll-in / with roll-in / required total:
  - 1–25 → 1/0/1 · 26–50 → 2/0/2 · 51–75 → 3/1/4 · 76–100 → 4/1/5 · 101–150 → 5/2/7 · 151–200 → 6/2/8 · 201–300 → 7/3/10 · 301–400 → 8/4/12 · 401–500 → 9/4/13 · 501–1000 → 2 %/1 %/3 % of total.
  - §224.3 additionally scopes accessible beds where beds exceed 25 (5 %); §224.5 requires dispersion across room types.
  - Spatial consequence carried by this file's translation table: 201–300 → 7 without roll-in + 3 with roll-in = **10 keys of 250 (4.0 %)**, each accessible bath 12–14 tiles against HS-08's 3.0–3.5 m² net, on the continuous accessible route of HS-22 with 2-tile minimum clear doors (HS-05).
- Source: 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design §224.2 / Table 224.2, §224.3, §224.5, read via the UpCodes reproduction https://up.codes/s/transient-lodging-guest-rooms — T1 (federal standard), reached through a code-hosting reproduction; same provenance caveat the corpus applies to other UpCodes reads.
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT (jurisdiction: United States transient lodging)
- Scope: US transient lodging. Documented elsewhere in this corpus as jurisdiction-dependent (HS-28, `building-codes.md`); the UK/EU equivalents were not retrieved this session.
- Confidence: High for the counts; the *jurisdiction* is the United States only. The communication-accessible (hearing) room scoping of §217/§224.4 is `NEEDS VERIFICATION:` read Table 217.2 from the standard's own text before quoting a percentage.
- Grid translation: Run the table on the key count first and carry the result as a hard programme row (never as residue), then check the roll-in subset against the bathroom tile minimum already fixed in HS-08 (3.0–3.5 m² net) and the accessible-route continuity of HS-22. Report `mobility_keys`, `roll_in_keys`, `dispersion_by_room_type`. For 250 keys the answer is **10 accessible keys, 3 with roll-in showers** (4.0 % of the house).
- Exceptions / failure mode: §224.2 is a *minimum*, not a target, and the table's 501–1000 percentage band means a 600-key property is scoped on a different unit than a 500-key one. Roll-in bathrooms are the larger room; scoping them above the minimum is a market choice that eats the HO-04 residual. Symptom (this file's register, deviation 7): the accessible-room count is drawn but the hearing-features scoping is absent — a compliance gap that must close before permitting, and the UK/EU case cannot reuse the table at all without re-scoping.

### HO-06 Publish the key mix as rows before publishing any area

- Rule: "250 keys" is not a programme. Standard king, twin/double-double, accessible, suite, and extended-stay kitchenette rooms each carry a different area, a different cleaning time (HO-09) and a different linen load (HO-15), so the mix — not the total — drives both the housekeeping catchment and the plant sizing.
- Evidence: The enforceable limb is §224.5's dispersion duty (T1, via HO-05): accessible keys must be dispersed across room types, so the type list is a code-relevant object, not a preference. The design limb is that each type carries a different area (HO-08 band), a different cleaning time (HO-09: 20–30 min stayover / 30–45 min checkout, and suite area shifts both) and a different linen load (HO-15: ≈2.5 kg per occupied room per day), so the mix — not the total — drives the housekeeping catchment (HO-07) and the plant sizing. Standard king, twin/double-double, accessible, suite and extended-stay kitchenette rooms are the rows this file's matrix treats separately (Extended-stay "kitchenette adds area", All-suite "separate living, adds area"). Suite and longer-stay depth per segment: no retrieved source giving a mix percentage was opened this session.
- Source: Dispersion duty from §224.5 (T1, via HO-05 — https://up.codes/s/transient-lodging-guest-rooms); suite and longer-stay depth per segment: no retrieved source giving a mix percentage was opened this session — T1 (dispersion) / none (mix percentages).
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE (its dispersion limb is code: §224.5)
- Scope: All hospitality; extended-stay and all-suite segments are defined *by* the mix (HO-01).
- Confidence: High for the requirement to mix; `NEEDS VERIFICATION:` for every mix percentage. **What to obtain:** a published brand or management-company room-type schedule, or an STR/CBRE segment report's room-type split for the subject market.
- Grid translation: Emit a key-mix table (type × count × area × clean minutes × linen kg) and require every row to inherit its cleaning time from HO-09 and its linen from HO-15 — one driver chain, no free numbers. Flag `non_standard_module_share` (EC-11's exception count).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Without a source, a mix is a client instruction or this file's silence — record which. Mix breadth adds rate potential and adds non-standard geometry, which is the cost lever EC-11 counts. Symptom: "250 keys" carried as a single module, then the suite and kitchenette rows appearing late as non-standard geometry the plant and catchments were not sized for.

### HO-07 Keys per floor is an output of the housekeeping catchment, not a plate decision

- Rule: The floor's key count is bounded by how far an attendant walks per room (OM-14's ~46 m from the lift lobby, PP-03's ~14-room pantry catchment) and by the trolley bay at each door (OM-01). Plate geometry sets the *maximum* keys per floor; the operating bound sets the *usable* one, and the smaller governs.
- Evidence: Two independent bounds converge on ~12–14 keys per served run. PP-03 gives **13–14 rooms per attendant shift** (single T4 staffing site, Confidence Medium, held there); OM-14 gives the **150 ft walk cap** from BASE4 (Confidence Medium-Low) — i.e. ~46 m from the lift lobby — and carries there the measured 4-star Spanish time study at **74 rooms over 6 floors ≈ 12 rooms/floor** (T2). Both are cited in their own files and are **not re-derived** here. Spatial consequence: the bound is a walk length and a pantry count, not a plate shape — the worked example puts two pantries on each of 12 floors (10–11 keys each, 24 pantries, 96 m² / 384 tiles at the 4 m² BOH-register minimum) because one pantry per floor would serve 21 keys against a ~14-room catchment.
- Source: PP-03 (13–14 rooms per attendant shift, single T4 staffing site, Confidence Medium) and OM-14 (150 ft walk cap from BASE4, Confidence Medium-Low; the measured 4-star Spanish time study at 74 rooms over 6 floors ≈ 12 rooms/floor, T2, carried there) — corpus-internal rules, no URL in this file; T2 (one measured study, already in OM-14) + T4 (staffing practice).
- Class: HEURISTIC
- Scope: Multi-storey hotels; resort and low-rise campuses substitute a building catchment.
- Confidence: Medium; the agreement of a measured 12/floor with a 13–14 planning band is corroboration of direction, not of a threshold.
- Grid translation: Assert `keys_per_floor_segment ≤ 14` per pantry and `max_walk_tiles ≤ 92` (46 m) from the service portal to the farthest key door; report `catchment_violations` and `pantries_provided`. The pantry itself is ≥16 t (4 m²) per the translation table, with a trolley bay at each served door (OM-01).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Single-loaded corridors and split-level floors break the walk arithmetic; the grid has no lifts-per-floor model beyond OM-15's car spec. More, smaller floor segments = more pantries = more non-key area (HO-04) for less walking. Symptom: the plate's key count is treated as the answer and the walk to the farthest door is discovered at handover — in the worked example, one core per floor puts the far door at the run end, so the ≤92-tile cap and not the catchment governs.

### HO-08 Quote key area as a band with its measurement basis, or not at all

- Rule: Guest-room areas are the single most-quoted and least-comparable number in hotel practice — inclusive or exclusive of bathroom, closet and the corridor's share of the wall changes a "300 sq ft" room by a third (the same warning SP already raises for the hotel band).
- Evidence: Studio Anyo, *Design Principles for DFMA Hotels* (US, undated band; already carried in EC-19): standard keys **300–400 sq ft**, lobby 10–15 % of area — a practice blog's repeatability argument, not a measurement series. The basis warning is `space-programming.md`'s hotel note, which governs: inclusive or exclusive of bathroom, closet and the corridor's share of the wall changes a "300 sq ft" room by a third. Translated for this grid, the band is a footprint arithmetic: the worked example takes the midpoint as 30 m² at the outer face → 7,500 m² of keys for 250 keys, and every +2 m² per key is 500 m² = 2,000 tiles.
- Source: Studio Anyo, *Design Principles for DFMA Hotels* — T4 (practice blog, US, undated band), already carried in `economics.md` EC-19 (no URL in this file); the inclusive/exclusive warning is `space-programming.md`'s hotel note, which governs.
- Class: EXPERIENCE-BASED ADVICE
- Scope: All hospitality.
- Confidence: Low. A measured cross-sectional room-area dataset was searched for and not found readable this session — `NEEDS VERIFICATION:` **what to obtain:** a segment-banded room-area series (e.g. a cost-estimating guide with room dimensions, or a peer-reviewed hotel-design study). Until then no rule may state that rooms are "shrinking" or "getting larger".
- Grid translation: Take key area from the band, convert with the wall tax included (SP-19), and print the basis: `key footprint m², outer face of wall, bathroom included, corridor excluded`. On the host grid: 1 m² = 4 tiles, and the 0.5 m wall tile is the wall tax EC-03 counts. Never carry a bare "300 sq ft".
- Exceptions / failure mode: The DFMA band assumes repeatable rectangular modules — which suits this grid unusually well and should not be mistaken for a general design standard. +2 m² per key over 250 keys is 500 m² (2,000 tiles) of capital and a proportionate cleaning and linen load forever after (HO-09, HO-15). Symptom: two programmes quoted at the same "300 sq ft" that are not the same room — one inclusive of bathroom and closet, one not.

### HO-09 Clean time is per condition, and the bathroom is where the minutes are

- Rule: Rooms per shift is an output, not an input. It is `productive shift minutes ÷ mean clean minutes`, and mean clean minutes depend on whether the room is a stayover refresh or a full checkout reset — so the mix of conditions across the day, driven by turnover behaviour, sets the crew.
- Evidence: RapidEye Inspections, "How Many Rooms Should a Housekeeper Clean Per Day?" (Jul 2026, US practice) — T4: stayover refresh **20–30 min**, checkout reset **30–45 min**, overall average 20–45 min. Independent operational corroboration that the band moves: HotelData/Actabl Q3 2025 (US, 2025, T3) reports housekeeping task duration cut **7.1 %** in 2025, with operators describing "five to ten minutes" removed per room turnaround by protocol changes — i.e. the minute figure is managed, not fixed. The wet-cell concentration of minutes is OM-04's finding and is not repeated here. The resulting arithmetic: mean clean minutes = `Σ (rooms_by_condition × minutes_by_condition) ÷ rooms`, so the worked example's declared 65 % checkout / 35 % stayover mix gives 0.65 × 37.5 + 0.35 × 25 = **33.1 min mean** and 156 × 33.1 = **5,164 min/day = 86 clean-hours**.
- Source: RapidEye Inspections https://rapideyeinspections.com/blog/how-many-rooms-housekeepers-clean-per-day/ — T4 (Jul 2026, US practice bands); HotelData.COM / Actabl Q3 2025 via *Hotel Operations* https://hoteloperations.com/2025-hotel-labor-productivity-report/ — T3 (evidence that the band moves year to year).
- Class: EXPERIENCE-BASED ADVICE
- Scope: All hospitality, all segments; upscale adds units of time per room for material and detail count.
- Confidence: Medium for the shape (checkout ≫ stayover), Low for the absolute values — no timed observational study at T1/T2 was readable this session (the JIEM Spanish time study PDF returned binary content; its rooms-per-floor facts are already carried in OM-14).
- Grid translation: Build a daily minutes ledger: `Σ clean_min = Σ (rooms_by_condition × minutes_by_condition)`, using the band's low end only where the design removes work (OM-05's cleanability geometry). Emit `mean_clean_min_per_key`, `checkout_share_assumed`, `daily_housekeeping_minutes`. Minutes are only a driver: they become tiles through HO-10's crew count → catchments → pantry count (translation table row "HO-09 clean minutes → via HO-10", governed by OM-02 and OM-27).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Both bands are US/UK practice statements, not standards; guest-count-per-room, pet policy and suite area each shift them. A property can be run on fewer minutes per room (fewer attendants, fewer pantries, less payroll) or on quality inspection; the plan cannot tell which was chosen unless the minutes are written down. Symptom: quality collapse concentrated in wet areas (OM-04's finding) while the room count per head is praised.

### HO-10 Rooms per attendant per shift is 12–18, and the segment picks the point

- Rule: The planning band is narrow enough to be a design driver: 8 rooms where suites dominate, 12–14 in full service, 16–18 in compact economy product. This is the number that converts a key count into a crew, and a crew into catchments, pantries and lockers.
- Evidence: Three practice sites bracket the same narrow band. RapidEye (Jul 2026, US) — T4: "most hotels target somewhere between **12 and 18 rooms per 8-hour shift**"; boutique/large-suite ~8; economy/compact 16–18. PP-03's **13–14 per attendant shift** (T4 staffing site, already in the corpus). A 2026 practice article claiming productivity improvement "from 15 to 18 rooms" (T4, LinkedIn — direction only). The band's segment points are the matrix row: economy 16–18, select-service 14–18, upscale 12–14, luxury/resort/all-suite 8–12, extended-stay 12–16 + linen duty. Cross-check the two routes on the worked example: 156 POR ÷ 13.5 (mid-band) = **11.6 attendants** against the minutes route's 12.3 — agreement within 6 %.
- Source: RapidEye https://rapideyeinspections.com/blog/how-many-rooms-housekeepers-clean-per-day/ — T4 (Jul 2026, US); PP-03 in `professional-practice.md` — T4 staffing site, no URL in this file; the 2026 LinkedIn practice article — T4, direction only, no URL retrieved. T4 (three independent-ish practice sites).
- Class: HEURISTIC
- Scope: All hospitality with daily cleaning; residential-hotel and hostel variants need their own rate.
- Confidence: Medium for the band's existence and position; Low for any single property's number. `HEURISTIC`: the merged band 12–18 is stated as a range *because* the constituents are incompatible sample bases (boutique vs extended-service vs economy), not averaged into a "typical 15".
- Grid translation: Size the crew two ways — from minutes (HO-09) and from rooms-per-attendant (this rule) — and require agreement within ~20 %; disagreement means the condition mix or the band is wrong. Publish both numbers. Rooms-per-attendant is the multiplier that converts keys into pantries and catchment tiles (translation table row "HO-10 rooms per attendant 12–18 → keys per floor segment").
- Exceptions / failure mode: None of these is a standard; none adjusts for room area, so combine with HO-09's minutes rather than replacing them. Higher rooms/attendant lowers payroll and lets one pantry serve more keys (HO-07) but lengthens the walk OM-28 measures. Symptom: a merged "typical 15" quoted at a boutique or a luxury floor where the constituents never applied — the band's endpoints are the finding, not its midpoint.

### HO-11 Where a jurisdiction caps the cleaning area per head, that cap outranks the band

- Rule: Some municipal codes bound housekeeping workload in **square feet of guest-room floor area per attendant per day** rather than in rooms. Where such a cap exists it is a legal ceiling on the labour assumption and therefore a hard geometric bound: `rooms_per_attendant ≤ cap_m² ÷ mean_key_area_m²`.
- Evidence: RapidEye (Jul 2026, US) reports Los Angeles caps floor area at **3,500 sq ft (≈325 m²) per day** for hotels with 60+ rooms and 4,000 sq ft for 45–59 rooms, with 500 sq ft allowances removed for heavy checkout loads, extra floors or separate buildings, citing Los Angeles Ordinance No. 187565, LAMC §182.03 — T4 *reporting* a T1 instrument. The geometric reading this file draws: a workload cap is a legal ceiling on the labour assumption, hence on `rooms_per_attendant ≤ cap_m² ÷ mean_key_area_m²`, which is how room size (HO-08) becomes a staffing constraint through law rather than through practice.
- Source: RapidEye https://rapideyeinspections.com/blog/how-many-rooms-housekeepers-clean-per-day/ — T4 as retrieved (Jul 2026, US); the underlying instrument is Los Angeles Ordinance No. 187565, LAMC §182.03 — a municipal code, **not retrieved** (see *Sources*).
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT (claim second-hand; ordinance text unread; jurisdiction: City of Los Angeles)
- Scope: Jurisdiction-specific. Treat as a worked illustration of "a code can set the labour assumption" until verified.
- Confidence: `NEEDS VERIFICATION:` the ordinance text was not readable this session — two primary URLs (the LA City Clerk PDF and the amlegal code library) returned binary content and HTTP 403 respectively. **What to obtain:** the current LAMC Article on hotel housekeeping workload, its section number, its effective date and whether it survives the 2022 Hotel Worker Protection Ordinance.
- Grid translation: Where a workload cap applies, compute `implied_rooms_per_attendant` from it and use the **lower** of that and HO-10's band; emit `workload_cap_source`, `implied_rooms_per_attendant`. The translation-table row is "cap ÷ mean key m² → rooms per head", which then drives the pantry and catchment counts of HO-07.
- Exceptions / failure mode: A floor-area cap interacts badly with large keys: the same legal cap yields fewer rooms per head, which is the mechanism by which room size (HO-08) becomes a staffing and payroll constraint. Compliance headcount versus programme area: the cap cannot be value-engineered (EC-22's mandated area). Symptom: the cap is discovered after the key module is fixed, so the only remaining lever is payroll; and this whole rule must not be asserted outside Los Angeles until the ordinance text is read.

### HO-12 Labour intensity is the segment's fingerprint, in hours per occupied room

- Rule: HPOR is the single measured number that separates segments reliably, and it is the bridge from key count to headcount to staff area. Design consequences (crew rooms, lockers, canteens, pantries, lifts) follow from it far more directly than from room area.
- Evidence: HotelData.COM / Actabl Q3 2025 labour research (US, 2025): paid hours per occupied room by segment — extended-stay **1.30 HPOR**, limited-service **1.44**, traditional full-service **2.57**. The trend and cost limbs (Hotel Tech Report summarising Hotel Effectiveness, Mar 2022, US): labour hours per occupied room rising from 1.3 to 1.6 across the industry 2020→2022, and labour cost per room sold up 26.8 % (full-service), 14.4 % (extended-stay), 31.1 % (select-service) year on year in 2022. Downstream arithmetic carried by this file: `daily_labour_hours = POR × HPOR`, so the worked 250-key upscale case gives 156 × 2.57 = **401 labour-hours/day**, which is what sizes crew rooms, lockers, canteens, pantries and service-lift demand. The matrix prints the ladder as HPOR ~1.44 limited / 1.3–1.6 select / 2.57 upscale / >2.6 luxury and resort / 1.30 extended-stay.
- Source: HotelData.COM / Actabl Q3 2025 via *Hotel Operations* (2025, US) — https://hoteloperations.com/2025-hotel-labor-productivity-report/ — T3; Hotel Effectiveness via Hotel Tech Report (Mar 2022, US) — https://hoteltechreport.com/news/hotel-labor-cost-index-and-housekeeping-report — T3. Payroll-platform census data reported through trade press — the platform, not the study, was read.
- Class: FACT
- Scope: Full-service and select-service hotels with comparable labour law; not hospitals or serviced apartments.
- Confidence: Medium-High for the ordering and the order of magnitude (large payroll samples); Low for transfer outside the US and outside 2025 conditions. **As of 2025, US only.** Two-source tension noted in *Weak or contested* item 4 (1.6 industry-wide vs 2.57 full-service — different scopes, never merge them).
- Grid translation: Select HPOR by segment, compute POR from HO-03, and carry `daily_labour_hours = POR × HPOR` through the rest of the file as the driver for headcount, welfare area and service-lift demand (OM-15/OM-16). HPOR itself draws no tiles; it converts into lockers, canteen seats and lift trips via HO-13 and HO-24.
- Exceptions / failure mode: HPOR is wage-, not headcount-neutral: an identical plan in a high-wage market carries the same hours at higher cost. Every service level added (valet, turndown, bell, F&B covers) is visible as HPOR growth and therefore as BOH area (HO-25). Symptom: a service level promised in the brand narrative with no HPOR increase in the model, so the welfare and locker rows were never bought.

### HO-13 FTE per 100 keys must be computed, never quoted

- Rule: "Staff per key" folklore is unreproducible; HPOR × POR is. The derivation is short, uses two stamped inputs, and can be audited line by line — which is exactly what the industry's own staffing guides cannot do.
- Evidence: The derivation is composed of two stamped measurements and one declared term: HO-03's occupancy (CoStar full-year 2025 via Hotel Dive, US, 62.3 %, T3), HO-12's HPOR by segment (HotelData/Actabl Q3 2025, US, T3), and this file's productive-hours divisor — `FTE = (keys × occupancy × HPOR × 365) ÷ productive_hours_per_FTE`. Worked for the 250-key upscale case: 250 × 0.623 × 2.57 × 365 ÷ 1,800 ≈ **81 FTE ≈ 32 FTE per 100 keys**, against the corpus's own FTE constant of ≈2,080 paid hours/yr. Industry staffing guides cannot be audited line by line; this can.
- Source: Composition of HO-03 (https://www.hoteldive.com/news/hotel-occupancy-revpar-decline-2025/810212/ — T3, 62.3 % occupancy, 2025 US) and HO-12 (https://hoteloperations.com/2025-hotel-labor-productivity-report/ — T3, HPOR by segment, 2025 US) plus one heuristic term — T3 inputs + HEURISTIC conversion term.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE (procedure; the divisor is HEURISTIC)
- Scope: All hospitality feasibility; the divisor moves with labour law, not with architecture.
- Confidence: Medium. `HEURISTIC`: the divisor — productive hours per FTE per year — merges no external source; 1,700–1,900 h is this file's assumption for paid hours net of leave, training and breaks. **NEEDS VERIFICATION:** obtain a jurisdictional payroll standard (HSMAI/union schedule or a management-company budget template) for the productive-hours convention.
- Grid translation: Compute FTE from HPOR, publish the divisor as a named assumption, and sanity-check against a second route (rooms ÷ rooms-per-attendant + F&B + public areas). Report `fte`, `fte_per_100_keys`, `derivation`. FTE itself is not a tile count; the translation-table row routes it ("HO-13 FTE per 100 keys ~32 → offices, training, locker rows, see HO-24").
- Exceptions / failure mode: This yields *total* property FTE, not the design-relevant number, which is **peak shift headcount** (HO-24). A 24/7 operation can be well staffed on average and still have no one on at 04:00. ### HO-14 Convert the shift's minutes into a walking budget the plan can be tested against

- Rule: The shift is a closed minutes budget: cleaning, restock, linen handling and travel. Once cleaning minutes (HO-09) are fixed, the remainder is travel and handling — which is geometry the plan controls. This is the arithmetic OM-27 and OM-28 ask for, and it is the only way a minute-standard becomes a tile count.
- Evidence: The identity is this file's own arithmetic on HO-09/HO-10 inputs: a shift is a closed minutes budget, so `travel_min = shift_min − Σ clean_min − restock_min − linen_min`, and the residual is geometry the plan controls. The measured walking comparator (nursing: mean 9,360 steps ≈ 5.79 km per shift; >6 km treated as a design signal) and the "13–14 rooms/attendant" signal threshold are OM-28's and are referenced, not repeated. Conversion of the residual: at the declared placeholder speed the translation table row reads "tiles × 2 per minute at 1 m/s".
- Source: Ledger identity from HO-09/HO-10 inputs; the walking comparator and signal threshold from `operations-maintenance.md` OM-28 (nursing step counts, T1/T2 already held there) — derived arithmetic + T1/T2 already held in OM-28, no URL in this file.
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: Any servicing operation with a fixed shift; hotels and hospitals share the arithmetic.
- Confidence: Medium for the identity; Low for the conversion to distance — `NEEDS VERIFICATION:` **what to obtain:** an indoor walking speed with a source (no figure was retrievable in this corpus; 0.8–1.0 m/s is this file's declared placeholder, not a citation).
- Grid translation: `travel_min = shift_min − Σ clean_min − restock_min − linen_min`; convert at the declared speed to metres, then tiles (`m × 2`), and compare against the plan's measured A\* route total for one shift. Symptom: a negative residual read as "tight but workable" — the worked example's −124 min is the case where the 12th attendant is arithmetic, not slack. **Sequencing with OM-28** (*Weak or contested* item 10): OM-28 treats >6 km/shift as a design alarm, whereas this ledger can show a *minutes* deficit before any walking is counted, because the ledger includes handling time that distance does not; a plan can pass OM-28's distance test and still fail this rule, so read the ledger first. ### HO-15 Linen is a mass flow: kilograms per occupied room per day

- Rule: Laundry plant, chute sizing, trolley volume, water demand and the soiled/clean separation all scale with kilograms per day, not with rooms. Convert keys → POR → kg/day → machine capacity → plant tiles, with the machine's usable fill stated.
- Evidence: G&D Laundry, "How to Buy Hotel According to the Hotel Room for Washing Capacity" (Nov 2020, vendor geography unstated) — T4: **≈2.5 kg of linen per room per day**, "actual loading capacity for hotel washing machine is controlled at about 80 %", and 100 rooms → a 30 kg machine. Cross-check: PP-03's **5-par linen standard** (T3 laundry-planning file, already cited there) sets storage volume rather than throughput. The flow chain this file builds on it: `linen_kg_per_day = POR × 2.5` → `machine_kg_rated = linen_kg_per_day ÷ 0.80 ÷ cycles_per_day` → plant footprint; worked at 156 POR: 156 × 2.5 = **390 kg/day**, ÷ 0.8 ÷ 2 cycles = **244 kg rated capacity**.
- Source: G&D Laundry https://www.gdlaundry.com/how-to-buy-hotel-according-to-the-hotel-room-for-washing-capacity-the-washing-machine.html — T4 (vendor, Nov 2020); PP-03's 5-par linen standard in `professional-practice.md` — T3 storage-par cross-check, no URL in this file.
- Class: EXPERIENCE-BASED ADVICE
- Scope: Properties with an on-premises laundry (OPL) or with chute-and-collection logic; not F&B linen hire.
- Confidence: Low–Medium. One vendor's per-room factor, undated occupancy basis, non-OECD geography unstated; the 2.5 kg figure is *per room*, and this file applies it to occupied rooms only, which is the conservative reading for throughput and the aggressive one for storage.
- Grid translation: `linen_kg_per_day = POR × 2.5`; `machine_kg_rated = linen_kg_per_day ÷ 0.80 ÷ cycles_per_day`; then size the plant from the machine footprint plus OM-01-style clearance and the clean/soiled separation AG-11/OM-08 require. Emit `linen_kg_per_day`, `wash_kg_capacity`, `plant_tiles`. The translation-table form is "machine + 2-t (1 m) ring".
- Exceptions / failure mode: Bathrobe/duvet programmes, towel-reuse rates and banquet F&B linen each add mass; none is in the 2.5 kg. EPA-derived share evidence (HO-16) shows reuse programmes cut laundry loads ~17 %, so the operating policy changes the plant size. Par levels are storage area; throughput is machine and utility area; outsource (HO-16) converts both into a dock and a holding room. Symptom: plant sized from the room count rather than the mass flow, so the stored-par volume and the machine throughput disagree by the occupancy assumption.

### HO-16 On-premises laundry is a utility decision before it is an area decision

- Rule: The OPL-or-outsource threshold is set by water, steam and effluent capacity as much as by floor area, and the answer changes the plan in two opposite directions: in-house adds a wet plant, a chute route and a service dock within the building; outsourced removes them and adds a holding-and-collection room on the guest-free route.
- Evidence: US EPA, *Saving Water in Hotels* (Mar 2016, US agency document), reached through Harvard Business School's Institute for Business Innovation hospitality-water submission (2016): on-site laundry ≈ **16 % of hotel water consumption** and cooling tower ≈ **12 %**; guest room-reuse programmes "reduce loads of laundry by 17 %"; high-efficiency washers cut washer water demand up to 40 %. The decision to outsource is already acknowledged as a local project choice in OM-03's exceptions (NHS wording cited there). Applied in the worked example: laundry would have been ~16 % of the property's water draw, and the 390 kg/day throughput sits exactly on the in-house/outsource decision point, so the example outsources.
- Source: Harvard Business School Institute for Business Innovation, *The hotel industry's roadmap to solving diminishing water supply* (2016) — https://aiinstitute.hbs.edu/platform-rctom/submission/the-hotel-industrys-roadmap-to-solving-diminishing-water-supply/ — T3 secondary reporting the T1 agency document (US EPA, *Saving Water in Hotels*, Mar 2016; the EPA publication itself was not opened, so the percentages are one step removed); Sustainable Hospitality Alliance, *Hotel Water Measurement Initiative* resource page — https://sustainablehospitalityalliance.org/resource/hotel-water-measurement-initiative/ — methodology named, per-POR baseline not read.
- Class: FACT (secondary read; the shares are measurements, not this file's inference)
- Scope: All hotels in water-stressed or metered regions; the arithmetic is the same wherever utilities are charged.
- Confidence: Medium for the shares' order of magnitude; Low for their currency (2016). **NEEDS VERIFICATION:** absolute litres per occupied room (the industry's HWMI methodology exists — Sustainable Hospitality Alliance / One Planet, v1.0 2016 — but its baseline value was not readable this session; obtain the methodology PDF's per-POR benchmark before quoting L/POR). No numeric OPL-vs-outsource break-even in keys was retrievable either.
- Grid translation: State `laundry_strategy: in_house | outsourced | hybrid` as a programme decision with its compensating rooms; for in-house, add water/steam load to the services row (SP-20) and enforce soiled-vs-clean separation by door-count per AG-11; for outsourced, grow the soiled-linen hold per OM-08 and route collections through the dock sized in OM-10. The translation-table row is "laundry ≈16 % of hotel water (EPA 2016 via T3) → hot-water/steam plant, metering, plant per OM-17".
- Exceptions / failure mode: Chutes save labour and add a shaft, a fire compartment and a blockage job (OM-09 owns that consequence). Symptom: the strategy is decided on floor area alone and the utility load (steam, hot water, effluent, metering) is discovered at plant sizing; with no sourced break-even in keys, the decision must be printed as a decision — the worked example outsources and books a weekly collection slot on the dock (OM-10).

### HO-17 Seats per key is derived from capture and turns, not from a ratio copied off a precedent plan

- Rule: F&B seating is a queueing problem: breakfast is a narrow peak whose size depends on how many guests capture, how long they stay and how many covers a seat turns. A ratio taken from a precedent hotel silently imports that hotel's capture mix.
- Evidence: Method only — the queueing arithmetic from HO-03's POR plus SP-08's demand-curve rule ("quantity per space type = peak concurrent users × area per user", with hotels driven by occupancy × turnover) and the daily-cover structure of SP-02: `breakfast_covers_peak = POR × capture_rate`, `seats = covers_peak ÷ turns`. Worked with the two terms declared as heuristics (capture 55 %, 1.8 turns; no source retrieved): 156 × 0.55 = 86 peak covers → 86 ÷ 1.8 = **48 seats** → 62 m² (249 tiles) of dining at 1.3 m²/seat. The matrix's seats-per-key row (0–0.1 economy · 0.1–0.3 select-service · derived capture-based for upscale/luxury/resort · 0–0.2 extended-stay and all-suite) is presence and driver, not a sourced ratio. The corpus's F&B area figure (1.1–1.6 m²/cover) is already flagged `UNCITED — heuristic` in `space-programming.md` and is quarantined there per SP-24.
- Source: Method composed from HO-03, SP-08 and SP-02 (corpus-internal rules, no URL in this file); derived method, with the `UNCITED — heuristic` F&B area figure quarantined in `space-programming.md`.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: All hotels with any food service; breakfast-only properties need only the peak row.
- Confidence: `NEEDS VERIFICATION:` no retrieved source gives seats per key or breakfast capture rate by segment. **What to obtain:** a hotel feasibility template (HVS/PKR/CBRE operating statement) showing F&B covers per key by segment, or a published brand food-and-beverage programming standard.
- Grid translation: Publish `breakfast_covers_peak = POR × capture_rate`, `seats = covers_peak ÷ turns`, with `capture_rate` and `turns` declared as assumptions and their sources marked missing; then size the room from HO-18's area allowance. On the grid the result is seats → tiles: 4–6 t per seat per the translation table. Never carry a seats-per-key figure without these two terms visible.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Group breakfasts arrive as a block (PP-08's coach/conference case), so the seat count is a peak-within-peak problem, and mobile/digital check-in shifts pressure onto the same window. Seats are revenue-visible and cost-heavy per m² (kitchen follows seats, HO-19). ### HO-22 Front-desk and critical-point counts are per-100-keys transactions, and the queue is the output

- Source: `professional-practice.md` PP-08 — T3/T4 already owned there (consultant + vendor, no URL in this file); Hotel Effectiveness via Hotel Tech Report (Mar 2022) — https://hoteltechreport.com/news/hotel-labor-cost-index-and-housekeeping-report — T3, the *stayover-share* series being new evidence for the same rule.

### HO-23 Parking is provisioned on observed stall demand per key, and the observed figure is well below the mandated one

- Rule: Parking ratios are the largest single non-revenue land item in many hotel sites, and the industry's own measurements show mandated ratios far above used ones — so state provided, mandated and observed separately rather than inheriting a code default onto the site plan.
- Evidence: SSTI (State Smart Transportation Initiative, 1 Mar 2021, US university-affiliated policy blog, T3 summary) reporting John Dorsett, *ITE Journal* (February 2021, T2 article; suburban Florida field observations late 2019–early 2020): municipal ordinances typically mandate **1 stall per bedroom**, observed **85th-percentile utilisation 0.58 stalls per bedroom**, recommended reduction to 0.58; downtown hotels averaged **0.2 stalls per room** — a ~3× spread inside one study. Provisioned-vs-used arithmetic this file draws: 250 keys × 0.20 = 50 stalls ≈ 700 tiles (175 m²); × 0.58 = 145 stalls ≈ 2,000 tiles (500 m²), which is why the row is a decision and not a code default.
- Source: State Smart Transportation Initiative (1 Mar 2021) summarising John Dorsett, *ITE Journal* (Feb 2021) — https://ssti.us/2021-03-01/hotels-have-too-much-parking/ — T3 summarising a T2 professional-journal article; the ITE article itself was not opened. Context on mandate relaxation: CREDA (Fall 2025, US, housing-oriented) — https://www.credaglobal.org/research-and-publications/magazine/2025/fall-2025/development-ownership/eliminating-parking-mandates-to-tackle-the-housing-crisis — T4, not a hotel ratio.
- Class: FACT (measured utilisation) — the mandate figure is jurisdictional, not a measurement
- Scope: US surface/structured hotel parking; resort and airport segments sit at the high end.
- Confidence: Medium — one consultant's field counts, one US state, one pre-pandemic season, and the source is one step removed. Geography is decisive: suburban drive-to versus urban transit-served differ by ~3× within the same study. No post-2021 evidence of the trend was retrievable — the CoStar/STR parking-count guide returned HTTP 403, so the "developers are cutting hotel parking" claim is directionally supported only by the ITE finding and by parking-mandate-elimination literature aimed at housing (CREDA, Fall 2025). `NEEDS VERIFICATION:` before citing a current 2025/2026 industry shift.
- Grid translation: Compute `stalls = keys × ratio`, choose the ratio by location type (0.58 suburban / 0.20 urban, as of 2021, US Florida) and publish `mandated_ratio` alongside; convert with a declared stall + aisle module (`HEURISTIC:` ~13–16 tiles per incl. its aisle share — obtain a sourced stall dimension, none in this corpus). Emit `stalls`, `parking_tiles`, `parking_share_of_site`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Every avoided stall buys site area for revenue programme or light; every under-provided stall transfers the cost to the guest and to the curb. Symptom: a code default inherited onto the site plan — the mandate stated as the requirement while the observed 85th-percentile demand sits at well over half of it, and the 4× urban/suburban spread is never chosen between.

### HO-27 Test the programme against RevPAR before testing it against the site

- Rule: A hotel programme must clear an arithmetic gate: annual revenue per key must plausibly service capital cost per key. This is where segment-level area decisions are actually made, and where an under-sized or over-specified programme is caught before the plate is drawn.

Supply side, the per-key cost ladder in EC-19 (HVS 2025 survey, 2024 costs): full-service median ≈$409k/key. Operating margin input unavailable — CBRE's operating-cost series was unreachable (already recorded in EC's *Weak or contested* item 9). The gate is therefore printed as arithmetic with a missing term: at US-average RevPAR against full-service cost per key, the gap must be closed by above-average ADR, a lower non-key share, or a cheaper segment — which is deviation 1 of the worked example's register.
- Source: Hotel Dive reporting CoStar's full-year 2025 US analysis (22 Jan 2026) — https://www.hoteldive.com/news/hotel-occupancy-revpar-decline-2025/810212/ — T3; the per-key cost ladder in `economics.md` EC-19 (HVS 2025 survey, 2024 costs; URL held there) — T3. T3 for both.
- Class: HEURISTIC (a ratio test with one unsourced term)
- Scope: New-build feasibility, all segments; conversion and lease deals change the terms.
- Confidence: High for the two stamped inputs; **Low for the conclusion**, because the missing term is the GOP margin. Stated as a ratio test, not a verdict: the scheme is feasible only where `RevPAR × 365 × GOP_margin × value_multiple ≥ cost_per_key`. At US-average RevPAR and full-service cost per key, the gap must be closed by above- average ADR, a lower non-key share, or a cheaper segment. **As of 2025, US national average, top-market aggregate.**
- Grid translation: Run the gate as the *first* computation in any hotel programme; if it fails, print the required RevPAR per key implied by the chosen programme and let that be the negotiation artefact. ## Worked example: 250-key upscale hotel on a fixed site

Segment: upscale full-service, US market logic, 2026 design exercise. Every input below is stamped; every derived
number is reproducible from the printed arithmetic. No figure is smuggled in.

**Frame inputs.** Keys 250. Occupancy 62.3 % (US 2025, T3, HO-03) → **POR 156**. ADR $160.54, RevPAR $100.02 (US 2025, T3) → revenue per key ≈ $36.5k/yr. Cost per key full-service ≈$409k (HVS 2024, T3, via EC-19).

**Gate (HO-27).** 100.02 × 365 = $36,507 RevPAR-year per key against $409k capital → the programme only pencils on an above-average ADR or a reduced non-key share. Carried as the deviations register's first entry: this example proceeds *as if* the market supports it, and the reviewer should read the area decisions as the levers.

**Keys per floor (HO-07).** 250 ÷ 12 floors = 20.8 keys/floor. One pantry per floor would serve 21 keys against PP-03's ~14-room catchment, so each floor gets **two pantries** at 10–11 keys each — **24 pantries** total. At the 4 m² minimum of OM's BOH register that is 96 m² (384 tiles) of floor pantry, plus a trolley bay at each served door (OM-01). Walk check: with a mid-run core each pantry's farthest door sits ~11 keys away, well inside the ≤92-tile (46 m) cap of OM-14; with one core per floor the far door is at the run end and that cap, not the catchment, governs.

**Accessible keys (HO-05).** Table 224.2 row 201–300 → 7 without roll-in + 3 with roll-in = **10 keys (4.0 %)**, dispersed across room types (§224.5). The 3 roll-in bathrooms are the larger geometry (HS-08), and their doors are 2-tile minimum clear (HS-05) on the accessible route (HS-22).

**Housekeeping (HO-09 → HO-14).** Assumed condition mix on 156 POR: 65 % checkout resets, 35 % stayovers (declared assumption; Hotel Effectiveness's 2022 series shows this ratio is volatile, so it is not a standard). Minutes: 0.65 × 37.5 + 0.35 × 25 = **33.1 min mean** → 156 × 33.1 = **5,164 min/day = 86 clean-hours**. At 7 productive hours per attendant shift → 12.3 attendants. Cross-check by rooms per attendant (HO-10, 13.5 mid-band): 156 ÷ 13.5 = **11.6**. The two routes agree within 6 % — the staffing assumption is internally consistent. Minutes ledger (HO-14): 12 attendants × 420 min = 5,040 available; cleaning needs 5,164 → **residual negative by 124 min**, so the 12th attendant is not slack but arithmetic; the plan must supply restock and linen time from a 13th head or from catchments short enough to make travel minutes ~zero. **This is the finding a plan would otherwise hide.**

**Laundry (HO-15/16).** 156 POR × 2.5 kg = **390 kg/day**. At 80 % fill and two cycles/day: 390 ÷ 0.8 ÷ 2 = 244 kg rated capacity. A 250-key upscale property at this throughput sits exactly on the in-house/outsource decision point; with no sourced break-even (HO-16), this example **outsources**, keeps a soiled-linen hold per OM-08 and a chute per OM-09, and books a weekly collection slot on the dock (OM-10). Water proxy: laundry would have been ~16 % of the property's water draw (EPA 2016 via T3).

**F&B (HO-17/18/19).** Breakfast capture 55 % and 1.8 turns are *declared heuristics* (no source retrieved). Peak covers = 156 × 0.55 = 86; seats = 86 ÷ 1.8 = **48 seats** → at 1.3 m²/seat (HO-21 intersection) = 62 m² dining (249 tiles) + servery. Banquet: one 200-person ballroom at 1.3 m²/person banquet = 260 m² (1,040 tiles) plus foyer at 0.5 m²/person = 100 m², plus furniture store on the changeover route (PP-09). Kitchen, stores and tray counts are `UNVERIFIED_INDEX` and are carried in the register as open items rather than as false precision.

**Staff (HO-12/13/24).** 156 POR × 2.57 HPOR = **401 labour-hours/day**; × 365 ÷ 1,800 = **81 FTE ≈ 32 FTE per 100 keys**. Shift slots 401 ÷ 8 = 50; peak shift ≈ 0.45 × 50 = **23 heads on site at once** (HEURISTIC split). OM-22's arithmetic then gives: lockers = 2 × ⌈23 × 1.1⌉ = 50 → bay 6.3 m²; changing 1.4 × 50 = 70 m²; rest 1.8 × 23 = 41 m²; staff WC per its table. Total staff welfare ≈ **118 m² (472 tiles)** on the staff circuit, never the guest one (AG-15, OM-22).

## Weak or contested

1. **Housekeeping minute and room-count bands are T4-only.** 20–30 / 30–45 min and 12–18 rooms/shift come from a single US inspection-software blog (Jul 2026) plus the corpus's existing 13–14 (T4 staffing site, PP-03). No timed observational study at T1/T2 was readable (the JIEM PDF returned binary content; the Spanish study's rooms-per-floor facts are already in OM-14). Confidence Low-Medium; usable as a planning band, never as a standard. No figure from *Weak or contested* item 1 was averaged across sources into a single "typical" number.
2. **ADA scoping read through a reproduction, and incomplete.** Table 224.2's counts were read from UpCodes' page, not from the Access Board's own chapter (which truncated before §224), so the table is T1-in-substance but T3-in- transmission. The communication-accessible (hearing) room scoping was **not** obtained: §217.2/§224.4 must be read before any rule quotes a hearing-room percentage — `NEEDS VERIFICATION`. Jurisdiction is US only; the worked example cannot be used in the UK/EU without re-scoping.
3. **Segment labels are not stable across sources.** CoStar/STR, HVS and the labour platforms use economy / limited-service / select-service / upscale / full-service / luxury / extended-stay inconsistently, and the HPOR series in HO-12 groups "traditional full-service" while the cost series in EC-19 groups "full-service". Mapping them is this file's inference; the two ladders are directionally identical but are not the same taxonomy.
4. **Two HPOR figures that must never be merged.** Hotel Effectiveness (2022) reports the *industry* mean rising 1.3 → 1.6 hours per occupied room; HotelData/Actabl (Q3 2025) reports 2.57 for full-service specifically. These are different scopes (all-property weighted mean vs segment mean), not a contradiction — the trap is averaging them into ~2.0 "typical hotel" HPOR, which would be wrong for both.
5. **The LA workload cap is a second-hand code claim.** A 3,500 sq ft/day housekeeper area cap is unusually jurisdictional and materially changes HO-11; neither primary URL was readable (binary PDF; amlegal 403). Printed with NEEDS VERIFICATION and excluded from every assert.
6. **Layout densities: two T4 sources, one real disagreement.** Banquet (1.0–1.4 vs 1.2–1.4) and classroom (1.4–1.8 vs 1.4–1.7) overlap; theatre does not (0.5–0.7 vs 0.75–1.0 m²). This file uses intersections where they exist and prints the union where they do not, and states that no code or brand standard underwrites either source — both calculators disclaim code authority themselves. Fire-code occupant load (SP-07) governs doors and outranks both.
7. **Every F&B and kitchen index is missing, not weak.** Seats per key, capture rate, turns, kitchen m² per seat and chef-commis per cover were searched at all tiers and no citable value was found; they appear above as declared assumptions or `UNVERIFIED_INDEX`. The 1.1–1.6 m²/cover figure in `space-programming.md` is already quarantined as `UNCITED — heuristic` there, and this file does not upgrade it.
8. **No sourced BOH percentage for hotels.** The corpus's own register flags hotel BOH ratios as unretrieved (`space-programming.md`, *Weak or contested* item 7) and HO-25 adds no number. The counted `BOH ÷ guest` ratio is the only defensible output until a T1/T3 source exists.
9. **Parking evidence is pre-pandemic, single-state US.** Dorsett's counts (2019–2020, Florida) are the only measured ratio found; the current-trend claim ("developers dropping hotel parking") is supported by mandate-reform literature aimed at housing, not by hotel data, because the CoStar/STR parking guide 403'd. Stated as such in HO-23.
10. **Conflict with existing corpus numbers — none found, one tension.** No figure in this file contradicts OM-02 (1,400 m²/closet), OM-14 (46 m walk cap), OM-22 (welfare arithmetic) or PP-03 (13–14 rooms/attendant): HO-10's band *contains* PP-03's 13–14, and HO-07 inherits OM-14's cap unchanged. The tension is with **OM-28's implicit signal threshold**: OM-28 treats >6 km/shift as a design alarm, whereas HO-14's worked 250-key ledger shows a *minutes* deficit before any walking is counted. Reading order matters — a plan can satisfy OM-28's distance test and still fail HO-14's ledger, because the ledger includes handling time that distance does not. Recorded as a sequencing clarification, not a contradiction.
11. **Grid blind spots that these numbers cannot cross.** Time, rosters, wages, cost, water/steam volumes, headroom and the difference between 1.2 and 1.6 turns per seat are all invisible on a 0.5 m tile plane with no levels. Every minute- or kilogram-based rule above therefore exists only as a *driver* for a tile count, and its own precision is capped by that translation (SP-19, EC-03).
