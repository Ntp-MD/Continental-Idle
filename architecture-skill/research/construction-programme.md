# Research file — Domain AJ: Construction programme and productivity arithmetic (time as a design consequence)

Scope: this file supplies the **numbers behind the schedule** — floor cycle time decomposed by activity,
formwork-system achievable rates, takt/flow arithmetic, learning-curve slopes and what they do to the
first floor versus the twentieth, crew and trade-stacking minima, the offsite/DfMA demands a design has to
meet before a module pays, the programme penalty of irregular geometry, and a duration model an agent can
run on a plan.

**Boundary.** Arithmetic only; no design decision is re-opened. `construction.md` owns the *logic* of
repetition and sequencing — CN-21 (kit of parts, count the repeats), CN-22 (price non-standard geometry in
setting-out), CN-23 (sequence the plan so crews repeat floor after floor), each of which printed
`specific cycle-day figures: UNCITED — heuristic`; those figures arrive here, the duty stays in CN. Cost
and consequence structure belongs to `economics.md` (EC-09, EC-11, EC-12, EC-13, EC-15) and
`lifecycle.md` (LC-18 phases, LC-20 base-build/fit-out line): durations here are *inputs* to those
arguments, not replacements. Structural capacity, span/depth and punching are ST-01…ST-25 and CN-04…CN-06 —
this file says how long a slab must be propped, never whether it can carry it; plan-regularity *structural*
tests are ST-22. Portal and lift-queue arithmetic is VT-01…VT-24 (VT-11 owns the stacking duties) — the
construction hoist appears here only as a capacity window. Shaft *content* is SV-*, *placement* CN-11…CN-13;
site logistics is SC-* and OM-10/OM-12.

**Reading conventions.** Tiers: T1 codes/standards/government · T2 peer-reviewed and university · T3
professional/specialist · T4 practitioner and vendor. Productivity data is perishable and regional, so every
figure carries **source + year + geography + system**, with `as of <year>` where market-sensitive; days are
*working* days and a 5- or 6-day week is stated where the source specifies one. Durations computed here on
stamped inputs are labelled *derived*; unverified is `HEURISTIC`; important-and-unverified carries
`NEEDS VERIFICATION:` naming the document to open. Nothing is invented and no rate is presented as a
*standard*. Host grid: 1 tile = 0.5 m, 1 m² = 4 tiles, no levels, no time axis, no section, no cost model —
so every duration enters the design loop as a **declared days-per-cycle parameter** and every difficulty
signal as a **tile-count metric**, never as a drawable object (CP-24).

## Rules

### CP-01 Publish a cycle time per floor type, with the activity that governs it named
- Rule: No design decision that changes the floor (plate size, bay mix, core position, slab system, façade type) may be reported without the cycle-time consequence: `cycle_days(floor_type)`, the governing activity, and the tier of the rate used. A single building-level duration is unreportable.
- Evidence: Published high-rise practice states the cycle per floor type, not per building: "In cast-in-place multi-storey concrete buildings a 'typical floor' construction cycle of 5 to 7 days per floor is easily achievable and 2 and 3 days cycling is not uncommon in some areas" (Daniel, undated conference paper, MENA/US case). Documented projects run different cycles for different parts of the same tower — Mumbai (Foster + Partners, 300 m, 77 storeys): "6-day cycle for core, 4-day cycle for supporting columns"; London (PLP, 136 m, 43 storeys, hotel + residential): 5-day core cycle; Seoul (555 m, 123 storeys): 7-day; New York (426 m, 96 storeys): 2-day/3-day; Vienna timber-hybrid high-rise: 10- to 14-day.
- Source: J.S. Daniel, *High-Rise Building Construction Cycles*, conference presentation (author-affiliated practitioner paper, no venue printed) https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/jihad-daniel-paper-for-high-rise-building-construction-cycle/13488220 — **accessed 2026-09-25** — T4; Doka, *Understanding how to build high faster* (formwork-engineering case collection, Amstetten, 2019) https://www.doka.com/web/media/files/DOA_19_Brochure_Highrise_68p_en_low-res-pdf_190401ca-low-res.pdf — accessed 2026-09-25 — T3 (technical case data) with T4 promotional framing.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE (procedure)
- Scope: multi-storey framed construction, strongest in repeated-cell types (hotel, residential, ward, office slab)
- Confidence: High that cycles are per-floor-type and vary 2–14 days across documented towers; Medium that any *specific* band transfers to a project outside its region and system
- Grid translation: Emit `floor_types[]` from the CN-23 cross-floor diff, and for each `{name, plate_tiles, plate_m², cycle_days, cycle_source, governing_activity}`. The engine has no time object, so `cycle_days` is a declared parameter in the report, not a simulated quantity — print it in the same register as `assumed` values (UN-20 style).
- Exceptions / failure mode: One-off levels (lobby double-height, transfer, plant, crown) legitimately have no cycle — report them as `event_days` and count the events. Failure symptom: an agent reports "the building takes 12 months" from a single rate applied to 21 different floors.

### CP-02 Decompose the slab cycle into seven activities; the cycle is the length of the chain that cannot overlap
- Rule: Model the floor cycle as formwork strip/reset → reinforcement → MEP embeds and reservations → pour → cure-to-strip → strike-and-shore → finishing trades, then ask of each pair whether it can overlap. A cycle is not "work content ÷ crew"; it is the sequence of non-overlappable steps plus the physical waits.
- Evidence: The published four-day sequence for a 600 m² floor on two shifts is literally this decomposition: "Day 1 – place the floor forms; Day 2 – place the slab reinforcing steel; Day 3 – place E/M reservations and pour the slab; Day 4 – erect the column reinforcing steel and pour the columns to the next floor", with MEP trades at "half day work before inspection and placement of concrete" and concrete placement/finishing at "1 day and night work". The 3-day variant collapses Day 1 and Day 2 into one day across three shifts and still has to "allow 2 shifts … 24 hours for concrete curing time of the slab before proceeding with columns of the next floor" — i.e. the cure wait is the irreducible link.
- Source: Daniel, *High-Rise Building Construction Cycles* (URL above, T4) — sections 5.5.4 and 6.5, quoted as read 2026-09-25.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: cast-in-place concrete frames; the same decomposition applies to composite decks with the rebar step replaced by decking + shear studs
- Confidence: Medium-High (one detailed practitioner source, internally consistent and arithmetically reproducible; no peer-reviewed cycle study was reachable this pass)
- Grid translation: The plan *can* move three of the seven links: reinforcement (bar congestion and lap rules are invisible here, but bay count and plate regularity are not), MEP embeds (riser positions fixed by CN-11/PP-17 mean fewer coordinated penetrations), and strike-and-shore (span and slab system from CN-04/CN-05). Emit per-floor `cycle_chain = [form, rebar, mep, pour, cure, strike, finish]` with days and `overlap: yes|no`; flag any floor whose chain differs from the modal chain.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Post-tensioned and precast floors shorten or delete the cure-to-strip wait (unbonded PT is stressed before the slab above is cast); steel frames delete it entirely and shift the governor to crane lifts. Failure symptom: summing the seven durations when the real constraint is one of them (CP-03).

### CP-03 The published cycle bands, and what actually governs each
- Rule: Carry the cycle as a band tied to a named system and geometry, name the governor, and never quote a bare "5-day cycle" — a cycle travels only with its project, storey count, system and geography (the transcribed register is `## Cycle time table`). At 2–3 days the governor is the curing/crane window; at 4–6 days it is reinforcement plus formwork repositioning; at 7–10 days it is concrete strength gain plus propping; above 10 days it is system or design complexity, not labour.
- Evidence: Governing-activity statements read this pass: "On a core build, 25-50% of the time in a cycle is spent on reinforcement activities" (Doka 2019, T3) — the 4–6 day governor; "It generally takes approximately two-to-three days to achieve sufficient strength to jump the platforms to the next pour position. Generally, structures with Jumpform cores would work on a five or seven-day cycle per floor" (Doka UK, 2025, T4) — the 7-day governor; 10 MPa in 24 h by accelerator "was needed in order to allow safe stripping of the forms" on the 4–5 day tower (Daniel, T4); New York 426 m/96 storeys achieved 2-day/3-day *only* on a 28 × 28 m square footprint "remaining rigorously unchanged over the entire height" with platform repositioning "in a single lift in less than 90 minutes" and "limited craneage; only one crane" (Doka 2019). Timber-hybrid Vienna: 10- to 14-day; Mumbai luxury residential 307 m/82 storeys: 10-day.
- Source: as CP-01 plus Doka, *Jumpform vs. Slipform: Choosing the Right High-Rise Core Construction Method* (Doka UK, 2025) https://www.doka.com/uk/news/slipform_vs_jumpform — accessed 2026-09-25 — T4.
- Class: FACT (the quoted bands) / HEURISTIC (the ordering of governors)
- Scope: reinforced and composite high-rise frames
- Confidence: Medium-High on the bands (multiple independent project records converge on 4–7 days for concrete cores and slabs); Medium on the governor attribution (single-source per band, vendor-reported)
- Grid translation: `governing_activity` is reportable from plan data for two bands only: if `rebar_window` is the governor, the plan metrics that matter are bay count, plate area and regularity (CP-09, CP-18); if `cure` is the governor, nothing on the plan matters except slab system choice (CN-04/CN-05) and the declared mix. Say which band you are in before quoting a cycle.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Vendor case data reports the *best* documented cycle for the system sold, so the bands are lower envelopes, not averages. Failure symptom: adopting 3-day because a tower did it, while designing a plate that tapers every six floors.

### CP-04 Reinforcement is a quarter to a half of the cycle: decouple forming from tying before buying a system
- Rule: Where reinforcement dominates, buy separation of the rebar operation from the forming operation (multi-level climbing platforms, pre-assembled cages, a deck area large enough to work two bays ahead) — and check that the plan geometry permits it. This is the arithmetic reason behind CN-23's "keep the geometry constant through the repeat".
- Evidence: "On a core build, 25-50% of the time in a cycle is spent on reinforcement activities. With a formwork solution designed specifically for your project, you'll benefit from the following: a large workspace between the reinforcement and formwork, or additional working platforms that allow you to perform formwork and reinforcement activities in parallel" (Doka 2019). Realised on two documented towers: Kuala Lumpur 452 m/92 floors — "the building core is exceptionally large … With a lifting capacity of 10 metric tons per climbing unit … forming times are fast … the formwork and the reinforcing operations are decoupled, so work proceeds on a number of different levels at once", at "a rate of 4 days"; Seoul 555 m/123 storeys — "formwork operations and reinforcement work separated for fast cycle times by using climbing scaffolds and working platforms" (Doka 2019, and Doka press release 2016).
- Source: Doka 2019 brochure (URL at CP-01) — T3; Doka, *Reaching new heights in a 4-day cycle with Doka* (Signature Tower, Kuala Lumpur, press release 12 Dec 2016) https://www.doka.com/en/news/press/Signature-Tower — accessed 2026-09-25 — T4.
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: concrete cores and heavily reinforced plates; weak for lightly reinforced slabs and for steel frames
- Confidence: High on the mechanism (two independent project records); Medium on the 25–50% share (single vendor, no method stated)
- Grid translation: The plan cannot see bar area, but it can see the two proxies that drive tying time: `plates_with_distinct_geometry` (each unique plate is a fresh setting-out and bar-bending schedule) and `bays_per_floor` (each bay break is a lap, a stop-end and a chair). Report both against the modal floor (CP-18). Where a system is chosen for its rebar-decoupling claim, the design must keep wall lines continuous for the platform's full climb height (CN-08/CN-09, CN-03).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Fabric cages need crane time and a lay-down area — decoupling trades the crew's clock for the crane's (CP-15, CP-22). Failure symptom: a "fast" system adopted on a site with no storage and one hoist.

### CP-05 Cure-to-strip is a physics floor under the cycle: 24 h to 10 MPa with accelerators, day-tables without
- Rule: Never shorten a cycle below the strength-gain wait unless the mix is engineered for it. Print the strip criterion as (in-place strength, age, temperature, cement type) and treat the calendar table as a planning estimate only.
- Evidence: Striking minima read this pass (T4 guide restating IS 456:2000 Table 11, India): columns/walls/beam sides **16–24 h**; slab soffit with props retained **~3 days**; beam soffit with props retained **~7 days**; props under slabs ≤ ~4.5 m **~7 days**, > 4.5 m **~14 days**; props under beams ≤ ~6 m **~14 days**, > 6 m **~21 days**. Temperature and cement modify all of them: cool 5–15 °C extends "meaningfully", blended cements (PPC/fly-ash/slag) gain early strength more slowly than OPC, and the guide states that "dropping from a moderate temperature toward freezing can roughly double or more the time needed". The accelerated alternative: "Strength accelerators were used in the concrete mix for the floor slabs in order to achieve 10 MPa within 24 hours. This strength was needed in order to allow safe stripping of the forms" (Daniel). Jumpform cores: "approximately two-to-three days to achieve sufficient strength to jump the platforms to the next pour position", with "2-3 alternative concrete mixes approved to react to temperature fluctuations and maintain consistent cycle times" (Doka UK 2025).
- Source: TryBuildCalc, *Formwork Striking Time: Minimum Removal Periods by Element, Temperature, and Cement Type* (page dated 2026-07-03, figures attributed by the page to IS 456:2000 Table 11) https://trybuildcalc.com/knowledge/concrete/formwork-striking-time-guide — accessed 2026-09-25 — **T4**; Daniel (T4); Doka UK (T4). **NEEDS VERIFICATION:** IS 456:2000 Table 11 itself, BS EN 13670 (execution-of-concrete formwork-removal table) and ACI 347 — none of the three primary documents was opened; the day figures above are one vendor-page's restatement of an Indian standard and must not be quoted as a European or US code value.
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT in intent (stripping is a code-controlled decision) / HEURISTIC in the printed days
- Scope: all cast-in-place concrete; the day table is India/OPC-anchored
- Confidence: Medium on the ordering (vertical before soffit before props) — universal; Low on the absolute days (unverified against the standard, region- and mix-dependent)
- Grid translation: Not representable — no section, no temperature, no mix. Emit a `cure-window-flag` whenever `cycle_days < 4` for a concrete frame, with the text "cycle below the published strip band for ordinary cement: requires high-early mix, maturity-verified stripping, and an engineered reshoring scheme". This is a *flag*, not a calculation (VA-04 discipline).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Maturity sensing (e.g. Concremote-type monitoring, named in Doka UK 2025) genuinely unlocks sub-24 h stripping; cold-weather sites lose the gain. Failure symptom: a 3-day cycle booked on an OPC/GGBS mix in a 6 °C winter.

### CP-06 Strike-and-shore: levels of propping are bought with calendar days, and they propagate down the building
- Rule: A fast cycle is paid for in prop levels, not only in crew. Report `prop_levels = ceil(prop_hold_days / cycle_days)` and remember that props under a slab constrain what can be struck below it, so the plan's cycle and the temporary-works depth are one decision.
- Evidence: The worked case states the arithmetic directly: "To achieve a four to five day cycle, seven levels of shoring would normally be required. The structural engineer for the project performed a finite element analysis of the floor slabs and shoring and determined that only four levels of shoring would be needed. Measurements obtained from load cells … compared very closely to the results of the analysis" (Daniel, 24-storey towers, 30.8 × 30.8 m typical floor, 200 mm slab, spans 4.0–7.3 m). Independently: "Backprops are typically kept in place for a meaningfully longer period than the main formwork was in place, and on multi-storey construction, this often means backprops must be maintained through two or more floors below the most recently cast one" (TryBuildCalc). The enabling device is the early-strip head: SKYDECK "allowed for removal of the form panels and beams without removal of the shores, thereby eliminating the need to re-shore" (Daniel).
- Source: Daniel (T4); TryBuildCalc (T4, URL at CP-05). Load-check *capacity* rules are ST-10/ST-12's territory — not restated.
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: propped cast-in-place frames; steel decks need the same reasoning for slab propping
- Confidence: Medium-High that prop levels scale inversely with cycle and that analysis can reduce them; Low on the absolute level counts (one project, seismic-governed)
- Grid translation: Invisible on the plan except through span and slab system: report `prop_levels_est = ceil(28 / cycle_days) + 1` for a span > 4.5 m (28 d ≈ the ~14-day prop hold doubled for construction-load carry — **HEURISTIC, derived from CP-05's table, not a code value**) whenever `cycle_days ≤ 5`, and hand it to ST-10/ST-12 as a stated temporary-loads request.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Table-form and column-mounted systems legitimately cut the prop count; a transfer floor invalidates the assumption for the storeys below it (CN-10, ST-21). Failure symptom: an agent claiming a 4-day cycle whose 7 levels of props make the two floors below unusable for follow-on trades.

### CP-07 The core leads the slab; two cycles run in parallel and the offset *is* the schedule
- Rule: Model core and slab as separate flow lines with an explicit lead (typical: 2–4 floors), because the core's cycle governs when the slab formwork can bear and when lift portals exist. Where the two are conflated, every downstream duration is wrong.
- Evidence: "The shear walls were constructed first, three floors ahead of the slabs and columns" (Daniel). Documented split cycles on one tower: "6-day cycle for core, 4-day cycle for supporting columns" (Mumbai, Doka 2019). Slipform runs further ahead: the method exists precisely because "the ability to complete the central lift shafts and stairwells as rapidly as possible is important", with "the continual pour method associated with a self-lifting Slipform rig … quicker than the Jumpform alternative", and on the Manchester case a "daily climb of between 3.1 and 3.4 metres over a nine-to-ten-hour day", "six-day per week concreting", on 250 mm core walls of a 14-floor building (Doka UK 2025) — i.e. ~1 floor per 1 working day for a 3.2 m storey.
- Source: Daniel (T4); Doka 2019 brochure (T3); Doka UK 2025 (T4).
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: multi-storey concrete and composite frames
- Confidence: High on the mechanism; Medium on the 3-floor lead (single project; the physical limit is the slab's ability to carry fresh concrete, i.e. CP-06)
- Grid translation: The core's *position and continuity* is exactly what CN-03/CN-08/CN-09 already police, and the lead is what makes VT-11's portal stacking meaningful before handover. Report `core_lead_floors` (declared), `core_cycle_days`, `slab_cycle_days`, and check `core_lead ≤ prop_levels − 1` — a lead longer than the propping scheme allows is a temporary-works question, flag it.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Steel frames invert the dependency (the core follows the frame and the frame waits on composite decks); jump-form cores cannot pass a storey-height change without re-engineering. Failure symptom: a design with a tapering core that jumps every fifth floor.

### CP-08 Formwork system selection is a plan-geometry decision: match the system to the plate, not the reverse
- Rule: Choose the slab-forming system from the geometry the plan actually has — open plate with few walls, plate with many small cells, regular prop grid, or wall-and-slab cast together — and state the achievable cycle each rewards. This is the quantified counterpart of CN-04/CN-05 (system from span) applied to plan form.
- Evidence: The published selection logic is geometric, not commercial: table-form systems suit "large spaces, few walls, continuous floor areas"; panel/gang-form systems suit "small to mid-size spaces, many walls, separate floor areas"; truss tables suit "large spaces, parallel walls, regular prop spacing" (Doka 2019, *Floors* section). Rapid-cycle categories: "Conventional and Gang Systems … Flying Truss Systems … Column-Mounted Shoring Systems: large deck panels with framing members that span between in-place columns or bearing walls with no intermediate vertical shoring … Tunnel Form Systems: factory-made U-shaped steel forms which permit casting of a slab and the adjacent supporting walls at the same time … the forms are collapsed or telescoped and moved to the next placement location" (Daniel). Core systems: automatic self-climbing suits "the more complex a building's geometry"; slipform suits "a rapid, join-free concrete finish", is "labour-intensive for the short period the system is in operation", and "minimises the need for cranage" (Doka UK 2025).
- Source: Doka 2019 (T3); Daniel (T4); Doka UK 2025 (T4).
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: multi-storey frames; tunnel form only where the wall grid is near-constant (bearing-wall residential/hotel cells)
- Confidence: High that the systems reward different plan geometries (three independent statements); Low on any cycle figure attached to a system *other* than the projects in the table below
- Grid translation: Compute per floor `wall_density = B_int/B_ext`, `open_plate_share = walkable tiles in spans > 20 tiles / W`, `prop_grid_regularity` = share of floor on the modal bay, then emit `suggested_form_family ∈ {table, gang-panel, truss-table, column-mounted, tunnel}` as an *advisory* tag with the geometry evidence printed. Cross-check the span band against CN-05 before recommending column-mounted shoring (it needs in-place columns to hang from).
- Exceptions / failure mode: A plate can be geometrically ideal and commercially wrong (no crane, tiny site). Failure symptom: recommending tunnel form on a hotel whose core walls change thickness every six floors.

### CP-09 Takt feasibility is a design-time check: the cycle must exceed the *longest* trade's window
- Rule: Define the takt as the fixed window every trade must clear in every work zone, then test feasibility as `max_i (work_content_i ÷ (crew_i × rate_i)) ≤ takt`. A design that breaks any one trade's window breaks the flow for all the others, and the break is visible in the plan.
- Evidence: "Here, the set takt time is the maximum time allowed for a trade at any stage of the project. This is to ensure that all trades move at the same pace … this ensures that overburdening (Muri) is eliminated while optimizing resources" (Yassine et al., IGLC-22, Oslo, June 2014, T2). Flow-line results: at the Sutter Health Women's and Children's Center "when takt time and production was managed at daily level, the project was completed in 5 months instead of the planned 11"; comparing LBMS with traditional scheduling in the same building, "productivity and production rates were enhanced by 18% and 10% respectively"; the Kampi Center project "delivered … six months ahead of schedule", and LBMS "can compress large-scale schedules by 10% or more" (all quoted in Yassine et al. 2014 from Linnik et al. 2013, Evinger et al. 2013, Kenley & Seppänen 2010). The same case study shows the failure mode: a two-crew scaffolding gang, capped by the width of the work face, set the pace for eight other trades and 105 days of works. Takt is "easiest to understand in a machine-paced flow line … In construction, work is categorized by tasks and phases" (Linnik, Berghede & Ballard 2013, in the Berkeley P2SL takt reader, T2/T3).
- Source: T. Yassine, M.B.S. Bacha, F. Fayek & F. Hamzeh, "Implementing Takt-Time Planning in Construction to Improve Work Flow", *Proc. 23rd Annual Conference of the International Group for Lean Construction (IGLC-22)*, Oslo, June 2014, pp. 787–797 https://iglcstorage.blob.core.windows.net/papers/attachment-bb70dc89-0329-4e6c-9ba5-72a1c999d9f2.pdf — accessed 2026-09-25 — **T2**; M. Linnik, K. Berghede & G. Ballard (2013) and I.D. Tommelein (2017), *Takt Planning Workshop reader*, UC Berkeley P2SL http://p2sl.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/08/Theis_Tommelein_Emdanat-2017-Workshop-Reader.pdf — accessed 2026-09-25 — **T2/T3**.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE (procedure) with FACT content for the three project results
- Scope: any repetitive flow — structural cycle, fit-out floor sequence, façade installation train
- Confidence: High on the method; Medium on the reported gains (each is a single-project before/after, not a controlled study); the 5-vs-11-months result is a *reported* case with a changed measurement regime, so it double-counts method and management
- Grid translation: Run the takt check at design time from plan quantities: `zones = plate ÷ work face`, `trade_window_i = distinct_element_count_i × unit_rate_i`. Print `takt_feasible: yes|no|binding_trade`. The three plan measures that decide feasibility are `distinct_floor_types`, `unique_slab_panels` and `bay_variation` (CP-18) — all three are computable here, and none of them is a planner input.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Takt degrades where the work face is not width-limited but crane-limited (CP-15); forcing takt onto non-repetitive work is the acknowledged misuse ("takt time planning has been used in construction, but was limited to highly repetitive projects", Linnik et al. 2013). Failure symptom: a beautiful 6-day takt line over a plan with nine distinct floor types.

### CP-10 Work zones and crew continuity: a floor is a production line, not a place
- Rule: Divide each floor into repeatable work zones sized so one crew clears one zone per takt, and keep the same crew on the same task across zones. Zone boundaries must follow the structural grid, so the design decides whether a workable zone even exists.
- Evidence: "The floor area is usually divided into zones to allow the labor force and formwork materials moving between zones. The preparation of the floor construction cycle would therefore be a resources allocation exercise … Floats are created deliberately in the schedule to ensure the balance in resources and to provide buffers" (Daniel, §5.5.2). The same author's simulation model is built on "the activities in the four zones" with "ten activities … scheduled in a sequential order" and loops "to show the re-cycling of the resources". Takt planning's own precondition is a "location breakdown structure" with work zones "kept as small as possible, given the speed of movement of workers" (Linnik/Berghede/Ballard 2013; Tommelein 2017). Crew continuity evidence is negative and concrete: in the IGLC-22 case the constraint was a crew capped at two because "catch pits are too narrow limiting crew size to 2", and the remedy was "the same team will move from zone to zone doing the same tasks without disruptions".
- Source: Daniel (T4); Yassine et al. IGLC-22 (T2); Berkeley P2SL reader (T2/T3).
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: repetitive structures and fit-out
- Confidence: High on the method (three sources agree), Medium on the zone count used (4 zones is one author's model)
- Grid translation: `zones_per_floor = ceil(plate_m² / target_zone_m²)` and **the zone cut must lie on a structural line** — so test `zone_cuts_on_grid = zone_boundaries ∩ grid_axes`. Report `min_work_face_tiles` per trade; a zone whose face is narrower than the crew needs is the same defect as the catch-pit case, and it is measurable here (a 1-tile-wide working strip cannot host a 20-person gang). Cross-ref GT-10/GT-11 (never express a dimension below one tile; no slivers).
- Exceptions / failure mode: More zones ≠ more speed once the crane or hoist is the shared resource (CP-15). Failure symptom: four zones drawn on a plate with one lift core and one hoist.

### CP-11 Learning curve: use the 70–90% band, print the formula, and cap it with the physical floor
- Rule: Apply learning to *crew-hours*, not to *elapsed cycle days*, and only within the published band. State `T_n = T_1 · n^b`, `b = ln(S)/ln 2`, and cap the result at the shortest cycle the physics allows (CP-05/CP-06) — otherwise the model promises a 3-day cycle on the 20th floor that no crew can deliver.
- Evidence: "The expected range of learning rate for most construction activities falls between 70% and 90%… if the cost to construct the first unit or cycle is 200 man-hours, then it would take 200 × 70% or 140 man-hours/unit on average to construct the next two units and 140 × 70% or 98 man-hours/unit on average to construct the next four" (IJERT 7(04), 2018, India, T4 — the band and the worked arithmetic are standard Wright-form material). Model form as printed there: `Y = T·X^b`, learning rate `S = 2^b × 100` (the page typesets this as "S = (2b)*100"; with b = −0.893 the printed result 53.84 % only reconciles as 2^b — the paper's arithmetic is reproducible, its notation is not). *Derived* on that form: with S = 0.90, b = −0.152 and floor 20 = 0.63 × floor 1; with S = 0.85, b = −0.234 and floor 20 = 0.50 × floor 1; with S = 0.95, b = −0.074 and floor 20 = 0.80 × floor 1. Practice statements agree with the capped reading: "Labor productivity may neither reach 100 percent of the normal level at the very first floors nor the very top floors" (IJSER 4(3), 2016, Kerala, T4), and CN-23's rule that a design should "give the structure enough floors to amortise the first-floor learning cost".
- Source: Narayanan K M, A. & Aswathi M P, "Study of the Applicability of Learning Curve Theory to Recurring Activities of Formwork, Reinforcing Steel Fixing and Concreting", *IJERT* Vol. 7 Issue 04, April 2018, ISSN 2278-0181 (CC-BY) https://www.ijert.org/research/study-of-the-applicability-of-learning-curve-theory-to-recurring-activities-of-formwork-reinforcing-steel-fixing-and-concreting-IJERTV7IS040391.pdf — accessed 2026-09-25 — **T4**; Amrutha Raman & Aby Varghese, "Study on Labour Productivity by Learning Curve Effect", *IJSER* Vol. 4 Issue 3, March 2016 https://www.ijser.in/archives/v4i3/IJSER15699.pdf — accessed 2026-09-25 — **T4**.
- Class: FACT (the formula, the band) / HEURISTIC (the cap)
- Scope: genuinely repetitive floors with the same crew; not for a 6-floor block or a project that changes detail design mid-stack
- Confidence: Medium-High on the band's existence (restated across the construction-management literature), High on the derived multipliers (arithmetic), Low on any specific S for a specific trade
- Grid translation: Report `floors_in_repeat` (CN-23's constancy numerator) as the learning-amortisation count, and `learning_premium_days = Σ_{n=1..k} max(0, T_1·n^b − T_plate)` for the first k floors. In the worked programme (CP-23) a 14-floor repeat at T₁ = 8 d, S = 0.85, T_plate = 6 d costs **≈ 3 extra days for the whole tower** (*derived*) — small enough that geometry, not learning, is the design lever.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Learning decays when the crew changes (a subcontractor swap mid-stack resets n), and *reverses* when the design changes: the influence factors named for high-rise learning are "task change, work adaptation, vertical transportation time" (Lee, Lee, Park & Kim 2015, ASCE — cited in IJSER's literature review, **not read directly**). Failure symptom: a bid that books 20% learning on a design with six floor types.

### CP-12 The naive learning-curve failure: measured slopes that cannot be believed, and the three reasons why
- Rule: Refuse a learning claim without (a) the unit measured, (b) the number of observations, (c) an explanation of what else changed. Where those are absent, model the first floors with a declared premium and print no slope at all.
- Evidence: The IJERT study fitted 14 identical floors of a real building and reports learning rates of **53.84% (formwork slab), 61.77% (column), 67.68% (beam), 42.95% (rebar slab), 53.25% (rebar column), 78.94% (rebar beam), 34.77% (concreting)** — all far below the same paper's own 70–90% expectation, and interpreted inconsistently inside the paper (78.94% is described as "basically no productivity improvement" while 34.77% is "significant"). Its dataset holds constant quantities per floor (1,475 / 123.4 / 147 with per-floor hours swinging 1,980 → 1,254) and no controls for crew, weather or sequencing. The IJSER study, same region and same 14-floor geometry, fitted partition-wall and plastering work and concluded the opposite direction: "the cumulative average labor inputs increase as the cycle number increases" — i.e. **no learning detected in finishing trades** (plastering ran at 7 days per floor, 21 workers, 1,666.9 m² per floor). The methodological caution is published independently: MCAA's own factors table is described as "gathered anecdotally … MCAA does not have in its possession any records indicating that a statistical or other type of empirical study was undertaken" (quoted in Ottesen 2019), and five-project evidence "does not represent a large enough sample size to drive statistical significance" with the explicit note that "neither the schedule itself nor the parameters drawn from it serve as cause(s) for the observed labor productivity losses" (Ottesen 2019).
- Source: IJERT 2018 and IJSER 2016 (URLs at CP-11) — T4, both read in full this pass; J.L. Ottesen, *CPM Schedule Density: A New Predictor for Productivity Loss*, PhD dissertation, University of Washington, 2019 https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/items/b6cce1ab-9ba0-4b55-8308-dc3c5af7e8cb — accessed 2026-09-25 — **T2**.
- Class: HEURISTIC (the caution) / FACT (the reported values, as reported)
- Scope: every productivity claim in this file
- Confidence: High that these specific slopes are not usable; Medium on the general criticism (it is well made in the literature but rarely with numbers attached)
- Grid translation: `learning_claim_allowed = (source_states_unit AND n_observations ≥ 10 AND confounders_declared)`; otherwise emit `learning_premium = declared_days, slope = not_modelled`. CN-21's "learning-curve percentages: UNCITED — heuristic" marker stays UNCITED until a Tier-1/2 slope for the specific task is opened — this file supplies the method and the band, not a licensed slope.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Manufacturing-led precast learning is real and steeper than site learning (see CP-19's precast study note). Failure symptom: bidding a 20% cycle reduction "because of learning" on a design that changes every fifth floor.

### CP-13 Trade stacking has published loss bands, and design controls the density that produces them
- Rule: Treat simultaneous trades in one space as a measurable load. Report planned `persons_per_100m²` per floor per day, and price the loss band where the plan forces density up.
- Evidence: Stacking-of-trades is the **first-listed** factor in the mechanical contractors' table with "percent loss in productivity to range from 10 percent for minor impacts to 30 percent for severe impacts" (MCAA, *Change Orders and Labor Productivity*, p. 77 — quoted in Ottesen 2019, T2, page as quoted; the manual itself was not opened). MCAA's broader factor set gives "productivity losses for such affects vary from five to 30 percent". AACE's recommended practice states the mechanism: "To achieve good productivity each member of a crew must have sufficient working space … when more labor is assigned to work in a fixed amount of space it is probable that interference may occur … when multiple trades are assigned to work in the same area, the probability of interference rises and productivity may decline" (AACE RP quoted in Ottesen 2019). USACE (1979, quoted in the same source) defines overcrowding as the increase of all labor types within a given work area and found productivity falls as it increases. Rojas (2008, via Ottesen) regressed electrical labour against working area, found "significantly lesser productivity rates with smaller areas", found losses "significantly greater than reported by USACE when non-electrical trades were stacked with electrical trades", and found stacking most likely in the **third and fourth quartiles** of project duration; Hanna, Russell & Emerson (2002, via Ottesen) found acceleration and schedule delay to be the two primary causes. Environmental context from the same review: hot/humid conditions ≈ 50% output loss and cold ≥ 40% (Adrian 1987, via Ottesen).
- Source: Ottesen (T2, URL at CP-12), pp. 25–29 and 44 as read.
- Class: FACT (the published bands, as quoted) / ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT (the mechanism)
- Scope: enclosed, multi-trade floors; weak for open structures
- Confidence: Medium on direction (corroborated by several independent bodies); **Low on magnitude — every figure traces to association tables the publishers themselves call anecdotal**; the *derived* head-counts below are firmer than the loss percentages
- Grid translation: Compute planned density from this file's own crew register: the 4-day, 600 m² floor puts ≈ 44 heads on the plate on pour day (MEP 9 + electrical 9 + workshop rebar 9 + placing/finishing 17, Daniel's lists) → `7.3 persons/100 m²` (*derived*). Two work faces on the same plate double that. Emit `persons_per_100m2_peak`, `trades_in_parallel`, and flag when peak density exceeds the modal-floor density by more than ~50% (**HEURISTIC threshold, not a standard**). This is a genuinely plan-visible number: it is a function of floor count per work front and plate area, both of which the engine has.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Density is unavoidable in fast fit-out and is bought with hours, not always with output. Failure symptom: adding a second work front to "halve" a cycle and observing that both fronts slowed.

### CP-14 Minimum crews to hold a cycle, and the arithmetic of adding a second work front
- Rule: A cycle is held by named crews per activity; halving it needs either doubled crews on the same face or a genuine second face. Report the crew list, not the intention.
- Evidence: Two published crew schedules for the *same* 600 m² floor (see `## Crew register`): the 7–8-day, two-shift case requires 20 formwork hands, 20 field-rebar hands, 7 workshop rebar, 6 plumbing/HVAC, 6 electrical, 12 concrete placing/finishing; the 4-day, two-shift case requires 25 formwork on Day 1, 25 rebar on Day 2, then a Day 3 stack of 9 MEP + 9 electrical + 9 workshop + 17 placing/finishing (44 heads simultaneous), and 14 + 14 + 5 + 10 on Day 4. The 3-day case is obtained "with a three shift work" by merging Day 1 and Day 2, not by adding people. *Derived*: at 600 m² the 4-day case implies roughly 25 formwork persons per floor-cycle-day and one full floor of slab rebar per 20-person crew-day.
- Source: Daniel (T4), §5.5.4, transcribed verbatim into the register.
- Class: HEURISTIC (the extrapolation to other plates) / FACT (the published lists)
- Scope: concrete frames around 600 m² plates, two shifts, Middle East practice as of the paper's date (undated; cites 1996–97 US/Canada case; UAE commentary)
- Confidence: Medium on internal consistency; Low on transfer to a 1,200 m² plate or a single-shift European site
- Grid translation: `crew_days_required(floor) = plate_m² / 600 × (sum of register crew-days)` as a *first-order* screen, printed as derived, and `second_front_test`: a second work front helps only if `zones ≥ 2` with independent access to a work face (CP-10) **and** hoist/crane capacity for the doubled material flow (CP-15, VT-11). Otherwise the second front is a stacking penalty (CP-13).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Labour availability, not arithmetic, binds: "The unprecedented number of projects in the UAE and the region makes the availability of such resources a challenge" (Daniel §5.5.3). Failure symptom: a two-front programme drawn on a single-core plate.

### CP-15 Vertical transport during construction is the scarce resource: crane time, picks and hoist windows
- Rule: Where two work fronts compete for one crane, the crane is the schedule. Report lift count and peak pick weight per cycle alongside the design, and treat the plan's coring/hoist provision as a capacity decision.
- Evidence: "Minimizing crane time was key to construction scheduling"; "Time-to-completion was enhanced due to the fact that the PERI systems move up the tower at all times and never have to be brought back to the ground" (Daniel). "Normally a tower crane can only be installed for a building block owing to both economic reasons and space availability. Therefore, the crane can only serve one activity at one time and it is important to optimize the usage of a tower crane which is one of the critical resources in high-rise building construction" (Daniel §5.6.2). Project-side constraints read this pass: "limited craneage; only one crane with needle-type boom" (432 Park, New York — which still ran 2/3-day cycles); "tight schedule and limited crane capacities" (One Bishopsgate Plaza, London, 43 storeys, 5-day core cycle, where the chosen climbing system "meant no obstructions for crane-lifting the prefabricated rebar cages into place" and gave "safe craneless repositioning"); a 10 t per climbing-unit lifting capacity as the enabling number at Kuala Lumpur; "the more typical constraints on site" driving a move to self-climbing systems so as not "to risk weather affecting the movement of the core's construction when crane wind limits are reached" (Doka UK 2025). Offsite modules inherit the same window: pods are normally mobile-crane set with tower cranes used "on high-rise projects" subject to "25–35 mph wind thresholds" (Projul, US, 2024).

### CP-16 Envelope close, not the frame, is frequently the critical path: resource the façade or lose the takt
- Rule: Compute the enclosure rate demand independently of the structural cycle, and compare. Where enclosure needs more calendar days than the frame, the frame cycle is not the lever and shortening it buys nothing.
- Evidence: Unitised façade installation is documented at "roughly ~3–4 m² per person per day," against "~1–1.6 [m² per person per day] for stick systems", with unitised work closing the building "watertight earlier" through "faster enclosure", and costing "10–30% more up-front engineering" (Staticus, UK contractor, page dated 2025-08-25, T4). *Derived* on the worked case (CP-23): a 21-floor hotel with 9,026 m² of exposed envelope needs **21 installers** (70.5 m²/day) to keep pace with a 6-day frame cycle; at a 14-person crew the envelope finishes **56 working days** behind the frame and *becomes* the completion date.
- Source: Staticus, *Unitised curtain wall facade* https://staticus.com/unitised-curtain-wall-facade/ — accessed 2026-09-25 — **T4 (vendor/practitioner; rates unstudied)**; derivation from CP-23's own arithmetic.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE with HEURISTIC rate
- Scope: all towers with a curtain-wall or unitised envelope; for masonry/prefaced systems replace the rate but keep the comparison
- Confidence: Medium on the direction (the enclosure-behind-frame pattern is universal in practice); **Low on the 3–4 m²/person/day figure — single vendor page, no method, UK only.** `NEEDS VERIFICATION:` a published façade installation productivity study (e.g. *Journal of Facade Engineering and Research*) or an RSMeans-documented unitised erection unit rate.

This file adds the comparison `envelope_days = Σ A_façade / (rate × crew)` vs `frame_days` and prints which is binding. Nothing new is measured; a new *verdict* is issued.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Where the envelope is a unitised rack hung from the slab edge, the frame and the façade cannot share a floor simultaneously — the lag then has a *minimum*, not just a rate. Failure symptom: reporting programme from the structural cycle alone and being 8 weeks wrong at practical completion.

### CP-17 Geometry penalty register: what each irregularity costs in days
- Rule: Charge geometry to the schedule as a stated day-penalty per floor or per event, with the evidence tier printed. Absorb CN-22's "setting-out cost" here as *time* and CN-23's constancy as *days saved*, and place the unavoidable one-offs at the **ends** of the repeat: a transfer, lobby, plant or crown level sandwiched inside the modal run costs more programme than the same level at the bottom or top, because it breaks the crew cycle twice.

- Confidence: High on direction (every documented fast cycle sits on a constant plate; every documented slow one sits on change), **Low on magnitude — the ranges are chosen, not derived.**

### CP-18 Repetition drift is the leading indicator: count floor types, slab panels and bay variants
- Rule: Drift — small per-floor changes to a nominally repeated module — is worse for the programme than deliberate variety, because it defeats reuse without paying for a design change. Measure it as counts, not impressions.
- Evidence: CN-21 already mandates `distinctPartCount ≤ 6` and "every repeated part is translated, not resized", and CN-23 mandates `constancy ≥ 0.6`, both flagged in `construction.md`'s own UNCITED ledger as chosen thresholds. The programme reason is now quantifiable from this file: reinforcement is 25–50% of the cycle (CP-04) and every new geometry re-starts it; the fastest documented tower cycle belongs to the only project recorded as having a footprint "remaining rigorously unchanged over the entire height" (CP-03); and the acknowledged cause of lost learning is "task change" (Lee et al. 2015, via IJSER).
- Source: in-corpus CN-21/CN-23 thresholds + Doka 2019 (T3) + IJSER 2016 (T4) for the mechanism.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE (measurement procedure)
- Scope: all repetitive types
- Confidence: High on the arithmetic (counts are exact); Medium on the transfer to duration (correlation, not measurement)

### CP-19 Offsite does not remove the cycle: it relocates it, and each system makes specific demands on the design
- Rule: Adopt an offsite system only with its design conditions met and its residual on-site sequence drawn. Panelised, pod, module and unitised systems each demand a different thing from the plan; none of them makes the plan free.
- Evidence: Documented demands, read this pass — **pods:** "10 to 20 pods can be set" per day with "2–4 hours of field utility work per unit", finished weight "3,000 to 8,000 pounds", road-envelope maxima "approximately 8 feet 6 inches wide, 13 feet 6 inches tall, and up to 40 feet long" without special permits, a required "flat, weather-shielded staging pad reachable by delivery trucks and lifting equipment", and "even an inch" of dimensional shift complicating placement (Projul, US 2024/2026, T4). **Unitised envelope:** ~3–4 m²/person/day installed, earlier watertightness, and "10–30% more up-front engineering", with a factory of 12,050 m² floor space rated at 180,000 m²/year (Staticus 2025, T4). **Prefabricated/volumetric generally:** "Factory and site storage requirements also need to be indicated by the manufacturer"; volumetric units need "lifting points … adequately engineered with supporting manufacturer documentation", protection from "moisture, vibration, impact damage", may need "temporary works … depending on the design and engineering", and "whole building superstructures typically require to be transported in sections" with on-site assembly at the substructure and plumbing interfaces (ABCB/Building 4.0 CRC, *Prefabricated, modular and offsite construction Handbook*, Australia, 2024 — **T1**-grade national guidance, read as PDF). **Precast learning:** factory production of precast components is where steep learning is documented (a Taiwan study on >90% of new precast projects 2015–2019, fitting worker learning curves with R² > 0.88 for main beam, minor beam and column production — Tai, Chen, Cheng et al., *Int. J. Civ. Eng.*, read as a repository copy; the factory-side gain is not automatically a site-side gain).

### CP-20 Repetition threshold: the count below which an offsite system loses money and adds programme
- Rule: State the repetition count an offsystem needs before it pays, as a property of the system, and test the design's counts against it before recommending offsite.

### CP-21 Tolerance stack-up and storey-height coordination are design-time conditions, not site fixes
- Rule: Wherever a manufactured element meets a cast one, the design must carry the tolerance explicitly: an interface zone in the tile model, and a declared mm budget on paper. Two compliant tolerances meeting can still be non-compliant together.
- Evidence: The mechanism is documented with the standard names: "problems can arise when a concrete slab is poured to the dimensional tolerance limits set out by Australian Standards AS 2870 Residential slabs and footings and AS 3600 Concrete structures. The slab is NCC compliant, but a dimensionally accurate and square wall placed adjacent may: create gaps that need to be weatherproofed; need to be rectified at the slab-to-wall or wall-to-wall interface; lead to lack of adequate fire protection; lead to reduced acoustic performance" (ABCB Handbook 2024, T1/T3) — note that the two failed performance properties are exactly the ones this corpus enforces as plan geometry (CN-26 party/acoustic, CN-13/CP-… rated separation). Interface risk is elevated for panelised systems against cast cores and for lift cores: interface issues "with post and plate systems often arise due to construction tolerance" and the interfaces that matter are "connections to external walls, windows, roofs, floors, foundations, and lift cores". The pod-side version is the vendor statement that "even an inch" of dimensional shift complicates placement (Projul).
- Source: ABCB/Building 4.0 CRC Handbook (T1/T3, URL at CP-19); Projul (T4). **NEEDS VERIFICATION:** the actual numeric tolerance bands in AS 3600/AS 2870, BS 5896/EN 13670 and ACI 117 — none of these was opened; no mm figure is quoted in this file.
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT in intent / HEURISTIC in magnitude
- Scope: every offsite-to-onsite interface, including unitised façades against slab edges (CN-28)
- Confidence: High on the requirement and on which performances break; Low on any millimetre value (not printed)
- Grid translation: On a 0.5 m tile grid a tolerance zone cannot be drawn at 25 mm, so express it as a **tag**: `tolerance_interface` on every tile where a manufactured element meets a cast one, with `mm_budget` as a declared annotation, and enforce `module_footprint_tiles × 0.5 m ≥ nominal_module + 2 × budget`. CN-28's "facade sits inside/outside the column line, declared once, repeated" is the plan-level version of this rule; this file adds the *programme* consequence — an uncoordinated tolerance is a rectification loop, which is days on the critical path (CP-13 density, CP-17 change).
- Exceptions / failure mode: All-cast-in-place buildings escape the interface at the price of the cycle (CP-04/CP-14). Failure symptom: pods that "fit on the drawing" and a floor-to-floor that is 40 mm short.

### CP-22 Site storage and delivery windows convert an offsite design decision into a tile count
- Rule: Offsite programmes are logistically bounded: the design owes the site a staging area, an unloading sequence and a just-in-time reliability statement. Model them as tiles on the site plan, or the delivery rate assumed in CP-19/CP-20 cannot happen.
- Evidence: "Factory and site storage requirements also need to be indicated by the manufacturer to ensure integrity of the product" and "Transport of wall systems via road, rail or ship attracts relevant rules and regulations. These are outside the NCC" (ABCB 2024); pods need "a flat, weather-shielded staging pad reachable by delivery trucks and lifting equipment" and are road-limited to ~2.6 × 4.1 × 12.2 m without permits (Projul); crane-independent repositioning and pre-built delivered units were chosen on a London scheme specifically to "minimise storage and reduce start-up time on site" where "the construction sites' limited space" was the binding challenge (Doka 2019 / Doka UK 2025).
- Source: ABCB (T1/T3); Projul (T4); Doka UK 2025 (T4).
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: urban sites and all offsite-heavy schemes
- Confidence: Medium-High (three sources agree that storage is a first-order constraint)
- Grid translation: `staging_tiles = required lay-down m² / 0.25` on the site layer, checked against SC-03's buildable envelope and OM-10's arrivals-per-hour bay arithmetic (both owned there, not restated). Report `staging_share_of_site` and `delivery_window_conflicts` when staging overlaps the permanent service yard.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Just-in-time delivery removes the tiles and imports the risk of a factory delay; state which of the two the programme assumes. Failure symptom: an offsite scheme on a site with no lay-down, whose programme is otherwise identical to a site with 800 m² of it.

### CP-23 The duration model an agent can run
- Rule: Reduce the design to a small, auditable model — floors, plate, cycle, work fronts, transport, envelope rate — and report the four outputs (structural frame, envelope close, fit-out, total) plus the binding path. Print every input's tier.
- Evidence: The model's shape is the published decomposition: cycle per floor type (CP-01/CP-03), core lead (CP-07), crew and stacking (CP-13/CP-14), envelope rate and the frame-vs-envelope comparison (CP-16), plus the learning premium (CP-11). Formulas (*derived*, this file): `frame_days = Σ_i cycle_i` with `cycle_i = max(cycle_plate, T_1 · i^b)` per CP-11's cap; `envelope_days = A_env_total / (r_env × n_env)` started at day `d_0` once the frame is two levels up; `fitout_days = Σ floors × t_fit / n_fronts` run `lag` levels behind the envelope; `total = max(frame, d_0 + envelope) + t_fit + handover`. Author's own caution adopted: "a simple approach is to adopt a constant duration for the construction of the typical floors. However, this always induces a false impression to site personnel that the construction processes are simple and could be achieved easily" (Daniel §5.5.2).
- Source: Daniel (T4) for the caution and the decomposition; IGLC-22 (T2) for the takt formulation; CP-11/CP-16 for the rates. Also note the published compression relation used as a sanity check: "a saving of approximately 35% in time could be achieved when the working period is extended by 20%" (Daniel §5.5.2, **T4, no method stated — do not quote as a standard**).
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE (procedure)
- Scope: multi-storey; degrades to a bar chart for one-off buildings, and say so
- Confidence: High on arithmetic, and the confidence of the *answer* is exactly the confidence of the weakest input row
- Grid translation: Inputs the engine has: `floors`, `plate_tiles/floor`, `floor_types`, `P_per_m`, `bay counts`. Inputs it must be **given**: `cycle_days`, `r_env`, `floor_to_floor`, `n_crew`, `d_0`, substructure duration, consent and weather. Outputs: `{frame_days, envelope_days, fitout_days, total_days, binding_path}` plus the sensitivity table (see `## Worked 21-floor programme`)
- Exceptions / failure mode: The model has no resource levelling and no weather calendar; a 244-day answer is 244 *working* days, not 8 months. Failure symptom: reporting the total without the binding path, which is the only decision-relevant column.

### CP-24 Grid adapter: what a plan-level engine can measure, and what it must ask a planner for
- Rule: Split the schedule inputs into *plan-measurable difficulty* and *declared rates*, and never derive the second from the first. Emit the difficulty metrics as a vector with the rate inputs separately sourced and tiered.
- Evidence: Plan-measurable, from this file's own rules: floor-type count, unique slab panels, bay variety, drift index, core regularity, façade perimeter per area, zone cuts on grid, staging share, tolerance-interface count, hoist-shaft presence. Not plan-measurable: mix design and strength gain (CP-05), prop capacity (ST-10), crew availability, crane model, consent, weather, market. The corpus already owns the regularity arithmetic for *structure* (ST-22 "test plan regularity with numbers the plan can answer") and the constancy check (CN-23) — this file's contribution is the *schedule* reading of those same counts, plus the explicit boundary that no duration is derivable from geometry alone (CP-17's ranges are HEURISTIC precisely because no complexity→duration regression was found).
- Source: this file's rules + ST-22, CN-21/CN-23, EC-08/EC-11 in-corpus; IGLC-22 (T2) and Ottesen (T2) for the measurable-density idea — Ottesen's schedule-density metric is quantified in Crew Day Resources from a resource-unloaded CPM file, i.e. it needs the *programme*, not the plan.
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT (about the tool, not the building)
- Scope: this host grid specifically
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: The adapter contract — **compute** `distinct_floor_types, unique_slab_panels, bay_width_variety, drift_index, constancy, P_per_m, zones_on_grid, tolerance_interfaces, staging_share`; **ask** for `cycle_days_by_floor_type, prop_hold_days, r_env, crew_sizes, d_0, substructure_days, weather_regime, consent_days`; **flag** `NEEDS VERIFICATION:` on any asked input the planner declines, and refuse to print a completion date without it (Quantitative-Layer rule 1: compute or flag, never guess).
- Exceptions / failure mode: A plan can be perfectly regular and the programme still slow — labour availability, crane and weather are outside it ("availability of such resources [is] a challenge", Daniel). Failure symptom: an agent reporting "buildable in 6-day cycles" as if the site were a factory.

## Worked 21-floor programme — 250-key upscale hotel

Inputs are stamped from the registers above or declared; every output is *derived* and reproducible. Working days, 5-day week; no calendar, weather or consent time.

**Declared inputs.** 21 storeys: 3 podium (1,200 m² plate, 5.0 m floor-to-floor), 1 plant (800 m², 3.4 m), 14 guest (850 m², 3.4 m, 18 keys/floor = 252 ≈ 250 keys), 3 crown/leisure (650 m², 3.4 m). `GFA = 3×1,200 + 800 + 14×850 + 3×650 =` **18,250 m²** — a cross-check against, not a derivation of, the 17,000–18,000 m² gross band the HO worked example reports for this programme. Cycles: podium 9 d, plant 8 d, guest 6 d (register band 5–7 d; the 2–4 d rows are 400 m+ towers with a dedicated formwork contract, CP-03), crown 7 d; core lead 3 floors; envelope 3.5 m²/person/day (CP-16); fit-out 10 d/guest floor with 4 floors in parallel (declared — the only published finishing figure read is plastering at 7 d/floor, 21 workers, 1,666.9 m², from CP-12's IJSER source); handover 30 d; substructure is a planner input and is **not** in the number below.

**Learning ramp.** `T₁ = 8 d`, `S = 0.85` ⇒ `b = ln0.85/ln2 = −0.2345`, `Tₙ = 8·n^−0.2345` capped at the 6-day physical floor: 8.00, 6.80, 6.18, then 6.00 × 11 → guest run **87 d** vs a flat 84 d → learning premium **≈ 3 d for the tower** (CP-11: real, and small).

**Frame.** 3×9 = 27 podium · 8 plant · 87 guest · 3×7 = 21 crown = 143; + one guest core cycle (5 d) for the 3-floor lead at the top → **148 working days**.

**Envelope.** Perimeters from the plates (square-ish): guest √850 = 29.2 m → P = 116 m → ×3.4 = 394 m²; podium 139 × 5.0 = 695; plant 113 × 3.4 = 384; crown 102 × 3.4 = 347. `A_env = 14×394 + 3×695 + 384 + 3×347 =` **9,026 m²**. At 3.5 m²/person/day a 14-installer crew lays 49 m²/day → **184 days**, starting day 20 (frame 2 floors up, first units delivered) → **ends day 204**. Keeping pace with the 148-day frame needs 9,026 ÷ 128 = 70.5 m²/day = **21 installers**: a 7-head difference that decides the completion date (CP-16).

**Fit-out.** 10 d ÷ 4 fronts = 2.5 d per floor against a 6-day frame → never binds; it trails the envelope by one cycle → **+10 d**; handover **+30 d**.

**Result.** `total = max(148, 204) + 10 + 30 =` **244 working days ≈ 11 months** from first floor slab to handover; **≈ 424 working days ≈ 20 months** with the declared 180-day substructure. **Binding path: the envelope, not the structure.**

| Case | Cycle (guest) | Plate (guest) | Envelope crew | Frame days | Envelope ends | Total (wd) | Δ vs baseline |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline | 6 | 850 m² | 14 | 148 | 204 | **244** | — |
| −1 day cycle | 5 | 850 m² | 14 | 137 | 204 | 244 | **0** — no saving; the frame is not the path |
| +1 day cycle | 7 | 850 m² | 14 | 160 | 204 | 244 | **0** — no penalty yet; crossover ≈ 10-day cycles (frame 199 vs envelope 204) |
| Envelope resourced | 6 | 850 m² | 21 | 148 | 143 | 188 | **−56** — the largest single lever in the model |
| Plate −20% | 6 | 680 m² | 14 | 148 | 193 | 233 | −11 (guest perimeter √0.8 = 0.894 → −10.6 % on guest envelope, −6.2 % on the whole) |
| Plate +20% | 6 | 1,020 m² | 14 | 148 | 216 | 256 | +12 |
| Geometry drift (9 floor types, +1 d/floor) | 6→7 | 850 m² | 14 | 160 | 204 | 244 | 0 on date, **+≈280 formwork crew-days** (14 extra floor-days × a 20-hand gang) and one more formwork set: the cost lands in £/m² (EC-12), not in days, while the frame has slack |

Three findings for a designer: (1) the structural cycle carries **56 working days of slack** — the frame could run at 10-day cycles before it displaces the envelope, so most geometry decisions are free in date terms and expensive in cost terms; (2) plate size moves the date (±11/12 days per ±20 %) while a whole day of cycle moves nothing, because plate drives envelope area and perimeter together; (3) seven extra façade heads buy back 56 working days — more than any formwork system in the register above.

## Weak or contested

1. **No Tier-1 cycle-time standard exists.** Floor cycles are project data published by formwork vendors; every row of `## Cycle time table` is a success story from the supplier of the system that delivered it. Direction is trustworthy, absolute days are not. Mitigation: bands + tier stamps + the worked model's binding-path arithmetic on declared inputs rather than a borrowed day count. Most 2–4-day rows are 400 m+ supertalls with dedicated climbing contracts — applying one to a 21-floor hotel is the classic error (the worked case uses 6 days and shows the frame is not the constraint).
2. **The learning-curve evidence is the weakest here and is reported as such.** Two Indian college-journal studies fit 14-floor datasets and produce slopes of 34.77–78.94 % (implausible against their own 70–90 % band, internally inconsistent in interpretation) and, in the second, *no* learning in finishing trades — kept as the cautionary record (CP-12), not as rates. MCAA/AACE stacking percentages are the same class of problem: self-declared anecdote ("MCAA does not have in its possession any records indicating that a statistical or other type of empirical study was undertaken"), so the 10–30 % band is management folklore with a citation trail. Ottesen's five projects are mechanical/electrical/piping/steel works, not floor cycles, and he states the schedule is correlated with, not causal of, the loss.
3. **The 25–50 % reinforcement share is one vendor statement with no method**, corroborated only *directionally* by two project records that separate forming from tying to accelerate.

## Type-specificity audit

- **Bites hardest on repeated-cell towers** — hotel, residential, student housing, ward, PRS: the cycle arithmetic means something only once a floor repeats ≥ 6 times (CP-11's amortisation, CP-20's thresholds), which is the flagship case.
- **Offices:** the frame usually outruns fit-out and the tenant-improvement pipeline, so CP-16's envelope and the base-build/fit-out line (LC-20) govern the date; CP-09's takt applies to fit-out zones rather than floors, because deep plates mean the work face is not the constraint.
- **Hospitals:** the most schedule-hostile type here — few identical floors, dense services, and the takt check (CP-09/CP-18) fails by design, not by error. Apply CP-17's penalties to the *ward* stack only; the theatre/diagnostic block behaves like bespoke work.
- **Low-rise (≤ 4–6 storeys), bearing-wall and timber light-frame:** CP-03/CP-05/CP-06/CP-07 are largely inert — no core to lead, no jump, often no propping ladder, cycle set by trades and delivery. Keep CP-09/CP-10 (takt, zones) and CP-19/CP-20 (offsite demands, thresholds), drop the rest rather than bending them (the caution CN-23 carries in `construction.md`).
- **Single-storey, industrial, warehouse, podium decks:** the floor-repeat model does not apply, but CP-14's crew arithmetic and CP-22's logistics do — a warehouse is a takt problem on a plan, not a stack problem.
- **Heritage/retrofit:** none of these cycles exist; LC-17's "the existing building will cost more than you think" governs, and discovery risk swamps takt logic.
- **Regional caveat:** the cycle table is ~70 % MENA/Asia high-rise with a UK/US/EU minority; the crew register assumes two shifts and abundant general labour; the striking table is Indian. Northern-European and North-American single-shift practice runs slower per cycle and cheaper per hour — use this file to compare *ratios* (envelope vs frame days, prop levels vs cycle), never to import days.
