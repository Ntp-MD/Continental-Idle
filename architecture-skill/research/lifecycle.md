# Research file 15 — Building lifecycle (adaptation, durability, reuse, change of function)

Scope: what the future does to a plan, expressed as *static-geometry* rules. The host has no time
dimension, no section and no material classes, so every lifecycle statement here must be converted
into something checkable in a finished tile map: which constraints lock, which are cheap to move,
what spare capacity is left, and what assumption the agent is making about the successor use.
TH-28 (shearing layers: separate long-life from short-life decisions) is the seed this file grows
from — it is not restated. PP-17 (wet stack + slope budget), PP-18 (service band), PP-19
(replaceable objects), PP-23 (change-cost escalation) and PP-25 (POE loop) are the operational
hooks; the rules below extend them into a lifecycle framework rather than repeating them.

Host grid: 1 tile = 0.5 m; 4 tiles = 1 m²; wall = one blocked tile; rooms = flood-fill typed by
fixture tags; tile states only `walkable | blocked | door`; vertical = lift/stair portals; access by
tags; no heights, no loads, no section, no time. Derived: 2 tiles = 1 m; 14 tiles = 7 m;
27 tiles = 13.5 m; 28 tiles = 14 m; 12×18 tiles = a 6×9 m bay; 15×24 tiles = a 7.5×12 m bay.

Because rooms are typed by tags, **a change of function is a change of tags** — and it succeeds only
if the geometry already satisfies the new tag's requirements. That single fact is what makes
lifecycle reasoning possible on this host: the agent is not predicting the future, it is testing
whether today's walls still answer to tomorrow's tag list.

---

## Rules

### LC-01 Publish a service-life and replacement record for every element class before the plan is final
- Rule: For each element class in the design (structure, envelope, internal linings, wet cells, doors and hardware, vertical transport, plant, distribution services, finishes, fixtures/loose parts) state a nominal service life in years, the maintenance frequency the geometry assumes, and how that element is reached for replacement. No element class may be left with "n/a". A plan without this table is not a finished design, it is an untested guess.
- Evidence: The US Department of Defense Green Construction Code (IgCC-based, 2018) makes exactly this a submission requirement: a Service Life Plan documenting "Building assembly description. Materials or products. Design or estimated service life in years. Maintenance frequency. Maintenance access", applied to structural, envelope and hardscape assemblies (UpCodes reproduction of the code text). Eurocode EN 1990 assigns design working life by works category — 10 y temporary, 10–25 y "replaceable structural parts, e.g. gantry girders, bearings", 15–30 y agricultural, 50 y building structures, 100 y monumental (Concrete Society summary of EN 1990 Table 2.3; UK National Annex modifies categories 2, 3 and 5). ISO 15686 is the dedicated standard family for this, covering "the service life of a building component, building or other constructed work", with a part specifically for "Reference service life and service-life estimation", and service life planning defined as giving "reasonable assurance that the estimated service life of a new building on a specific site, with planned maintenance" meets the design. EN 15978 fixes a default "reference study period" of 50 years for whole-building assessment, which is the horizon over which replacement counts are computed.
- Source: IgCC/DoD Green Construction Code §1001.3.2.3 via https://up.codes/s/service-life-plan — T1; Concrete Society, *Design working life* https://www.concrete.org.uk/fingertips/design-working-life/ — T1 standard via T3 summary; ISO 15686 series https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_15686 — T3/T4 (clause text paywalled); EN 15978 modules and 50-year RSP via https://docs.realtimelca.com/methodology-and-compliance/en-15978 — T3 summary of T1.
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT
- Scope: universal; the element list shortens for single-storey and shed types, lengthens for healthcare/hospitality
- Confidence: High that the record is required and what fields it has; Low on any specific year value for non-structural elements (see Weak or contested)
- Grid translation: Emit a `service_life` table alongside the plan: `{element_class, nominal_life_y, replacement_access, maintenance_freq}`. Compute `replacement_count = ceil(50 / nominal_life_y)` per class (50 y = the EN 15978 study period) and assert: `replacement_count > 1` for every class ⇒ that class must have a proven access route and a *removable* boundary (LC-02, LC-16). Report `elements_with_life_unstated`, target 0.
- Exceptions / failure mode: The host cannot carry materials or thicknesses, so the table is an *annotation attached to tile sets*, not a derived property — an agent that skips it loses the only lifecycle artefact the grid can hold. Failure symptom: a plan where a lift, a damper or a floor waste cannot be reached without demolishing a `blocked` line that is also a structural line (LC-03).

### LC-02 Never trap a short-life element behind a long-life one
- Rule: Order every element by service life and check that replacement can be performed from the shorter-life side. Anything with a life materially shorter than the enclosing assembly (LC-01) must sit inside an access envelope that survives the enclosure — a door, a removable panel line, a service band, a shaft. If the only way to reach it is to break the long-life element, that is a design defect, not a detail to resolve later.
- Evidence: The IgCC service-life plan field list pairs "Design or estimated service life in years" with "Maintenance frequency" and "Maintenance access" as one obligation (UpCodes/T1) — access is documented *because* lives differ. EN 1990 explicitly separates "replaceable structural parts" (10–25 y) from the 50-year structure, i.e. the code itself assumes some parts cycle inside the frame (Concrete Society/T1-via-T3). "Long life, loose fit", formulated by Alex Gordon in 1972 and carried into high-tech practice, is defined operationally as flexible grids, generous heights, **accessible services** and no fixed internal walls (Lifschutz Davidson Sandilands/T3) — the accessible-services clause exists precisely to stop services being buried in the slab. PP-19 established the plan-level test for individual objects (removal path, access tile, test point); this rule generalises it to the whole ordering of layers, extending TH-28.
- Source: https://up.codes/s/service-life-plan — T1; https://www.concrete.org.uk/fingertips/design-working-life/ — T1/T3; https://lds-uk.com/idea/long-life-loose-fit/ — T3.
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: Tag each `blocked` line as `layer = support | envelope | subdivision | fixture`; assign the LC-01 life to each. Validation, per serviced object: `exists path P from a walkable tile to the object such that every blocked tile crossed has layer life ≥ object life`. Report `trapped_short_life` count; target 0. Practical proxy on this grid: any fixture that is not reachable from a corridor without crossing a `subdivision` line whose own life is *shorter* than the fixture's is a trap.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Buried waste pipes and embedded conduit genuinely cannot be surfaced anywhere in this model — record them as `unreachable_by_design` with a stated leak/inspection consequence rather than pretending the check passed. Symptom: a "flexible" plan where every future change requires breaking the same wall line.

### LC-03 Declare which wall lines are structure, on a host that does not model structure
- Rule: Because the host has no structural system, the agent must label every `blocked` line as `bearing`, `non-bearing` or `unknown` at design time, keep the label consistent across floors, and forbid itself from treating an unlabelled line as removable later. The structure label is the single most valuable lifecycle annotation the plan can carry, because it is the only thing that tells a future adapter what cannot move.
- Evidence: Reletting/adaptation practice is written in exactly these terms: steel-frame office guidance promotes "fully adaptable buildings" in which "all internal walls [can] be relocated", with long span used explicitly to give "more flexible use of space" and to "maximise the economic life of the building" (Steel Construction Info/T3). The residential-conversion evidence is that the *structure* is what kills conversions: "their layouts and structures will not lend well to repurposing" so "demolition will be necessary", and "removal of floor slabs may be necessary" for leisure uses (Chapman Taylor/T3). Conversely, documented office column grids (6×9 m, 7.5×12 m) exist so that floor plates stay divisible (Steel Construction Info/T3).
- Source: https://steelconstruction.info/sectors/multi-storey-office-buildings/ — T3; https://www.chapmantaylor.com/insights/repurposing-the-department-store — T3.
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT (self-imposed, since the host is silent)
- Scope: universal; multi-storey types most acute
- Confidence: High (the practice), Medium (the specific grids, single vendor-adjacent source)
- Grid translation: `bearing_lines = {blocked tiles}` with a rule: no future revision may convert a `bearing` tile to `walkable`/`door` without an explicit scheme-change declaration (ties to TH-28's "long-life revision = scheme change"). Metrics: `bearing_share` (target low, but nonzero), `max_clear_span_tiles` between bearing lines (report in metres), `span_consistency` across floors (identical bay lattice or flagged). A `bearing` label that appears on one floor and not the next at the same grid position is an error.
- Exceptions / failure mode: A plan that labels everything bearing is as useless as one that labels nothing (no future change is possible on paper); a plan that labels nothing bears no cost until the first conversion, when everything turns out to be load-bearing. Walls the agent has *guessed* bearing must be reported as `unknown` — do not silently promote unknown to non-bearing.

### LC-04 Cap the floor plate at the depth the *successor* use can tolerate, not the current one
- Rule: Choose the plate depth from the most demanding plausible successor use, and record the margin. Office-daylit practice caps a single-aspect plate near 13.5 m for natural ventilation; residential comfort caps a single-aspect room near 7 m and a double-aspect plate near 14 m. A plate deeper than the successor-use cap is not flexible — it is a one-use building with a facade.
- Evidence: Steel office guidance limits "floor plate … to around 13.5 m" where natural ventilation is intended, with cores "located centrally" (Steel Construction Info/T3). Retail-to-other-use practice gives the residential screening thresholds directly: problems begin above "a single-aspect floor depth of over 7m, or double-aspect of over 14m", against a target "free height of at least 2.7m" and residential module widths "from the length of a bed (2-2.5m) to a more reasonable 4-4.5m"; back-of-house "may constitute up to 20%" (Chapman Taylor/T3 — practitioner judgement, not measured). The legal consequence in England is a *test*, not a preference: change-of-use to dwellings under Class MA is subject to prior approval on "adequate natural light in all habitable rooms" (GPDO 2015 Sch. 2 Pt 3 Class MA, as inserted by SI 2021/428/T1).
- Source: https://steelconstruction.info/sectors/multi-storey-office-buildings/ — T3; https://www.chapmantaylor.com/insights/repurposing-the-department-store — T3; https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2015/596/schedule/2/part/3 — T1.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE (thresholds are heuristic screening values; the per-room light test that enforces them in England is in LC-21)
- Scope: offices, retail, hotels, large public buildings; weak for industrial, hospitals (deep plan-lit wards are a deliberate exception), and any below-grade volume
- Confidence: High that depth is the binding geometric constraint (and that it is a legal test in England); Medium on 7 m / 13.5 m / 14 m as transferable numbers
- Grid translation: Per floor compute `max_depth_from_glazing_tiles` in tiles over every habitable tile set (BFS from façade tiles through open plan, blocked tiles stop it): single aspect ≤ 14 tiles, double aspect ≤ 28 tiles, current-use office target ≤ 27 tiles. Emit `over_depth_habitable_tiles` and `second_use_survivable = (all future-habitable tiles ≤ 14 tiles from a façade)`. Record the plate as a claim with its number: "plate = 24 tiles deep; supports office now, residential later (≤ 28 double-aspect), not single-aspect residential."
- Exceptions / failure mode: Atrium, gallery, terminal hall and theatre volumes legitimately exceed the caps and must be reclassified as non-habitable (TH-26 does the same for daylight). Failure symptom: an agent claims "adaptable to residential" while every interior tile is a corridor and no bedroom can be placed within the light reach.

### LC-05 Draw a plumbing-radius around every wet core and publish what falls outside it
- Rule: For every stack/shaft, compute the horizontal radius within which a waste fixture can sit given the pipe fall you have assumed and the floor build-up you are willing to spend. Fixtures outside the radius need pumped or vacuum drainage, a new shaft, or a section resolution — all of which are *future-expensive*. Say the radius out loud as a number of tiles.
- Evidence: Gravity is the governing constraint (PP-17): UK drainage guidance reproducing Approved Document H gives a 100 mm WC branch a minimum fall of 1:80 and a recommended 1:40, a 150 mm branch a minimum of 1:150, and 75 mm as "not permitted" for WC discharge (Rospower quick reference/T4 quoting T1). Arithmetic on this grid at 1:80: each tile (0.5 m) of run drops the pipe 6.25 mm, so an assumed 150 mm of build-up buys 24 tiles (12 m) of run; at the recommended 1:40 the same budget buys 12 tiles (6 m). Ellen & Kazis's New York conversion model treats the fixed position of risers and cores as a first-order determinant of which floors can convert (Federal Reserve Bank of New York staff report/T1-T2).
- Source: https://rospower.co.uk/resources/drainage-gradients/ — T4 (quoting Approved Document H, T1); https://fedinprint.org/item/fednep/97155 — T1/T2.
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT
- Scope: universal where water is reticulated
- Confidence: High for the fall ratios and the arithmetic; the build-up allowance is an assumption the agent must state
- Grid translation: Emit `shaft` columns (as PP-17) and, per shaft, `wet_radius_tiles = floor(D_allow_mm / (500/fall_denominator))` with `D_allow_mm` declared as an assumption (default 150 mm at 1:80 ⇒ 24 tiles). Validation: every existing wet fixture inside its radius; report `fixtures_outside_wet_radius`; then report `future_wet_positions_available = tiles inside radius that are currently non-wet and can become wet without crossing a bearing line` — that count *is* the plan's wet-adaptability, and a value of zero means the floor can never take a kitchen or bathroom.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Macerator/pumped drainage genuinely liberates remote fixtures at the cost of mechanical failure and noise (real practice, unquantified here). Top-floor and single-storey plans have more build-up freedom than mid-floor plates; ground floors can go to below-slab drainage. Symptom: a "flexible" office floor with 2 wet tiles of capacity and 400 tiles of usable area.

### LC-06 Provision the load and riser reserve for the successor use, and record it as an annotation
- Rule: Check the use-change in two directions. Imposed load: residential is *lower* than office in the load tables, so conversion is normally governed by added permanent load (new partitions, wet-cell screed, acoustic floating floor, baths) and by concentrated loads, not by the live-load category. Services: successor uses add risers (soil, vent, hot water, extraction) the current scheme may not have capacity for. Both reserves must be stated as design assumptions, since the host models neither.
- Evidence: EN 1991-1-1 floor categories as reproduced in engineering teaching material: domestic/residential 1.5, office 2.0, assembly 3.5–5.0, storage 5.0–7.5, warehouse 15.0 kN/m², with a 1.0 kN/m² allowance modelled for movable partitions (Structville/T4; note the standard's category B is elsewhere quoted as 2.5 — treat the exact value as unverified, the ordering as reliable). Movable-partition allowances exist because plans are expected to change. Soft-landings practice adds the complementary obligation: performance targets are tracked into the aftercare period, when the load/servicing assumptions are tested against use (isurv summary of the BSRIA framework/T3, BS 8536).
- Source: https://structville.com/live-loads-imposed-loads-on-floors — T4 (secondary reproduction of EN 1991-1-1 Table 6.1); https://www.isurv.com/info/390/features_archive/11897/soft_landings_framework_the_principles — T3 (BSRIA/BS 8536 framework summary).
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT (proxied)
- Scope: multi-storey, all types with upper floors; industrial governed by point loads and crane/vantry needs
- Confidence: Medium (secondary reproduction of the load table); High on the principle that partition and build-up mass, not occupancy, drives conversion structure
- Grid translation: Per floor emit `assumed_imposed_kNm2`, `assumed_partition_allowance_kNm2`, `added_dead_load_budget_if_retyped` as declared assumptions, plus the host-computable proxy `riser_spare_tiles` = tiles inside the shaft column not currently occupied by a pipe/duct symbol, and `new_risers_required` for the successor tag set (kitchen ⇒ soil + vent + extraction; laundry ⇒ soil + supply). Validation: `new_risers_required ⊆ available_shaft_tiles` on every floor between the change and the outlet level. A shaft with zero spare tiles has no adaptation path (LC-09).
- Exceptions / failure mode: Heavy successors (archive, library stacks, data halls, commercial kitchens, laboratories, retail warehouse racking) exceed both office and residential values — the reserve is only sized against the *declared* successor, never against "anything". Symptom: the plan claims adaptability while no riser can be extended and no wet zone can be added anywhere.

### LC-07 Write the second use into the programme and test the plan against it
- Rule: For each zone, name at least one alternative room tag the geometry genuinely supports, and re-run the room's requirement set for that tag (light reach, wet radius, door/access rules, adjacency grade, min dimension). "Flexible" is only an admissible claim when it is a list of tested alternatives; otherwise write "single-use" and stop implying otherwise.
- Evidence: The adaptation literature's operative distinction is *function change* itself: adaptive reuse and conversion are separated from refurbishment precisely "by a change in a building's function or use", where refurbishment improves conditions "for the _existing_ use" (Shahi et al., Sustainable Cities and Society 2020, open access/T2). The consequence of not defining these crisply is financial, not semantic: the paper's stated purpose is "avoiding the high costs arising from codes, specifications, and project descriptions that confuse these definitions". England's change-of-use regime enforces the same test per room (Class MA adequate-natural-light prior approval, GPDO/T1), and New York's conversion economics are gated by which buildings can physically take a residential section at all (Ellen & Kazis/T1-T2).
- Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7326450/ — T2; https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2015/596/schedule/2/part/3 — T1; https://fedinprint.org/item/fednep/97155 — T1/T2.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal; weakest for single-function types (tank farm, car park, plant house)
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: Extend the TH-01 programme table with `alt_tags[]` per zone, then run the validator twice: once with the primary tag set, once with each alternative substituted. Report `alt_tags_supported / alt_tags_claimed` per floor. Any claimed alternative that fails validation must be removed from the claim, not quietly ignored. This is the *only* honest way to express adaptability on a host with no time dimension.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Substituting one identical-tag cell for another is not adaptability (that is repetition), and an agent listing "office / residential / retail" for every zone is performing the claim rather than testing it. Symptom: a plan billed as mixed-use-capable whose only circulation is a single 2-tile corridor that no residential layout would accept as a escape-and-daylight solution.

### LC-08 Make the daylight metric survivable by the successor use, and count the unlit share
- Rule: Compute glazing-to-floor per room and the share of plate area that can never receive light under any subdivision. Deep plates convert the problem from a comfort issue into a viability issue: rooms that fail light cannot be rented, sold, or — in England — approved as dwellings.
- Evidence: The English test is per habitable room, not per floor (Class MA prior approval on adequate natural light, T1). BRE-derived daylight guidance is non-statutory but is the reference planners use, with the vertical sky component threshold (VSC ≥ 27% at the centre of the lowest habitable-room window) and the annual probable sunlight hours tests (25%/5% by orientation) as its working criteria (Anstey Horne's summary of BR 209, 3rd ed. 2022/T3-T4; secondary source, single-verified). Retail-conversion practice quotes the residential depth caps LC-04 uses as its screening rule (Chapman Taylor/T3). On the demand side, occupancy density is the number that sets how much of the plate must be lighted at all: 1 person per 10–15 m² in office guidance (Steel Construction Info/T3).
- Source: https://www.ansteyhorne.co.uk/news/daylight-and-sunlight-bre-guidelines — T3/T4 (BR 209 secondary); https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2015/596/schedule/2/part/3 — T1; https://steelconstruction.info/sectors/multi-storey-office-buildings/ — T3.
- Class: STANDARD (BRE BR 209 is non-statutory guidance; the statutory limb is the Class MA prior-approval test recorded in LC-21)
- Scope: residential, education, healthcare, office, hospitality guest areas; void for plant, retail-back, storage
- Confidence: High on the constraint's existence and per-room character; Medium on the VSC 27% number (not read from BR 209 itself)
- Grid translation: Define `glazed` façade tiles (a subclass of the façade line the agent must maintain — report it if absent). Emit per room `glazing_ratio = glazed_tiles / (perimeter_tiles)` and `daylight_reach_tiles`; per floor `unlit_core_share = tiles > 14 from any glazed tile / habitable_tiles`. Validation: `unlit_core_share ≤ declared budget` (0.10–0.15 typical for a plate intended to convert), and every tile proposed as a future habitable room lies within reach. Report the number, not the adjective.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Top-lit atria, light wells and reflected-light courts can rescue interiors but cannot be modelled here — flag as "requires section/rendering". A genuinely dark-successor programme (data centre, archive, studio, hospital imaging) inverts the metric; declare it rather than leaving the check failing. Symptom: an agent that reports "daylit on three elevations" while 40% of the plate has no legal residential future.

### LC-09 Buy flexibility where it compounds: the vertical line and the façade module
- Rule: Two decisions govern nearly all future change and both must be made once, correctly: the fixed vertical line (structure bay + shaft + portal stack, aligned floor after floor) and the repeatable horizontal module (façade bay, leasehold subdivision unit). Everything else is negotiable later at modest cost; these two are not.
- Evidence: TH-28 established cross-floor alignment as the long-life obligation. Practice repeats it as a cost-and-life argument: office systems are made "fully adaptable" by keeping internal walls relocatable and the core in a position that serves both escape and servicing, so that flexibility "maximise[s] the economic life of the building" (Steel Construction Info/T3). Documented grids (6×9 m, 7.5×12 m) are module decisions; the residential module widths of 2–2.5 m to 4–4.5 m quoted by retail converters are the same decision seen from the successor use (Chapman Taylor/T3). ISO 55001 practice makes the identical point in asset-management language: strategy must have "line of sight" from board objectives down to the component, which is only possible if the component is a repeatable unit (Glocert guide/T4).
- Source: https://steelconstruction.info/sectors/multi-storey-office-buildings/ — T3; https://www.chapmantaylor.com/insights/repurposing-the-department-store — T3; https://www.glocertinternational.com/resources/guides/asset-management-policy-samp-and-asset-management-plans-iso-55001/ — T4.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal for multi-storey; module still governs single-storey shells
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: Report `bay_module_tiles` (the repeating structural/façade interval), assert it divides every façade run without remainder, and assert `shaft_and_portal_positions` identical across floors (or the deviation is a logged scheme change). Emit `subdivision_points = façade tiles where a party line can sit on the module`, and `units_possible = plate_tiles / module_area` for both merge and split. A façade whose module cannot be expressed in whole tiles (e.g. a 4.3 m bay) is a defect of the design, not of the grid.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Deliberately irregular, signature or site-driven plans may have no module — then state `module = none; adaptation cost = high` instead of inventing one. Symptom: floors whose shaft sits two tiles off the floor below, so no riser and no portal can be aligned (TH-28's failure, now with a lifecycle price tag).

### LC-10 Keep the change-cost gradient visible: cheap changes are tile-state flips, expensive ones move a line
- Rule: Rank every contemplated future change by what it costs in the geometry, and design so the common changes are the cheap ones. Cheapest: change a fixture tag (no work). Then: open a door in an existing non-bearing line; close a door; move a non-bearing line within a bay. Dearest: move a shaft or portal line, alter the façade, break a bearing line, change the escape strategy.
- Evidence: This ordering is the direct consequence of the host's state machine (`walkable | blocked | door` — a partition change is a tile-state edit; a shaft change invalidates every floor above and below) plus the layer discipline in TH-28 and the cost-escalation evidence in PP-23 (rework ~5% of project cost, 2–20% by type, and "coordination clashes found on site cost 10 to 50 times more than clashes found in design", CCC Engineering/T3; multipliers are industry-reported, order-of-magnitude only). The same gradient shows up in the adaptation literature as the distinction between refurbishment (same use) and conversion (function change) with their different works content (Shahi et al./T2).
- Source: https://cccengineering.com.au/design-memos/services-coordination-ceiling-voids/ — T3; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7326450/ — T2.
- Class: HEURISTIC (the cost ordering; the tile-state mechanics behind it are arithmetic)
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High for the ordering, Low for any specific multiplier
- Grid translation: Maintain a `change_cost` class per plan element: `tag | door | non_bearing_line | bearing_line | shaft | facade | core`. Emit, per floor, the count of tiles in each class and the derived metric `cheap_change_share = (tag+door+non_bearing tiles) / (all modifiable tiles)`. Validation for the claimed alternatives in LC-07: the change from primary to alternative tag must be expressible using only the first three classes, or it is not "flexible", it is "a refurbishment".
- Exceptions / failure mode: Some changes look cheap in plan and are not in reality (a door into a stair enclosure, a new opening in a compartment wall, a WC added under a stack that has no vent). Where the host cannot see the reason, annotate `cost_hidden: compartmentation/egress/vent` rather than downgrading the class. Symptom: an agent that computes adaptability from wall counts and concludes a 1-tile-thick everything is trivially reconfigurable.

### LC-11 Argue retain-first, and make any demolition option clear an explicit payback test
- Rule: Default to the existing building or the retained frame, and treat demolition as needing to *prove* it beats retention on carbon, cost and waste. If a new-build option is proposed, state the extra upfront emissions, the operational saving rate, and the payback horizon; if that horizon is long, the option is rejected on the stated rule, not on preference.
- Evidence: Peer-reviewed LCA work makes the comparison rule explicit: renovation "is more climate-friendly than a new building" in the studied cases unless the new construction's emissions can be paid back on the order of "circa 10 years" (Huuhka, Moisio, Salmio, Köliö & Lahdensivu, *Buildings & Cities* 4, 2023, DOI 10.5334/bc.309/T2 — the authors also warn that "decades-long payback times … are not helpful" as a decision metric and that the choice should be framed "neutrally" at the planning stage). Statutory planning in London already imposes the retain-first logic: Policy SI 7 requires referable applications to submit a Circular Economy Statement demonstrating, among other things, material re-use and "disassembly for future use", against 95% reuse/recycling/recovery and zero biodegradable or recyclable construction waste to landfill by 2026 (London Plan 2021/T1 as reproduced on planningpolicy.co.uk). The EU waste hierarchy (Directive 2008/98/EC) ranks prepare-for-reuse above recycling above recovery above disposal, and UNEP's building-materials guidance states the same priority in design form: "Avoid-Shift-Improve", where *avoid* begins with not extracting new material — i.e. reusing buildings (GlobalABC/T2-T3). Retained structure is where the carbon sits: firm research summarised for practice states that "structure holds most" of a building's embodied carbon and that reuse "usually ha[s] … much lower total carbon emissions" than new build (LMN Architects/T3 — secondary; the underlying datasets cited are AIA, EPIC, CARE, EC3, Architecture 2030).
- Source: https://journal-buildingscities.org/articles/10.5334/bc.309 — T2; https://www.planningpolicy.co.uk/policy/london-plan/si-7 — T1 (reproduced); https://environment.ec.europa.eu/topics/waste-and-recycling/waste-framework-directive_en — T1 (landing page; article text not machine-readable this pass); https://globalabc.org/buildingmaterialsandclimate/chapte-2-life-cycle-thinking/2-6-strategies-towards-a-building-materials-revolution-avoid-shift-improve.html — T2/T3; https://lmnarchitects.com/lmn-research/10-existing-building-reuse — T3.
- Class: STANDARD (the retain-first and waste-hierarchy logic is codified as policy in some jurisdictions — e.g. London Plan SI 7 — and as an assessment standard generally)
- Scope: universal for renovation and extension projects; universal in jurisdictions with demolition-reuse policies
- Confidence: High on the direction and on the existence of the policy tests; Medium on the "circa 10 years" threshold as a transferable number; the widely quoted "reuse saves X% of carbon" family of percentages could not be verified this pass (see Weak or contested)
- Grid translation: Before generating a new form, run `retain_first_check`: (1) is there an existing tile set (in brief, site or inherited plan)? (2) if a new build is proposed, emit `demolition_case` with the retained-element list that was *not* used and the reason. (3) emit `spare_structure_carbon_claim` — never as a percentage unless a specific dataset is named; instead as "retained: floors 1–6 frame and envelope; not retained: internal linings, finishes, vertical transport" with the LC-01 table attached. (4) Report `elements_reused / elements_replaced` by count of tile-sets.
- Exceptions / failure mode: A poor-quality existing frame (contamination, insufficient clear height, no spare capacity, wrong grid, defective foundations) can genuinely lose the argument — the rule is to run the test, not to always answer "retain". Failure symptom: an agent that reports "adaptive reuse" while replacing every element except the walls.

### LC-12 Claim design-for-disassembly only with the evidence grade attached, and only where someone will pay for it
- Rule: Treat "designed for disassembly" as a specific, costed commitment on named connections and a named future actor, not as an adjective. State what will be recovered, by whom, with what documentation, and what the premium is; if any of those four are unknown, downgrade the claim to "reversible construction" or drop it.
- Evidence: A systematic review of 21 policy-oriented papers finds the DfD evidence base thin and the market barriers structural: "A limited empirical basis for many proposed measures shows the need for stronger evidence on policy effectiveness", and "major barriers still limit its uptake, including inadequate legislation, a paucity of financial incentives"; it identifies seven instrument families (law updates, awareness, certification, producer responsibility, municipal action, fiscal tools, collaboration) but explicitly "does not yet provide a strong empirical basis for ranking policy instruments by effectiveness"; the binding examples it records are legislative rather than design-led (Italian rules requiring a 50% reusable-component design threshold, a California statute mandating 70% construction-and-demolition waste recycling), and DfD costs are noted as potentially higher (Vorobjev, Uotila, Joensuu & Saari, *Buildings & Cities* 7(1), 2026/T2). The one place the claim becomes enforceable is where a policy demands the document — London Plan SI 7's Circular Economy Statement requires disassembly-for-future-use to be demonstrated (T1).
- Source: https://journal-buildingscities.org/articles/10.5334/bc.722 — T2; https://www.planningpolicy.co.uk/policy/london-plan/si-7 — T1.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE (claim discipline) — with the supporting evidence graded as weak
- Scope: reversible/lease-driven/short-life types first: retail fit-out, exhibition, education, temporary and modular; weakest for deep-basement, cast-in-situ and high-durability public buildings
- Confidence: High that the evidence is weak; High that documentation obligations are real where policy exists
- Grid translation: Any `dismantlable` annotation must carry: `actor`, `horizon_y`, `connections_specified (bool)`, `documentation (bool)`, `premium_tiles` (the plan area the reversibility costs — e.g. a service band you cannot build out, LC-09/LC-13). Validation: an element may be counted as reusable only if it is bounded entirely by lines the change-cost class in LC-10 puts in the first three tiers; anything crossing a `bearing` or `facade` line is not reusable, it is demolishable. Report `claimed_reusable_elements_without_named_actor`, target 0.
- Exceptions / failure mode: The common failure is the reverse: designing mechanical, bolted connections whose penalties (robustness, airtightness, acoustic separation, fire stopping) are paid immediately for a benefit nobody collects. Where the brief has no reuse policy, no deconstructor and no second life in prospect, the honest answer is "not designed for disassembly, designed for durability" (LC-16, LC-17).

### LC-13 Reserve capacity you cannot see: services spare, in plan
- Rule: Because the host has no section, every service-capacity decision must be expressed as spare *plan area* and a spare *route*: keep the service band continuous, keep riser tiles unoccupied, keep a reachable plant zone with a removal path, and never let a room consume the last band tile. Future function change adds services before it adds structure.
- Evidence: PP-18 established the band's existence from a costed MEP memo (void stack-up; "+100 mm of void ≈ $50–80/m² of facade and structure versus $100–200/m² of rework if too tight"; "late changes to floor-to-floor height affect every level, every facade panel, and every stair flight", CCC Engineering/T3). The lifecycle extension: the band must be sized for the *successor*'s services, not today's — a residential successor needs soil, vent, hot water and extraction that an office floor does not have, and hotel practice warns that cutting capital expenditure by "reducing ceiling heights or eliminating service redundancy … permanently destroys future repositioning potential" (Hotel Development Guide/T3). IgCC's "Maintenance access" field is the same obligation from the operator's side (T1).
- Source: https://cccengineering.com.au/design-memos/services-coordination-ceiling-voids/ — T3; https://hoteldevelopmentguide.com/architectural-planning/ — T3; https://up.codes/s/service-life-plan — T1.
- Class: ENGINEERING CONSTRAINT (proxied in plan)
- Scope: universal; acute in healthcare, hospitality, laboratories and any deep plate
- Confidence: Medium-High (the money and millimetre figures are single-source estimates)
- Grid translation: Extend PP-18's `service_band`: report `band_spent_tiles / band_total_tiles` per floor and per branch, and require `band_spare ≥ 30%` where the brief claims a function-change option (LC-07), ≥ 15% otherwise. Report `riser_spare_tiles` per shaft (LC-06) and `plant_removal_path_intact (bool)` per LC-02/PP-19. A floor at 100% band utilisation is a floor that can never change use, whatever the claim in the programme says.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Small buildings genuinely share one route for everything; then say `capacity = zero spare` and drop the adaptability claim. Symptom: a "flexible" floor whose only unassigned tiles are circulation, and whose successor tag set needs a duct that has nowhere to go.

### LC-14 Give every replaceable thing a life shorter than the thing that encloses it *or* make it an object of the enclosure
- Rule: Where a short-life object must sit inside a long-life assembly (a lift in a shaft, a pipe in a slab, a window in a façade), either provide a maintenance route and a replacement aperture, or re-classify the object as permanent and price its replacement into the assembly. Ambiguity here is what produces the 20-year-old "non-replaceable" item.
- Evidence: This is the operational reading of the IgCC requirement to state service life, maintenance frequency and maintenance access as a single obligation per assembly (T1) and of the durability categories in EN 1990, where replaceable parts (10–25 y) and the 50-year building frame are separated by design assumption (T1 via T3). ISO 15686 service-life planning is defined against a life achieved "with planned maintenance" (T3/T4), i.e. the maintenance regime is part of the design assumption, not an operating afterthought. Soft Landings institutionalises the same link across the handover boundary: the design, construction and aftercare phases (six of them, with post-occupancy evaluation continuing past 12 months) are run by the team that specified the components (isurv/BSRIA, 2018/T3, with BS 8536).
- Source: https://up.codes/s/service-life-plan — T1; https://www.concrete.org.uk/fingertips/design-working-life/ — T1/T3; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_15686 — T3/T4; https://www.isurv.com/info/390/features_archive/11897/soft_landings_framework_the_principles — T3.
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: universal; most consequential for lifts, plant, façades and embedded drainage
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: For each element class in LC-01 with `replacement_count > 1`, require `access_route` (LC-02 test) AND `aperture_proven`: on this grid, the object's bounding rectangle must be passable through the access door run — assert `object_size_tiles ≤ door_run_tiles` and `route_clear_of_bearing`. Report `elements_with_count_gt1_and_no_aperture`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Whole-assembly replacement (a re-roof, a re-clad façade, a re-fit floor) is legitimate and often cheaper than component access; declare the object as `replaced_as_part_of_assembly = true`. Symptom: a machine-room plan whose only door is 2 tiles wide and whose chiller is 4 tiles long.

### LC-15 Design the maintenance regime the plan assumes, and design for the failures that regime produces
- Rule: State who services what, how often, and along which route — then test whether the plan still works the week the regime is skipped. Maintenance assumptions are lifecycle decisions with geometric consequences: an assumed annual inspection route that crosses a tenant's rooms is not a route, it is a dispute.
- Evidence: ISO 15686 makes planned maintenance an explicit condition of achieving estimated service life (T3/T4), and IgCC makes "Maintenance frequency" and "Maintenance access" code-documented fields (T1). Empirically, operation dominates: a systematic review of life-cycle costing for upgrading existing buildings (47 Scopus papers, 2009–2024) reports that the "operation stage has highest cost" (Huang 2018 as synthesised in the review), several studies find simple paybacks beyond 20 years, and salvage/residual value is often omitted from the models (PMC/T2). The barrier review of 1,402 Web-of-Science papers attributes renovation under-performance to financial, regulatory and organisational causes including "risk aversion and litigation culture" (Liao, Ren & Li 2023/T2) — i.e. maintainability failures are as much contractual as physical.
- Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12425850/ — T2; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10001863/ — T2; https://up.codes/s/service-life-plan — T1; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_15686 — T3/T4.
- Class: STANDARD
- Scope: universal; the multi-tenancy version binds hardest in commercial, retail and mixed
- Confidence: High on direction; Medium on magnitudes — class kept: the `assumed` in the rule names the design's own maintenance assumptions, which are the rule's subject, not a gap in its citation
- Grid translation: Emit a `maintenance_route` set: walkable tiles that must remain permanently reachable, tagged as such, with `crosses_tenancy = bool` per segment. Validation: `no maintenance route crosses a tenancy boundary`, or the crossing is declared with an access tag (host has tags, so this is enforceable); `route_survives_fitout`: the route must not pass through any tile whose tag set is in the alternative-use lists (LC-07). Report `routes_blocked_by_lettable_area`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Some regimes genuinely need tenant entry (in-unit inspection, meter replacement); mark them `entry_required` with the notice obligation, rather than deleting the route. Symptom: the plan's only riser access is through a leasehold room — the shaft becomes non-maintainable the day it is let.

### LC-16 Trade durability against maintainability deliberately, element by element
- Rule: For each element class, choose one of three postures and record it: (a) hard — survives without intervention for the whole horizon; (b) soft — cheap to replace on a proven route; (c) monitored — inspected and repaired on a stated cycle. Never default to "hard everywhere": capital is spent, the replacement route is never built, and when the element finally fails the only option is demolition.
- Evidence: EN 1990's category structure is an explicit durability choice at the top of the list (10 y to 100 y by works type), and ISO 15686's whole purpose is to make durability a *specified* performance rather than an aspiration, with reference service life estimated under a maintenance scenario (T1 standard, T3/T4 access). Renovation-cost evidence supports the "soft and reachable" side: the LCC review finds operation-stage cost dominant and paybacks long (T2), which penalises capital-heavy durability bought without an access strategy; the barriers review finds households and owners cannot fund the works even where needed (T2), which penalises any strategy whose maintenance depends on capital injections. The IgCC fields (life, frequency, access) are exactly the three postures, required per assembly (T1).
- Source: https://up.codes/s/service-life-plan — T1; https://www.concrete.org.uk/fingertips/design-working-life/ — T1/T3; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_15686 — T3/T4; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12425850/ — T2.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: Add `posture = hard | soft | monitored` to each LC-01 row and require `soft ⇒ access route proven` (LC-02) and `monitored ⇒ test point in a walkable tile` (PP-19). Report `posture_mix_share` per floor and flag any floor where >80% of annotated elements are `hard` — the statistically common outcome is that the hard elements were never actually specified, they were just never thought about.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Structural frame, foundations, roof membrane above a hard ceiling and anything cast into the ground are legitimately `hard` because there is no alternative; say so and set `no_alternative = true`. Symptom: an agent using "durability" as a way of avoiding the maintenance-access decision.

### LC-17 Assume the existing building will cost more than you think, and put the uncertainty in the design, not the tender
- Rule: When designing against or inside an existing fabric, carry an explicit allowance for unknown conditions, prefer interventions that do not depend on unverified assumptions about the existing structure, and sequence the works so the cheap investigation happens before the irreversible one. On new builds, the equivalent rule is: do not add a second basement-level constraint you cannot yet prove.
- Evidence: The evidence is consistent across two independent syntheses. A review of life-cycle cost estimation for upgrading existing buildings (47 papers, Scopus 2009–2024) records payback periods commonly exceeding 20 years and the systematic omission of salvage/residual value from the models (T2). A 1,402-paper review of renovation barriers records financial (owners "cannot finance necessary renovations"), regulatory ("government lacks political will") and technical/organisational drivers, and identifies occupant-behaviour uncertainty as the reason measured savings diverge from predicted (T2). England's prior-approval regime is itself a conditional-cost driver: conversion may be refused on adequate natural light in all habitable rooms, and requires the building to have been in the qualifying class for two years and not listed (Class MA, T1).
- Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12425850/ — T2; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10001863/ — T2; https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2015/596/schedule/2/part/3 — T1.
- Class: HEURISTIC
- Scope: universal for renovation, adaptation and extension; not applicable to undifferentiated greenfield design
- Confidence: High on direction; Low on any percentage (no overrun figure could be retrieved this pass)
- Grid translation: Classify every design dependency as `verified | assumed`, and count `irreversible_decisions_depending_on_assumptions`. Emit a two-stage work sequence with the investigation gate: no `bearing_line` change, `shaft` extension or `facade` opening may appear in the plan before the assumption it rests on is marked verified. Report `assumption_exposure = assumed_dependencies / total_dependencies`; > 0.3 on a conversion plan is a stop-and-flag condition.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Over-investigation is also a cost, and a fully designed new building has near-zero existing-condition exposure — the rule is proportionate to how much existing fabric you are trusting. Symptom: the conversion scheme that discovers, after the party walls are set, that the frame has no spare riser (LC-06) and half the rooms fail light (LC-08).

### LC-18 Keep phases self-sufficient, and never make phase 2 depend on phase 1 being finished in one specific way
- Rule: Where growth is expected, design each phase to close its own loop: its own entry, its own vertical circulation, its own riser stub, its own escape route and its own plant connection point. The interface between phases is the highest-risk geometry in the plan and must be drawn as a designed line, not left as a gap.
- Evidence: Long-range institutional planning practice is built on exactly this: a master plan "allows a phased approach" while the "end goal is fixed in place", and the advice is to prioritise "multi-use spaces" early so later phases can absorb needs nobody has yet specified (Tonn & Blank/T4 — practitioner, no numbers). The asset-management frame says the same in requirement language: ISO 55001 expects a Strategic Asset Management Plan and asset management plans that keep objectives and capability in "line of sight", which is only auditable if the increments are defined (Glocert/T4). On the fabric side, the change-cost gradient (LC-10) says a phase boundary made of non-bearing subdivision is cheap and one made of core and façade is not.
- Source: https://www.tonnandblank.com/long-term-construction-strategies-for-expanding-higher-education-campuses/ — T4; https://www.glocertinternational.com/resources/guides/asset-management-policy-samp-and-asset-management-plans-iso-55001/ — T4.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: campuses, hospitals, hotels in resort/expansion mode, large public buildings, industrial, stations, multi-let commercial
- Confidence: Medium (T4 practice sources; the ISO anchor is real, the guide is a consultancy summary)
- Grid translation: For each declared phase boundary, run `phase_self_sufficiency`: phase 1 alone must have ≥1 public access from site, ≥1 vertical portal, ≥1 shaft connection, a legal escape for every occupied tile, and no walkable tile whose only route passes through phase 2. Emit `orphan_tiles_if_phase2_not_built` (must be 0) and `phase_boundary_class` (which change-cost tier, LC-10). Draw the boundary as `blocked` tiles marked `future_interface`, never as an absence of wall.
- Exceptions / failure mode: A single-phase building should declare `phasing = none` rather than carrying empty spare land and dead corridors. Symptom: an existing wing whose only fire exit runs through the unbuilt extension, or a plant room sized for phase 2 that phase 1 cannot afford.

### LC-19 Keep subdivision and merger both legal on every floor
- Rule: Plan so a large tenant can be split into small ones and small ones merged into a large one, with the split lines on the module, each resulting unit holding its own light, access and (where wet) its own stack tie. A floor that can only be let whole is a floor with one tenant and no future.
- Evidence: Retail/department-store conversion practice reasons in modules — widths "from the length of a bed (2-2.5m) to a more reasonable 4-4.5m" — because that is what makes a big plate habitable in small units (Chapman Taylor/T3). Office guidance makes the same property the source of marketability: long span plus relocatable internal walls plus a core positioned to serve both escape and lettings, so the plate can be re-sliced without touching anything permanent (Steel Construction Info/T3). The US stock profile shows why it matters commercially: the overwhelming majority of commercial buildings are small enough that they must remain lettable in fragments (LMN Architects, reporting that 86% of US commercial stock is under 25,000 sf/T3).
- Source: https://www.chapmantaylor.com/insights/repurposing-the-department-store — T3; https://steelconstruction.info/sectors/multi-storey-office-buildings/ — T3; https://lmnarchitects.com/lmn-research/10-existing-building-reuse — T3.
- Class: BUILDING-TYPE CONVENTION
- Scope: commercial, retail, hotels (room combination), education (suite merging), industrial (unit subletting); weak for hospitals and single-operator public buildings
- Confidence: Medium-High
- Grid translation: Test at `module/2` and `module×2`: for each candidate split line on the module, assert both halves satisfy `min_unit_tiles`, ≥1 glazed tile, a door to a common route, and (if the half is to be wet-capable) sit within the plumbing radius (LC-05). Report `splits_legal / splits_possible` and `min_viable_unit_tiles`. A floor where no legal split exists should be reported as `single_occupancy_only = true`.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Types that are intrinsically single-occupancy (theatre, ward, trading floor, museum gallery) should declare it; the failure is the accidental single-occupancy floor — one that could not be split even if the market asked. Symptom: deep floors whose only corridors are dead-end and whose every column sits mid-room.

### LC-20 Draw the base-build / fit-out boundary as a real line, and name who may change each side
- Rule: Split every element into landlord/owner (base) and tenant/user (fit-out) and put that ownership on the plan. The lifecycle failures on this axis are not technical but contractual: nobody invests in change they do not control, and nobody can maintain what nobody owns.
- Evidence: PP-23 and PP-25 already show the mechanism on the operational side (late programme change re-solves routes; measurement loops go unclosed). The lifecycle literature generalises it: renovation barrier reviews identify financing and ownership mismatch as first-order ("households … cannot finance necessary renovations"; regulatory and organisational barriers including "risk aversion and litigation culture") (Liao, Ren & Li/T2), and the DfD review lists "inadequate legislation, a paucity of financial incentives" as the reason good technical intent goes unimplemented (Vorobjev et al./T2). ISO 55001's "line of sight" is the same idea stated as an obligation to connect objectives to the people acting on components (Glocert/T4). The UK's Soft Landings regime addresses the ownership gap in time rather than in space, by keeping the delivery team engaged through aftercare to post-occupancy evaluation beyond 12 months (isurv/BSRIA/T3).
- Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10001863/ — T2; https://journal-buildingscities.org/articles/10.5334/bc.722 — T2; https://www.glocertinternational.com/resources/guides/asset-management-policy-samp-and-asset-management-plans-iso-55001/ — T4; https://www.isurv.com/info/390/features_archive/11897/soft_landings_framework_the_principles — T3.
- Class: DESIGN PRINCIPLE
- Scope: strongest for leased commercial, retail and multi-tenant public buildings; nominal for owner-occupied housing
- Confidence: Medium (inference from ownership/barrier evidence, not a measured base-build effect)
- Grid translation: Two tile attribute sets: `base` (structure, envelope, core, shaft, portal, common route, LC-09 band) and `fitout` (subdivision, fixtures, finishes, tags). Validation: no `fitout` element may be load-bearing, service-critical or escape-critical; every `fitout` element must be reachable from a `base` route. Report `fitout_items_behind_base_lines` (should be 0 or declared, cf. LC-02) and `fitout_change_cost_class`. Then emit the assumption: `lease_horizon_y` — the period the boundary is expected to hold.
- Exceptions / failure mode: In owner-occupied and single-institution buildings the distinction collapses; declare `ownership_split = none` so future agents do not inherit an imaginary constraint. Symptom: a tenant-demountable partition that carries a sprinkler main, or a meter on the wrong side of a lease line.

### LC-21 Treat a change of use as a formal plan event with a named legal test, not a relabel
- Rule: When the design or a revision changes a room's function tag, re-run the acceptance criteria for the new tag and name the real-world test that would be applied (natural light, escape, drainage, acoustic separation, structural reserve). Record pass, fail, or cannot-determine. An untested relabel is a defect with a delay attached.
- Evidence: England's permitted-development regime states the test explicitly: residential conversion under Class MA requires prior approval on "adequate natural light in all habitable rooms", and is available only where the building was in the qualifying class for two years and is not listed (GPDO 2015 Sch. 2 Pt 3, as inserted by SI 2021/428/T1). The adaptation taxonomy is the same discipline in design language: conversion and adaptive reuse are defined by *function change* and separated from refurbishment accordingly, with the stated motive of avoiding cost from specifications that confuse the categories (Shahi et al./T2). The market evidence says the test is binding at scale: only a minority of obsolete office stock is a conversion candidate, and the modelled prize for New York is bounded by that filter — roughly 75,000 homes if about 10% of eligible office and hotel stock converts (Ellen & Kazis, Federal Reserve Bank of New York, 2023/T1-T2 — modelled scenario, not observed outcome).
- Source: https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2015/596/schedule/2/part/3 — T1; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7326450/ — T2; https://fedinprint.org/item/fednep/97155 — T1/T2.
- Class: CODE REQUIREMENT (jurisdiction-specific: England's Class MA prior-approval test; the re-test discipline is what travels)
- Scope: universal where use classes/zoning regulate function; strongest in commercial→residential and retail→residential
- Confidence: High on the English test as retrieved; the share-of-stock figures are modelled and jurisdiction-bound
- Grid translation: Any tag change on a room tile-set triggers a `change_of_use_test` record: `{from_tag, to_tag, light (LC-08), wet_radius (LC-05), egress, acoustics (PP-16), load_class (LC-06), verdict}`. Emit `retags_not_tested` (target 0) and a jurisdiction field so an English brief gets the Class MA wording and another gets `equivalent test required — not verified here` rather than a silent pass.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Same-tag re-layout (a bigger bedroom, a merged office) is not a change of use and must not be blocked by this check. Symptom: agents that treat relabelling as free and later discover the new tag's requirements were the binding constraint all along.

### LC-22 Adapt a heritage or retained shell under a "continued use" test, and record what significance limits
- Rule: When designing inside an existing protected or valued building, state the specific features that may not change (façade lines, stair, plan typology, fabric of special interest) and treat adaptation as the default path: the building should be *used*, not merely preserved. Every intervention must be listed with its reversibility class (LC-10) and its reason.
- Evidence: Historic Environment Scotland's guidance note *Managing Change in the Historic Environment: Use and Adaptation of Listed Buildings* (February 2020) states that the guidance exists to "support, promote and enable the continued use, reuse and adaptation" of listed buildings (T1, publication-page wording as retrieved). The retain-first carbon argument (LC-11) is the modern ally of the same position — the retained element that must not be lost is now also the element that stores the most carbon (LMN/T3; Huuhka et al./T2) — but they can conflict, since deep energy retrofit of a protected envelope is precisely what the heritage regime constrains; the DfD review's finding of thin evidence on policy effectiveness applies with equal force to claims that heritage status automatically delivers sustainability (T2).
- Source: https://www.historicenvironment.scot/publications/all/publication/?publicationId=8ab1f9c9-521a-435e-a3f2-aa240119b5e1 — T1; https://journal-buildingscities.org/articles/10.5334/bc.722 — T2.
- Class: STANDARD (policy guidance, not a numeric requirement)
- Scope: heritage, retrofits of existing stock, culturally or architecturally valued public buildings; irrelevant to greenfield
- Confidence: High that the guidance is change-enabling rather than change-blocking; Medium on the carbon-vs-fabric conflict resolution
- Grid translation: Tag protected elements: `retained_protected = {façade lines, stair, portal, courtyard, named rooms}` and forbid them from the LC-10 cheap-change tiers. Emit `interventions_by_reversibility` and `irreversible_interventions_on_protected` — the last number is the one to escalate, not to bury. Validation: any new `shaft`, `portal` or `facade` opening in a protected shell must carry a `reversibility = declared` note.
- Exceptions / failure mode: "Heritage" used as a blanket veto on all change is a misuse of the guidance, whose stated purpose is enabling continued use. Symptom: a retained shell with no route, no riser and no light that can be adapted at any price.

### LC-23 Do not buy flexibility whose cost is certain and whose benefit is speculative
- Rule: Every adaptability measure has a price in tiles, perimeter, height or structure and a probability-weighted benefit. Charge the measure against a named successor use with a plausible horizon; if the only justification is "flexibility", reduce it to the cheapest tier (reversible lines, spare riser tiles) and record that you declined the expensive version.
- Evidence: The practice sources that recommend flexibility are largely vendor- or profession-adjacent: steel office guidance argues long span "provides more flexible use of space" and that flexibility maximises economic life (Steel Construction Info/T3 — the supplier of the long-span solution), and the classic slogan "long life, loose fit" is a design philosophy attributed to Alex Gordon from 1972 (Lifschutz Davidson Sandilands/T3), not a measured return. The counter-evidence is empirical: policy and market reviews find the good intentions unimplemented because of cost and incentive structures (Vorobjev et al./T2), owners cannot or will not fund the works (Liao, Ren & Li/T2), and lifecycle models systematically omit salvage and residual value, i.e. the benefits side of the flexibility ledger is not even counted (PMC LCC review/T2). High-tech flexible shells are a documented case of the philosophy outrunning the demand, criticised in the same literature Brand's book generated (Wikipedia/T3-T4 summary of the critique of Rogers, Fuller, Gehry).
- Source: https://steelconstruction.info/sectors/multi-storey-office-buildings/ — T3 (supplier-adjacent); https://lds-uk.com/idea/long-life-loose-fit/ — T3; https://journal-buildingscities.org/articles/10.5334/bc.722 — T2; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10001863/ — T2; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12425850/ — T2; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/How_Buildings_Learn — T3/T4 (critique summary).
- Class: HEURISTIC
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: Medium-High (the reasoning is well supported; no measured return on flexibility could be retrieved)
- Grid translation: For each flexibility measure, emit `{measure, cost_tiles | cost_perimeter_tiles | cost_band_tiles, successor_use_claimed, horizon_y, cheapest_tier_sufficient}` and a decision record when a pricier tier is chosen. Enforce a `flexibility_premium_share` budget (a stated % of plate area, e.g. ≤ 5%) and report when exceeded. Where `successor_use_claimed` is absent, the measure fails the rule by definition.
- Exceptions / failure mode: Under-flexible cheap plans have a documented failure mode too — the single-use plate that outlives its use (LC-11's carbon argument, LC-04's depth trap). The rule targets *unpriced* flexibility, not flexibility. Symptom: a 12-tile-wide corridor justified by "future reconfiguration" that no alternative tag requires.

### LC-24 State every future-facing claim in a fixed assumption form, and give it a re-check trigger
- Rule: No lifecycle sentence in the output may be a bare adjective. Every claim about the future is recorded as: the decision it constrains, the assumed driver, the horizon, the confidence, what would falsify it, and when it will be revisited. Where two futures are genuinely plausible, prefer the decision that does not commit (defer), and say so.
- Evidence: Appraisal doctrine is explicit. HM Treasury's Green Book (2026) requires practitioners to "clearly communicate the sources of this uncertainty", to "explicitly adjust their appraisals for optimism bias", and to run "sensitivity analysis to examine how changes in key assumptions … might affect the summary metrics" (T1). Standards give the horizon discipline: EN 15978's assessment runs over a reference study period (50 years as the default used in practice), and IgCC requires a *stated* service life in years per assembly, which is an assumption made auditable (T1). The adaptation-robustness literature — dynamic adaptive policy pathways, developed for climate planning under deep uncertainty — is the transferable method: stage commitments and pre-agree the trigger conditions under which the next step is taken (Haasnoot et al., cited via the Deltares and university records/T2-T3). Huuhka et al. make the framing demand from the other side: the renovate-or-replace choice should be made "neutrally" at the planning stage, not after the design has committed (T2).
- Source: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/the-green-book-appraisal-and-evaluation-in-central-government/the-green-book-2026 — T1; https://docs.realtimelca.com/methodology-and-compliance/en-15978 — T3 (citing CEN/EN 15978); https://up.codes/s/service-life-plan — T1; https://www.deltares.nl/en/expertise/areas-of-expertise/sea-level-rise/dynamic-adaptive-policy-pathways — T3 (and https://research.utwente.nl/en/publications/dynamic-adaptive-policy-pathways-a-new-method-for-crafting-robust/ — T2 record); https://journal-buildingscities.org/articles/10.5334/bc.309 — T2.
- Class: STANDARD (the register fields follow the Green Book disclosure duty and the IgCC per-assembly service-life declaration; the commit-later limb is priced in LC-23)
- Scope: universal
- Confidence: High
- Grid translation: Emit an `assumptions[]` block with fixed fields — `{id, constrains (tile/rule), driver, horizon_y, confidence, falsifier, recheck, committed: bool}` — and reference the id from every lifecycle claim in the narrative. Cross-checks: every LC-01 life value, LC-05 `D_allow_mm`, LC-06 load reserve, LC-08 `unlit_core_share` budget, LC-20 `lease_horizon_y`, LC-23 successor use must carry an assumption id; report `lifecycle_claims_without_id`, target 0. Add `deferred_decisions[]` for anything deliberately left open, with the trigger that would close it.
- Exceptions / failure mode: A register of 40 unfalsifiable assumptions is noise; keep only the ones that change geometry. Symptom: "the building is flexible" written where `successor_use_claimed = null`, and no one revisiting it in year 8.

## Weak or contested

- **There is no verifiable, open-access "service life per element class" table.** This is the biggest hole in the file. ISO 15686-8 ("reference service life") is paywalled; BS 7543's design-life category values could not be read (a BSI trade summary returned only page furniture); the NAHB *Study of Life Expectancy of Home Components* (Feb 2007) and the InterNACHI component chart returned raw PDF bytes or 403; HUD's Estimated Useful Life table returned 403; Fannie Mae's multifamily EUL table is a PDF. **Consequence: this file supplies only the structural categories (EN 1990, verified) and the *obligation* to state lives (IgCC, verified). Any per-finish, per-pipe, per-lift number an agent writes elsewhere must be either sourced to a document it actually opened or labelled `UNCITED — heuristic`.** Do not fill this gap with confident-looking year ranges.
- **"Reuse saves 50–80% of the carbon of new build."** The claim is everywhere and this pass retrieved no primary support for any specific percentage; the sources I did open say the direction ("structure holds most carbon", reuse "much lower total carbon") without a transferable number. Use the qualitative argument plus LC-11's payback test. The commonly quoted "% of UK/US stock that will exist in 2050" figures were likewise unverifiable and are not used.
- **Whether renovation always beats replacement.** Huuhka et al. is the strongest evidence found and it is a *framework with case results*, not a universal law; it explicitly says payback-time metrics of several decades are unhelpful for decisions. Retained frames with poor clear heights, contamination, no riser capacity or a wrong grid can lose. State the test, not the conclusion.
- **Design for disassembly's realised benefits.** Honestly graded, the evidence is thin: the *Buildings & Cities* policy review (T2) finds a limited empirical basis, no ranking of instruments, and notes DfD cost premia; the only hard cases are legislative thresholds (Italian 50% reusable-component design, California 70% C&D recycling), which are compliance requirements rather than proof that disassembly works in use. Material passports and "buildings as material banks" were searched but no peer-reviewed outcome study could be opened; treat all DfD benefit claims in this file as unproven and gate them with LC-12.
- **The adaptability case is largely vendor- and profession-adjacent.** The clearest numeric flexibility guidance (floor plate 13.5 m, 6×9 / 7.5×12 grids, "relocatable walls", "maximise economic life") comes from a steel-industry information service; the residential screening thresholds (7 m / 14 m / 2.7 m / 2–4.5 m modules) come from one practice article, uncorroborated by a measured study this pass. Both are usable as *order-of-magnitude screening*, which is how they are labelled — and the "flexibility pays" proposition is itself asserted, not demonstrated (hence LC-23).
- **EN 1991-1-1 office value.** The engineering-teaching reproduction read here gives domestic 1.5 and office 2.0 kN/m²; category B is more commonly quoted as 2.5 kN/m² with a separate movable-partition allowance. The exact values are unverified; what the file relies on is the ordering (storage/assembly > office > residential-imposed) and the existence of a partition allowance, from which the practical conclusion follows that conversion is governed by added permanent load, not by live load. Verify against the standard before quoting a number.
- **New York conversion figures.** The ~75,000-homes / ~10%-of-eligible-stock figure is a modelled scenario in a Fed staff report, not an observed conversion rate; CBC New York's screen of the stock and the NYC Comptroller's costing of conversions were both unreachable (403) this pass. Do not convert "a model says" into "the market does". The US conversion-incentive measures (zoning, tax, state-law changes) mentioned in secondary coverage are unverified here.
- **Post-occupancy evidence on how often functions actually change.** No longitudinal study of interior change rates could be opened (the office fit-out change-frequency paper I looked for is on a blocked host), so this file has no measured replacement interval for fit-out and space-plan layers, and does not assert one. Brand's per-layer half-lives remain unverified for the second pass running (layer *names* only, per TH-28). A follow-up should retrieve a building-physics or real-estate dataset of fit-out intervals; until then LC-01's "cycle" column is an obligation, not a table of values.
- **Criticism of flexibility as a value.** Documented and pointed (Brand's book criticises high-tech flexible shells; Rogers and others are named in the secondary account), but I could not retrieve a peer-reviewed test of whether provided flexibility gets used. LC-23 is therefore written as an accounting rule (price the measure, name the successor) rather than as an empirical claim that flexibility is wasted.
- **Jurisdiction leakage.** Class MA and London Plan SI 7 are England-specific; the DoD Green Code is a US federal-agency adoption of IgCC; EN 1990/15978 are European. The rules are written so the *test* travels ("a prior-approval-style light test exists; name it") while the numbers stay labelled with their jurisdiction, matching the approach in `building-codes.md`.
